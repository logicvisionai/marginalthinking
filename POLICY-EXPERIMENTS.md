# Marginal Thinking — Policy Experiments & Institutional Transitions

**Status:** normative editorial method  
**Version:** 1.0  
**Effective:** 2026-09-22  
**Cadence:** one deep evergreen case study per week, with event-driven revisions when new evidence changes the assessment.

## Purpose

This series studies implemented programs, reforms and institutional transitions as observable policy systems. The unit of analysis is the intervention, not the ideology or the country. Cases may come from democracies, authoritarian systems, socialist economies, market economies, monarchies or hybrid institutional arrangements. Political regime is treated as context when it changes implementation capacity, incentives, coercion, accountability, distribution or transferability.

The series does not publish a binary list of policies that "worked" or "failed". A policy can achieve its immediate target while creating later vulnerabilities, distribute gains and costs unevenly, work only under specific institutions, or require later redesign. Assessments therefore preserve time horizon and outcome dimension.

## Selection rule

A case enters the research queue when it has a material economic or social problem, an identifiable intervention with a reconstructable mechanism, enough primary and independent evidence to distinguish design from implementation, observable outcomes across more than one horizon where possible, high comparative value, and enough context to explain limits to replication.

Selection is deliberately heterogeneous across geography and political-economic systems. The corpus must not converge on one regime, income group or ideological tradition merely because documentation is easier to obtain.

## Mandatory analytical structure

Every case reconstructs:
1. initial condition;
2. declared objective;
3. institutional and legal architecture;
4. behavioural or accounting mechanism;
5. sequencing and reversibility;
6. financing and balance-sheet incidence;
7. political economy and implementation capacity;
8. immediate outcomes;
9. durability;
10. distributional effects;
11. second-order effects;
12. external shocks and confounders;
13. strongest available causal evidence;
14. competing interpretations;
15. reversals and later adaptations;
16. transfer limits.

## Outcome vector

No overall score is assigned. Cases are described separately across target achievement, speed of transmission, fiscal and balance-sheet sustainability, institutional durability, distribution, second-order effects, resilience, adaptability, evidence strength and context dependence.

## Evidence hierarchy

Primary sources are preferred for legal design and official implementation: legislation, central banks, statistical agencies, ministries, program manuals, budgets, regulators and multilateral documents. Independent evaluations and academic research test causal claims, compare interpretations and identify effects that implementing institutions may not measure. Contemporary high-quality reporting may document events for which primary records are incomplete.

A later favorable or unfavorable outcome is never sufficient evidence that the policy caused it. Each article states the strongest causal design available and the main confounders.

## Comparative discipline

Comparison is mechanism-first. Cases belong together when they address a sufficiently similar problem through comparable channels. Monetary stabilization cases may compare unit-of-account reform, fiscal correction, exchange-rate anchors, wage-price agreements or currency-board rules without treating the political systems that adopted them as equivalent.

## Atlas presentation contract

The Policy Case Atlas is a comparative index, not a compressed substitute for the underlying case studies.

- the comparison matrix contains only compact fields that are genuinely comparable across cases, such as country, intervention, launch period, controlled domain, evidence status and availability of a deep study;
- paragraph-length descriptions of problems, mechanisms, outcomes or transfer limits belong in structured case blocks, not table cells;
- each case block keeps **problem**, **mechanism**, **observed outcome** and **transfer limits** visually distinct;
- the public index must remain usable without zooming at narrow viewport widths; comparison rows may reflow into labelled vertical blocks on mobile;
- source lists and related deep research remain accessible from each case without overwhelming the primary comparison layer;
- filtering and discovery controls must operate on the same canonical structured case data rather than a separate manually maintained catalog.

## Public outputs

Each approved case produces a bilingual deep research article, a structured Policy Case Atlas entry, links to country/topic/series pages, a reconciliation decision for cumulative Marginal Thinking tools, and source-linked revisions when later evidence materially changes the case.

Canonical chain:

problem → institutional design → incentives → implementation → financing → adaptation → outcomes → distribution → second-order effects → durability → transfer limits
