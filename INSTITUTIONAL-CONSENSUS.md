# Marginal Thinking — Institutional Consensus Intelligence

**Status:** transversal internal research method  
**Version:** 1.0  
**Effective date:** 2026-09-16

## 1. Purpose

Marginal Thinking may use public research, portfolio commentary, disclosed holdings, fund/ETF flows, regulatory filings and other observable signals from large asset managers, banks, insurers, pension funds, sovereign investors and other major allocators as a **sensor of institutional consensus**.

This layer exists to answer four questions:

1. What are large institutions increasingly agreeing about?
2. Is that view supported by observable capital allocation and primary data, or only by narrative?
3. Where is the consensus diverging, becoming crowded or beginning to break?
4. What second-order effects, smaller markets, supply-chain links or cross-asset consequences remain underfollowed?

It is not a new editorial program, public ranking, fixed watchlist or recommendation engine. It is a transversal research method available to all four canonical programs when useful.

## 2. Core principle

> Institutional research is evidence about **what major allocators are saying or doing**, not authority about what is true.

A BlackRock, JPMorgan, Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, UBS, Vanguard or other institutional publication may be highly informative, but it must never determine the Marginal Thinking thesis by itself.

The intended chain is:

```text
institutional signal
        ↓
classify what kind of signal it actually is
        ↓
compare with primary data and observable flows
        ↓
compare across institutions and time
        ↓
identify consensus / divergence / break
        ↓
trace transmission and second-order effects
        ↓
look for underfollowed markets or assumptions
        ↓
independent Marginal Thinking assessment
```

## 3. Dynamic actor universe — no fixed giant list

Do not maintain a mandatory list of institutions that must be checked every day. The relevant actor set must emerge from the research question, market regime and available evidence.

Potential actors may include:

- global and regional asset managers;
- investment banks and broker research desks;
- insurers and pension investors;
- sovereign wealth funds and official institutions;
- large ETF/index providers when flow or positioning data are material;
- hedge-fund or alternative-investment research when public and verifiable;
- central banks, finance ministries, multilateral institutions and development banks when they shape institutional expectations;
- exchanges, clearing houses, regulators and market-infrastructure providers when their data reveal positioning, liquidity or structure.

No institution receives permanent priority merely because of size or brand. Prefer the source that best observes the mechanism being studied.

## 4. Signal classes — never collapse them

Every institutional signal used in analysis must be classified conceptually before interpretation.

### A. Capital evidence

Examples: disclosed holdings, regulatory filings, ETF/fund flows, net subscriptions/redemptions, publicly reported allocation changes, AUM flow data, issuance/underwriting data or other observable capital movement.

This is the strongest evidence that capital actually moved, but it can still be delayed, incomplete, aggregated or constrained by disclosure rules.

### B. Explicit portfolio view

Examples: tactical/strategic asset-allocation view, overweight/underweight statement, duration preference, sector preference or formal portfolio guidance.

This is evidence of a stated institutional stance, not proof that all portfolios managed by the institution hold that exposure.

### C. Research thesis

Examples: outlook, thematic research, capital-market assumptions, macro thesis or scenario analysis.

This is evidence of analytical framing. It may influence allocations but must not be presented as an observed position unless separate evidence supports that claim.

### D. Commercial narrative

Examples: thematic marketing attached to a fund range, product launch, client campaign or sales material.

This may reveal what the institution believes clients are interested in, but commercial incentives must be explicit in the interpretation.

### E. Attributed public comment

Examples: speech, interview, webcast or conference statement by a named investment executive.

Treat as the speaker's dated view. Do not generalize it automatically to the whole institution.

### F. Media paraphrase

Use only as a lower-confidence pointer when original material cannot be obtained. Prefer reopening the institution's primary document, filing, transcript or data release.

## 5. Disclosure limitations

Never infer a complete portfolio from a partial disclosure.

Examples of limitations that must be considered:

- regulatory filings may be quarterly and therefore stale;
- some disclosures cover only specific security classes or jurisdictions;
- short positions, derivatives, cash, private assets or non-reportable instruments may be absent;
- client portfolios may differ materially from a house view;
- aggregate AUM may combine index, active, advisory and client-directed mandates;
- a manager can publicly discuss a theme without making a material new allocation to it;
- product-provider flows may reflect client behavior rather than the provider's own conviction.

If the disclosure cannot support a claim about actual positioning, say so.

## 6. State change matters more than static opinion

The intelligence layer should prioritize **changes in stance** rather than repeatedly reporting stable views.

Ask:

- What changed since the institution's prior outlook/commentary?
- Did a theme move from research discussion to explicit portfolio expression?
- Did a previously favored asset become neutral or vice versa?
- Did the horizon change from tactical to strategic or the reverse?
- Did the institution broaden a theme from one market to another?
- Did the stated thesis remain unchanged while observable flows moved in the opposite direction?

A stable opinion with no new evidence normally deserves no new article space.

## 7. Consensus-state vocabulary

Use qualitative labels only when multiple credible signals justify them. Do not assign numerical consensus scores.

- **Emerging** — a thesis appears across a small number of credible institutions or new flow evidence begins to support it.
- **Broadening** — more institutions, asset classes or geographies begin to express the same mechanism.
- **Established** — the thesis is widely represented in institutional research and/or observable positioning.
- **Crowded** — the thesis is established and evidence of concentration, valuation, positioning or one-sided assumptions makes crowding analytically material.
- **Diverging** — credible institutions disagree on mechanism, horizon or asset expression.
- **Breaking** — new primary data, flows or price relationships materially weaken a previously established consensus.
- **Unclear** — evidence is too mixed, stale or incomparable to classify responsibly.

These are research descriptors, not recommendations.

## 8. Consensus is not opportunity

Do not equate broad institutional agreement with an attractive trade.

An opportunity candidate becomes interesting only when at least one of these mechanisms is present:

- the consensus thesis is correct but second-order beneficiaries or losers are underfollowed;
- the consensus is expressed mainly in crowded/expensive assets while upstream/downstream exposures remain less repriced;
- actual flows contradict public narrative;
- primary data are changing faster than institutional outlooks;
- a smaller or less liquid market cannot absorb large institutional capital easily and therefore receives less research attention despite real structural change;
- the market is pricing the first-order effect but not a credible transmission mechanism;
- institutions agree on the structural direction but disagree on timing, instrument or geography.

Every opportunity candidate still requires independent evidence, liquidity/access analysis where applicable, contrary evidence and an invalidation condition.

## 9. Capacity and scale asymmetry

Large institutions have informational and analytical advantages, but their size also creates constraints. Marginal Thinking should explicitly ask whether scale changes what can be researched or owned economically.

Potential scale constraints include:

- market liquidity;
- position-size limits;
- mandate restrictions;
- benchmark constraints;
- operational complexity;
- political/regulatory exposure;
- inability to build meaningful exposure in small markets without moving prices;
- client suitability and product-distribution constraints.

This can create a useful research niche: a market or supply-chain link may be economically important but too small, illiquid or operationally difficult to matter to a trillion-dollar allocator. That does **not** make it attractive automatically; it only explains why institutional coverage may be sparse.

## 10. Source hierarchy

Prefer, in order appropriate to the claim:

1. regulatory filings, official holdings disclosures and audited/regulated reports;
2. fund/ETF flow and market-structure data from primary or recognized providers;
3. official institutional outlooks, capital-market assumptions and portfolio-view publications;
4. earnings calls, investor presentations and AUM/net-flow disclosures;
5. named speeches/interviews/webcasts with date and speaker;
6. reputable press summarizing the above only when primary material is unavailable.

For the underlying economic reality, institutional sources do not replace primary macro/market sources such as central banks, treasuries, statistical agencies, exchanges, regulators, BIS, IMF, World Bank, IEA/EIA/OPEC, USGS and similar authoritative sources.

## 11. Evidence matrix

When institutional consensus is material, the internal analysis should be able to answer:

| Field | Question |
|---|---|
| Actor | Who is expressing or revealing the signal? |
| Date | When was the signal observed? |
| Signal class | Capital evidence, portfolio view, research thesis, commercial narrative, public comment or media paraphrase? |
| Theme | What mechanism or market is being discussed? |
| Horizon | Tactical, cyclical, strategic or unspecified? |
| Prior stance | What was the actor's previous observable position/view? |
| Delta | What changed? |
| Capital confirmation | Is there observable flow/holding/issuance evidence? |
| Primary-data confirmation | Do independent data support the thesis? |
| Contrary evidence | What weakens it? |
| Consensus state | Emerging, Broadening, Established, Crowded, Diverging, Breaking or Unclear? |
| Second order | What upstream/downstream/geographic effects follow if the mechanism is real? |
| Underfollowed exposure | Which market, sector, relationship or constraint may be less researched? |
| Invalidation | What would change the assessment? |

The matrix may remain internal; public articles should use only the portions that materially improve the reader's understanding.

## 12. Daily use

The daily market task should use this layer selectively. It should not summarize institutional publications merely because they were released.

A daily institutional signal deserves space when it:

- changes a major house view;
- reveals material actual flow/positioning;
- shows meaningful disagreement among large institutions;
- confirms or challenges the day's cross-asset regime;
- creates a credible second-order opportunity candidate;
- helps explain why market pricing differs across regions or asset classes.

Otherwise omit it.

## 13. Weekly and monthly use

Weekly and monthly research should place more weight on persistence and state change.

Useful questions include:

- Which themes gained or lost institutional support over the period?
- Did flows follow the stated thesis?
- Which consensus became more crowded?
- Which institutional view was invalidated by new data?
- Which second-order beneficiaries/losers became visible?
- Which smaller markets remain underfollowed despite improving evidence?
- Which themes migrated from public markets into private credit, infrastructure, real assets or other vehicles?

## 14. Research-context integration

Institutional-consensus knowledge may be retained in `research-context/briefings/` only when it is durable enough to reduce future research cost.

Do not create a fixed briefing for every institution. A dynamic briefing may instead emerge around a durable analytical object such as:

- an institutional consensus around a structural theme;
- a persistent divergence between allocators and primary data;
- a long-running shift in global asset allocation;
- a repeated transmission mechanism from institutional capital to a market or production system.

Ephemeral house views should remain in the source report and Git history rather than create permanent context clutter.

## 15. QA guardrails

QA must reject or require correction when research:

- treats a public outlook as proof of actual portfolio holdings;
- treats fund-client flows as the asset manager's proprietary allocation decision;
- treats a delayed regulatory filing as current positioning without qualification;
- ignores material disclosure limitations;
- labels a view as consensus based on one institution;
- uses consensus as proof that a thesis is correct;
- calls an exposure an opportunity without independent mechanism, risk and invalidation analysis;
- reproduces commercial marketing language as Marginal Thinking's own conclusion;
- converts institutional political interpretation into unattributed fact.

## 16. Editorial boundary

The purpose of this layer is not to imitate giant institutions. It is to know where institutional attention and capital are moving so Marginal Thinking can independently investigate:

> **consensus vs. evidence → capital vs. narrative → first-order vs. second-order effects → crowded vs. underfollowed exposures.**

That distinction is the intended source of analytical value.
