# Marginal Thinking — Economic Paradigms & Market Design

**Status:** normative editorial method  
**Version:** 1.0  
**Effective:** 2026-09-23  
**Program:** `political-economy-markets`  
**Related program:** `technology-production-society` when technological capability materially changes the economic mechanism.  
**Cadence:** continuous monitoring, selective event-driven publication, weekly screening, quarterly synthesis when evidence warrants it.

## Purpose

This controlled series studies the point at which economic ideas, market design and technology become operating economic systems. Its central question is not whether an idea is intellectually novel, but whether the conceptual tools used by economists, policymakers and regulators are keeping pace with changes in how prices are formed, money settles, capital is allocated, markets aggregate information and economic decisions are automated.

The unit of analysis may be a theory, policy framework, market architecture, monetary design, regulatory experiment or technological mechanism. A paper, ministerial speech, regulatory consultation and live system are not treated as equivalent evidence.

## Evidence-stage ladder

Every object is located internally on a maturity path:

**theory → proposal → controlled experiment → implementation → operating scale → measurable outcomes → revision / abandonment**

A theory can be important before implementation, but public research must state clearly what stage has actually been reached. Announcements, roadmaps and consultation papers do not count as implemented systems. A live market does not by itself prove a welfare improvement.

## Controlled domains

### Economic Thought & Policy Paradigms

Tracks new or materially revised frameworks proposed by researchers, finance/economy ministries, central banks, multilateral institutions and policy communities. Coverage includes growth, industrial policy, fiscal/monetary frameworks, distribution, development, productivity, state capacity and institutional economics.

The series distinguishes intellectual origin, policy translation, instruments, implementation constraints, empirical tests and transfer limits. It never treats a minister's adoption of an idea as evidence that the theory is correct.

### Market Microstructure & Algorithmic Markets

Studies how HFT, co-location, latency, matching engines, tick sizes, auctions, dark trading, automated market making, market fragmentation and algorithmic execution alter liquidity, price discovery, volatility, systemic risk and the cost of capital.

The market's technical architecture is treated as an economic institution rather than neutral plumbing.

### Money, Tokenisation & Digital Finance

Studies stablecoins, tokenised deposits, central-bank money, CBDCs, wholesale settlement, programmable finance, DLT and alternative ledger architectures through monetary economics, financial stability, settlement finality, liquidity, collateral and sovereignty.

Technology claims must be separated from operational deployment and legal finality.

### New Markets & Information Mechanisms

Studies new or rapidly expanding market forms such as prediction/event markets, carbon and environmental markets, compute/data markets, electricity flexibility and capacity markets, and other mechanisms that turn information, risk or scarce capacity into tradable contracts.

Research asks what information the market aggregates, who can participate, how manipulation and adverse selection work, and whether market prices improve decisions outside the venue itself.

### AI, Automation & Economic Institutions

Studies algorithmic pricing, AI agents, automated negotiation, algorithmic management, autonomous financial decision systems and machine-mediated allocation. The focus is on how automation changes information, agency, transaction costs, market power, labour, governance and institutional design.

## Theory-lag test

Every assessment asks:

1. What changed in the real economic or market system?
2. Which established theory already explains it well?
3. Which assumptions became materially less realistic?
4. What new models or empirical research attempt to close the gap?
5. Has the new framework survived evidence outside the original setting?
6. Are institutions adapting through law, regulation, market design or operational infrastructure?
7. What remains unresolved?

The series does not manufacture a "paradigm shift" label merely because technology is new. Existing theory remains the baseline until evidence shows that a materially important mechanism is missing or mis-specified.

## Researcher and policymaker tracking

Discovery is global and dynamic. It may include academic economists, finance and economy ministries, central banks, regulators, market-design researchers, computer scientists working on economic mechanisms, multilateral institutions and practitioners with unique operational data.

Prestige is not evidence. The series records what a person or institution actually proposed, the mechanism claimed, the implementation stage and the evidence available. Political office does not make an economic claim correct, and ideological alignment does not make it false.

## Market-design standard

For trading and financial infrastructure, distinguish:

- venue rules from participant strategy;
- latency from information quality;
- quoted liquidity from executable liquidity;
- price discovery from welfare;
- volume from capital formation;
- trading efficiency from distribution of rents;
- gross settlement speed from net liquidity needs;
- technical finality from legal finality;
- decentralisation claims from actual governance and concentration.

When technology changes the feasible mechanism, research must trace the consequence beyond the venue to financing, production, households or institutional capacity when evidence permits.

## Economic-policy experiment standard

For policy paradigms, trace:

**idea → stated problem → instrument → institutional prerequisites → implementation → financing → behavioural response → outcomes → distribution → second-order effects → durability → transfer limits**

Where causal identification is weak, say so. Observing a later boom, crisis or productivity change does not establish that the policy caused it.

## Source hierarchy

Prioritise primary and close-to-mechanism evidence: peer-reviewed research and high-quality working papers; official regulatory and market-infrastructure documentation; central banks, ministries and statistical agencies; exchange rulebooks and technical notices; BIS, IMF, World Bank, OECD and comparable institutions; reproducible datasets and independent replications.

Corporate or protocol documentation may establish how a system is designed or deployed, but cannot alone establish its economic effect.

## Selection rule

Publish only when at least one condition is met:

- a theory or policy framework gains credible implementation;
- a new market architecture reaches operational scale;
- evidence materially changes the assessment of an established mechanism;
- regulators or public institutions redesign rules because technology changed market behaviour;
- an economic mechanism appears in a new market form with consequences beyond the venue;
- accumulated evidence shows a theory or policy experiment failing, adapting or becoming context-dependent.

Novel vocabulary without a new mechanism is not sufficient.

## Publication discipline

The series is selective. Weekly screening does not imply weekly publication. Repeated commentary without evidence-stage progression stays in monitoring.

Public articles should explain the substantive economic problem directly and should not narrate the editorial process, taxonomy, automation, QA or internal stage labels. Stage concepts may be explained when they are substantively necessary for the reader.
