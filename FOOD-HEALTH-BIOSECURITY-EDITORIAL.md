# Food Systems, Public Health & Strategic Biosecurity — Research Agenda

**Established:** 2026-10-12  
**Status:** editorial research agenda; not a new controlled taxonomy or publishing route  
**Languages:** English and Brazilian Portuguese for individual research outputs.

## Mandate

Trace how food production, diet quality, environmental and occupational exposures, human and animal infections, institutional capacity, and chemical/biological security shape economic development, demography, labour productivity, distribution and international power.

These are two interconnected research streams, not autonomous primary programs. Assign every report to exactly one of the four frozen primary programs in `data/taxonomy.json`; other programs may be related. Neither this document nor an individual producer is authorized to extend the controlled taxonomy.

### A. Food Systems, Nutrition & Civilizational Development

**A1. Historical political economy:** domestication, storage, taxation, state formation, irrigation, agricultural surplus, urbanization, sanitation, colonial crop regimes, agricultural mechanization, fertilizer, the Green Revolution, industrial food and contemporary logistics. Do not infer that a single agricultural innovation caused civilizational development.

**A2. Food security and distribution:** availability, access, utilization and stability; food-price inflation, wages, household purchasing power, trade, climate shocks, reserves, transport, cold-chain capacity, drought, conflict and coercive restrictions. Differentiate hunger, malnutrition, affordability, food safety and food sovereignty.

**A3. Nutrition and long-term health:** deficient diets and micronutrients; obesity, metabolic disease, cardiovascular disease, dietary patterns, ultra-processed foods, life-course exposures and intergenerational human capital. Distinguish observational association from causal evidence; control for confounding and socioeconomic factors.

**A4. Contaminants and agricultural chemicals:** occupational pesticide exposure, dietary residues, environmental persistence, dose–response, regulatory thresholds, biomonitoring, toxicological uncertainty, water contamination, endocrine-disruption research and trade standards. Never equate hazard identification with measured population risk; compare active ingredients and exposure pathways individually.

**A5. Animal systems:** herd density, housing, transport, welfare, waste, veterinary services, vaccination, biosecurity, feed, slaughterhouse inspection and antibiotics. Connect production incentives to zoonotic and antimicrobial-resistance risks without asserting uniform harm across farming systems.

**A6. Institutional comparisons:** Brazil and Latin America, the EU, US, China, India, sub-Saharan Africa, and import-dependent economies. Compare institutions, enforcement, incentives, budgets and outcomes rather than relying on stereotypes of production methods.

### B. Disease, Biological Risks & Strategic Security

**B1. Epidemiology and microbial ecology:** viruses, bacteria, parasites and fungi; host ecology, zoonoses, foodborne and waterborne illness, surveillance, diagnostics and ecosystem interfaces.

**B2. Disease and development:** mortality, disability, fertility, migration, educational outcomes, health expenditures, labour supply, insurance and fiscal effects; historical pandemics and heterogeneous local consequences.

**B3. Antimicrobial resistance:** human prescriptions, veterinary antimicrobial use, wastewater, infection prevention, selection pressure, surveillance and incentives for antimicrobial innovation. Separate association, attributable burden and scenario projections.

**B4. Institutions and preparedness:** WHO, FAO, WOAH, UNEP, national regulators, laboratories, procurement and supply chains; preparedness, public-health capacity, epidemiological data and conflicts of interest.

**B5. Chemical and biological weapons governance:** the Chemical Weapons Convention and OPCW, Biological Weapons Convention, UN disarmament institutions, humanitarian law, allegations and attribution standards, inspections, verification constraints, historical cases, nonproliferation, dual-use governance and civilian protection. Coverage is analytical, defensive and historical. No agent enhancement, synthesis, weaponization, dispersion procedures, attack optimization or acquisition instructions.

**B6. Crises and political economy:** military conflict, deliberate sabotage allegations, famine, health-system disruption, displacement, misinformation, international monitoring, sanctions and reconstruction. Distinguish documented intentional attacks from accidents, speculation and unresolved attribution.

## Integrated causal questions

1. Agricultural productivity → prices/incomes → dietary choice → health → educational attainment and economic productivity.
2. Intensification of livestock production → animal health/biosecurity and antimicrobial practices → infectious disease risk → clinical burden → public budgets and trade.
3. Pesticide regulation → farm costs and exposure → worker health and exports → distributional outcomes; separate risks and benefits by intervention and region.
4. War or climate shock → crop yields, transport and imports → hunger and infectious disease → migration, institutional legitimacy and security.
5. Epidemic shock → illness and mortality → labour and fiscal capacity → trade restrictions and geopolitical bargaining.
6. Dual-use technical capacity → civilian utility and safeguards → international institutions, verification and political trust.

## Publication method

Each article begins with one falsifiable research question and establishes the mechanism, historical baseline, measurement strategy, current evidence, comparisons, counterfactual, institutional actors, distributional consequences, second-order effects, uncertainties and conditions that would change the conclusion.

Use full source provenance and observation dates. Give research findings, estimates, scenarios and unresolved allegations separate labels. Avoid substituting correlation for causality or declaring a pesticide, food class, animal practice or infectious agent uniformly harmful without exposure and context. For chemical and biological incidents separate allegation, official attribution, independent confirmation and unresolved evidence.

Minimum first-party sources include FAO/SOFI/FAOSTAT, WHO, WOAH, UNEP, Codex Alimentarius, IPCC, World Bank, ILO, OECD, national sanitary agencies including ANVISA/MAPA/IBGE, OPCW and UNODA. Use peer-reviewed systematic reviews and epidemiological studies where causal inference is needed. Record methodology and limitations of any country rankings.

## Commissioned first six investigations

1. **The price of a healthy diet and the economics of development** — affordability, nutritional quality, productivity, food systems and distribution. Anchor: SOFI 2026.
2. **Industrial livestock, zoonotic hazards and antimicrobial resistance** — production systems, veterinary governance, surveillance and economic incentives.
3. **Pesticides, occupational exposure and regulatory divergence** — hazard vs exposure, worker health, international standards and trade-offs.
4. **Food safety as national infrastructure** — sanitation, cold chains, inspection, surveillance and international commerce.
5. **Epidemics, state capacity and economic transformation** — comparative historical cases and current preparedness.
6. **Chemical and biological arms-control regimes** — CWC/OPCW versus BWC, verification, dual-use governance and geopolitical incentives.

## Cross-language release and QA

English and Brazilian Portuguese source texts must present semantically equivalent research, charts and qualifications. Publication requires the repository's staging → pending → independent QA approval → publisher sequence. Do not create `reports/**` directly. New reports must satisfy current editorial depth, source, visualization, attribution and build checks. If source verification or QA is incomplete, leave the study unpublished.

## Regra editorial em português

As duas linhas tratam a saúde coletiva como parte da infraestrutura econômica das sociedades. Segurança alimentar não equivale apenas à quantidade produzida; inclui preço, acesso, qualidade nutricional, inocuidade, estabilidade e capacidade institucional. A investigação de doenças distingue mecanismos biológicos de seus determinantes econômicos e sociais. Alegações sobre uso de armas químicas ou biológicas exigem evidência documental, verificação independente e classificação explícita do grau de atribuição. Nenhuma publicação deve confundir perigo potencial com risco efetivo, estimativa estatística com observação direta, ou hipótese com fato.
