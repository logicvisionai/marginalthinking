# Economists who transformed the world: from the earliest theories of wealth to economics in 2026

Economic questions preceded economics as a profession by millennia. A Mesopotamian administrator had to record obligations and manage grain; a Greek philosopher asked how property and exchange related to justice; Chinese ministers debated food reserves and state monopolies; Islamic jurists considered fair taxation and commerce; European writers eventually treated trade, money, production and public debt as recurring problems of government. None of the early authors had a modern degree in economics. Their tools came from philosophy, law, religion, public administration, trade and mathematics.

The differences among later schools reflect differences in the problems they were trying to solve. Smith examined specialization and institutions. Marx examined property and the relationship between labour and accumulation. The marginal revolution reformulated prices and choice. Keynes examined persistent unemployment and uncertain investment. Hayek analysed decentralized information. Sen studied capabilities beyond monetary income. Ostrom documented communities that built their own rules for shared resources. Contemporary researchers use experiments, historical records and statistical techniques to examine how decisions and institutions work.

Influence is not evidence of correctness. Important thinkers made errors, worked under institutional constraints and proposed theories subsequently revised. Elegant mathematics does not guarantee a true description of the world; persuasive criticism of inequality does not guarantee a correct quantitative forecast. The history of economics is neither a linear sequence of triumphant schools nor a contest with one permanent winner.

## I — Before the profession: philosophers, administrators and imperial political economy

### 1. Xenophon: the household and the organization of production

**Xenophon** (c.430–354 BCE) was born into a relatively prosperous Athenian family, was associated with Socrates and gained experience in military and administrative life. His *Oeconomicus* explored the management of an agricultural household, including the assignment of work, use of tools, land management and the discipline of those responsible for production.

The word *oikonomia* described household management, not the measurement of national output. Xenophon treated economic competence as something that could be acquired through learning and practice. Yet his household was patriarchal and depended on slavery: efficiency appeared within a social structure that denied equal authority to women and enslaved people.

His enduring contribution is the idea that coordinating resources requires knowledge and organization. He did not develop modern equilibrium-price or labour-market theories. The social context of his writing is indispensable to understanding what the management techniques were intended to achieve.

### 2. Aristotle: value, money, justice and unlimited accumulation

**Aristotle** (384–322 BCE) was born in Stagira; his father **Nicomachus** was connected to the Macedonian court. Aristotle studied in Plato's Academy, became a philosopher and teacher, and instructed the young Alexander of Macedon. In *Politics* and the *Nicomachean Ethics*, he examined exchange, property, money and justice within the political community.

Aristotle distinguished the acquisition of goods to meet needs from an unrestricted search for money as an end in itself. Money made dissimilar products commensurable, but monetary equality did not settle the question of justice in their exchange. His objections to usury reflected a moral and legal approach, not a modern model of default risk or opportunity cost.

His economic thought was influential in medieval scholasticism. It was inseparable, however, from his defence of hierarchical social arrangements and arguments for natural slavery. Its historical significance is not a permanently correct model of finance but the attempt to make economic activity accountable to a conception of social life.

### 3. Kautilya or Chanakya: public finance and economic surveillance in ancient India

The *Arthashastra* is associated with **Kautilya**, also identified in tradition with **Chanakya**, a statesman linked to early Mauryan India. The text's composition, dating and authorship are debated. It cannot safely be treated as a single work personally written in every detail by one fourth-century BCE minister.

The treatise addressed taxation, agricultural production, administration, prices, trade inspection, spies and military finance. State power required a reliable treasury; the treasury required production and taxes; excessive extraction could undermine cultivation and commerce. Standard measures, officials and grain reserves were concrete instruments of public administration.

Its significance is the systematic treatment of production and state capacity in South Asian political thought. It was not a modern development textbook and did not describe every part of ancient India as a unified market.

### 4. Guan Zhong and the Guanzi tradition: markets and state stabilization in China

**Guan Zhong** (d. c.645 BCE) was a minister of Qi in ancient China whose reputation became associated with later administrative writings. The *Guanzi* is a compilation of ideas developed and transmitted over time, not an entirely authenticated personal manuscript of Guan Zhong.

The text contains approaches to food, trade, taxes, money and state reserves. Buying food during abundance and releasing stores in scarcity could, if well administered, reduce sharp changes in prices while preserving access to supplies. But control over stocks also gave government officials opportunities to extract revenue or favour particular groups.

These discussions show that economic reasoning about market stabilization existed outside Europe. Equating the *Guanzi* directly with modern Keynesianism or central-bank policy would ignore fundamental differences in institutions and available information.

### 5. Sang Hongyang: salt, iron and the fiscal economy of Han China

**Sang Hongyang** (c.152–80 BCE) was a Han official associated with state monopolies and fiscal policies under Emperor Wu. Arguments about salt and iron were preserved in the *Yantielun*, a later compilation of political-economic debates.

The question was whether monopoly revenue and state control over key goods strengthened defence and administration or undermined production and consumer welfare through high prices, poor quality and corruption. Authorities needed reliable revenue to finance armies and officials; farmers and merchants faced the costs of extraction.

The debate anticipated a persistent problem of public enterprise: evaluating both fiscal returns and the economic burden imposed on users. It did not correspond neatly to modern capitalism-versus-socialism categories.

### 6. Abu Yusuf: taxation without destroying cultivation

**Abu Yusuf Yaqub ibn Ibrahim** (c.731–798), a jurist educated in the Islamic scholarly tradition of Kufa and connected to **Abu Hanifa**, advised the Abbasid court on taxation and administration. His *Kitab al-Kharaj* discussed land revenue, collection and obligations of rulers and subjects.

Revenue depended on successful cultivation. Excessive levies, arbitrary collectors and failure to consider harvest conditions could lead to abandonment of land and ultimately lower fiscal capacity. Rules and justice were therefore practical supports of government finance, not simply moral ornaments.

The work was grounded in Islamic law and cannot be retrofitted into a modern estimated tax-rate curve. Its historical contribution lies in the relationship between fiscal legitimacy, productive incentives and state capacity.

### 7. Al-Ghazali: markets, necessity and moral constraints

**Abu Hamid al-Ghazali** (1058–1111) was born in Tus in Persia, received extensive theological and legal education and taught in institutions associated with the Nizamiyya tradition. His writings, including *Ihya Ulum al-Din*, discussed work, trade, property, charity and economic obligations.

Markets allowed specialists to exchange goods they could not all produce alone, yet transactions were expected to conform to standards of fairness, honest dealing and religious responsibility. Profit and exchange could fulfil social needs; fraud and exploitation could violate legitimate obligations even when privately lucrative.

Al-Ghazali did not formulate modern mathematical price theory. He represents a tradition in which market activity was embedded in legal and moral institutions, a subject that remains important to contemporary analyses of trust and governance.

### 8. Thomas Aquinas: the just price and the law of lending

**Thomas Aquinas** (1225–1274) was born to a noble family in southern Italy, became a Dominican and studied in Naples, Paris and Cologne. His work combined Christian theology, Aristotelian philosophy and medieval legal reasoning.

The *Summa Theologiae* discussed exchange, fraud, private property and interest on loans. Arguments about the just price concerned the circumstances of exchange and the duty not to deceive buyers. The critique of usury depended on medieval conceptions of money and lending; it was not an analysis of contemporary interest rates or default-adjusted returns.

His influence persisted through later scholastics and jurists. The importance of his economic thought is the view that contracts and prices must be examined alongside justice and obligations, rather than as a realm entirely outside morality.

### 9. Ibn Khaldun: labour, cities and the fiscal cycle of dynasties

**Ibn Khaldun** (1332–1406) was born in Tunis into an educated family. He studied law, language, mathematics and philosophy and held administrative and judicial positions across North Africa and later Cairo. Political service, imprisonment, exile and changing rulers gave him direct experience of state fragility.

In the *Muqaddimah*, he analysed specialization, urban production, taxation, government expenditure and the rise and decline of dynasties. Dense towns and differentiated skills could support higher output. A government depended on the economy it taxed; escalating levies and coercion might ultimately undermine commerce and state revenues.

His concept of **asabiyyah**, or group solidarity, connected political cohesion to the capacity of dynasties to gain and retain power. It was a historical mechanism, not a statistically proven fixed lifespan of every state. Later claims that Ibn Khaldun “invented” a modern tax curve oversimplify his work.

The enduring achievement was combining institutions, production, group conflict and public finance within a theory of historical change.

### 10. Al-Maqrizi: money, famine and the Egyptian state

**Taqi al-Din al-Maqrizi** (1364–1442) was an Egyptian historian and jurist whose work addressed prices, monetary disturbances and famine under the Mamluks. His education and career were tied to religious and administrative institutions in Cairo.

He examined corruption, the circulation of copper money, the deterioration of purchasing power and the burden of scarcity on households. Monetary measures could redistribute losses among debtors, taxpayers, wage earners and holders of better-quality coins. Yet famine also depended on agricultural supply, transport and political disruption.

Al-Maqrizi's importance lies in identifying interactions among money, governance and real scarcity before modern monetary economics. He did not provide an econometric account of the exact contribution of each cause.

## II — Renaissance, conquest and European political economy

### 11. Nicole Oresme: monetary legitimacy and the limits of debasement

**Nicole Oresme** (c.1320–1382) studied at the University of Paris, became a mathematician and theologian and advised the French monarchy. In writings on coinage, he examined the power of rulers to change precious-metal content and monetary standards.

Debasement could provide revenue to a ruler while undermining the expectations of people obliged to accept the currency. Oresme argued that money should not be treated as the ruler's unlimited private property and that changes required legitimate public justification.

His analysis helps explain the tension between fiscal expedience and contractual trust. It did not anticipate the full institutional architecture of modern central banking, but it addressed the political constraints that monetary issuers face.

### 12. Martín de Azpilcueta and Salamanca: prices in a world of transatlantic silver

**Martín de Azpilcueta** (1492–1586) studied and taught law and theology at Iberian universities, including Salamanca and Coimbra. He lived through a major expansion of transatlantic commerce and precious-metal flows into Europe.

His work on exchange examined how the purchasing power of money varied with its relative abundance and with local market conditions. Other scholastics of Salamanca, including **Francisco de Vitoria**, **Domingo de Soto**, **Luis de Molina** and **Juan de Mariana**, discussed property, contracts, prices and political legitimacy.

Their arguments cannot be reduced to a modern school of laissez-faire. They also debated conquest, Indigenous rights and the justice of European imperial rule. Their contribution was rigorous reasoning about exchange and obligations in a rapidly changing and coercive world.

### 13. Jean Bodin: sovereignty and the Price Revolution

**Jean Bodin** (c.1530–1596), a French jurist and political theorist, lived amid religious wars and disputes about the power of rulers. He examined price inflation during the sixteenth century and assigned substantial importance to inflows of precious metals.

More money could alter purchasing power while prices responded differently across goods and markets. Trade, demography and productive conditions also mattered. Bodin did not create modern monetary macroeconomics, but he linked sovereign authority, currency and international metal flows.

His work illustrates how early monetary analysis emerged from practical debates about political power and inflation, rather than from a separate department of economics.

### 14. Antonio Serra: productive diversity and international payments

**Antonio Serra**, active in early seventeenth-century Naples, published a treatise on why some countries attracted gold and silver even without domestic mines. Written in **1613**, his work was shaped by the fiscal and commercial problems of his time.

Serra emphasized manufacturing, specialized occupations and commerce as sources of external earnings. A diversified productive structure could attract payments and accumulate resources without relying solely on mineral deposits.

His education and family history are incompletely documented. His importance lies in shifting attention from stocks of metal to productive capability, without implying that permanent trade surpluses are the only measure of prosperity.

### 15. Thomas Mun: the commerce of an international kingdom

**Thomas Mun** (1571–1641) came from an English merchant family and became involved with the **East India Company**. He acquired financial knowledge through trade, foreign payments and company affairs.

In *England's Treasure by Forraign Trade*, published posthumously in **1664**, he examined the relationship between international commercial balances and the kingdom's monetary resources. A shipment of money abroad might be profitable if used to acquire goods that could be resold at a gain; a single outward movement of bullion was not necessarily evidence of national loss.

The theory operated in a world of mercantilist rivalry and colonial privilege. Commerce can benefit participants on both sides, and enduring prosperity cannot be measured only by metal accumulation. His company's business also depended on imperial and coercive institutions that narrow financial calculations often omitted.

### 16. William Petty: the first ambitions of political arithmetic

**William Petty** (1623–1687) was born in England, studied medicine and mathematics and became involved in research, administration and large surveys of Irish land under English rule.

He attempted to estimate income, population, land and taxable resources through **political arithmetic**. This was a major methodological shift: economic claims should be related to measured quantities rather than moral assertion alone. The underlying data were often unreliable by modern statistical standards.

His surveying activities were also connected to colonial land dispossession in Ireland. Measurement could serve state control and unequal property redistribution. The legacy joins an advance in quantitative analysis with the political uses of the information it produced.

### 17. Richard Cantillon: entrepreneurship and money's unequal entry points

**Richard Cantillon** (c.1680–1734) was an Irish-born financier active in European markets during speculative expansion associated with John Law. His *Essai sur la nature du commerce en général*, published in **1755**, analysed commerce, money and uncertainty.

Cantillon characterized entrepreneurs as agents who commit costs before knowing their selling prices. Income is uncertain when many obligations must be met. He also observed that newly created or imported money reaches people in sequence, affecting some markets before others.

The latter mechanism became known as the **Cantillon effect**, but it does not show that monetary changes always enrich the same group. His commercial life included litigation and an uncertain account of violent death. The surviving economic argument is clearer than many sensational stories about his fortune.

### 18. David Hume: money, trade and automatic adjustment

**David Hume** (1711–1776), born in Edinburgh in a family of modest Scottish landed status, studied at the University of Edinburgh and worked in philosophy, history and diplomacy.

His essays on money, interest and trade developed what became known as the **price-specie-flow mechanism**. Under a metallic monetary standard, an inflow of gold can increase relative prices and thereby reduce exports or encourage imports, potentially reversing some of the initial flow.

The mechanism depends on monetary institutions and adjustment costs. It is not a guarantee that deficits correct themselves painlessly or quickly. Hume's friendship with Adam Smith and his criticism of narrow mercantilist views helped shape a wider theory of commerce among mutually benefiting nations.

## III — Political economy becomes a discipline: physiocrats and classical thinkers

### 19. François Quesnay: the physician who modelled economic circulation

**François Quesnay** (1694–1774) rose from comparatively modest origins to become a physician at the French royal court. His medical training shaped his search for economic interdependence and regular circulation of income.

The ***Tableau économique* (1758)** represented flows of output and payments between social groups. The physiocrats regarded agriculture as the principal source of net product, a classification incompatible with modern measurement of value added in manufacturing and services.

Quesnay advocated reforming taxation and allowing agricultural commerce greater freedom. His table nevertheless influenced later approaches to national accounting and input-output analysis by treating the economy as connected flows rather than isolated exchanges.

### 20. A. R. J. Turgot: capital, liberalization and the political costs of reform

**Anne Robert Jacques Turgot** (1727–1781) was born into a French administrative family, studied in Paris and developed practical experience as an intendant in Limoges. He confronted the actual problems of taxes, grain markets, infrastructure and poor households.

His writings analysed savings, capital advances, prices and productive activity. As controller-general of finance under Louis XVI from **1774**, he attempted to reduce privileges and reform fiscal and commercial restrictions.

Poor harvests, food insecurity and organized opposition weakened his position; he was dismissed in **1776**. The episode demonstrates that a logically defensible policy may encounter implementation costs and political resistance, especially when short-term losses are concentrated.

### 21. Adam Smith: moral philosophy and the wealth of nations

**Adam Smith** (1723–1790) was born in Kirkcaldy, Scotland. His father died before he was born, and his mother **Margaret Douglas** played a significant role in his life. He studied at the **University of Glasgow** and **Balliol College, Oxford**, later becoming a professor of logic and moral philosophy at Glasgow.

In [***The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759)**](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/67363), Smith analysed sympathy, moral judgement and the social formation of norms. People were not merely profit-maximizing machines. His approach to economics remained connected to justice and the credibility of institutions.

Travelling in France introduced Smith to the physiocrats and a wide range of political-economic arguments. In [***An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations* (1776)**](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3300), he examined specialization, the division of labour, market size, capital accumulation, trade, taxation, public works and monopolies.

The pin factory illustrated gains from dividing tasks, while the extent of specialization depended on markets sufficiently large to support it. Smith criticized merchants who used political power to restrict competition and raise prices. His limited references to an **invisible hand** were not proofs that every competitive market necessarily reaches an optimal result under all conditions.

Smith also identified the risks of monotonous labour and acknowledged public roles in justice, defence, infrastructure and education. He never married and lived for long periods with his mother, but these personal facts do not substitute for his arguments. His legacy is the systematic analysis of production, specialization and institutions within a framework of moral philosophy.

### 22. Thomas Robert Malthus: population, food and aggregate demand

**Thomas Robert Malthus** (1766–1834) was raised in an educated English family, studied at **Jesus College, Cambridge**, entered the Anglican clergy and later became a professor at the East India Company College.

In ***An Essay on the Principle of Population* (1798)**, he argued that population growth could outpace food supply in the absence of preventive or material constraints. His celebrated geometric-versus-arithmetic formulation was a theoretical comparison, not a demonstrated universal demographic law.

Subsequent productivity growth, technological innovation, trade and demographic transition showed how elastic food production and fertility behaviour could become. His writing on poor relief remains controversial for its treatment of poverty and individual responsibility.

In ***Principles of Political Economy* (1820)**, Malthus also examined insufficient demand and disagreement with classical interpretations of automatic market adjustment. That debate influenced later discussions of crises, including Keynesian economics.

### 23. David Ricardo: a securities trader theorizes distribution

**David Ricardo** (1772–1823) was born in London to a Sephardic Jewish family with roots in the Netherlands. He learned finance through his father's stockbroking activities, later becoming a wealthy trader. His marriage to **Priscilla Anne Wilkinson** contributed to a rupture with relatives over religion.

Inspired by Smith, Ricardo developed a systematic approach to wages, profits, rent and international trade. In ***Principles of Political Economy and Taxation* (1817)**, he examined how land scarcity and productivity differences affect the distribution of income among workers, farmers and landowners.

His theory of **comparative advantage** demonstrated how two countries might benefit from trade because of differences in relative opportunity cost even when one was absolutely more productive in multiple sectors. The theorem does not guarantee that each region or worker gains after liberalization; adjustment costs and income distribution remain real.

Ricardo entered Parliament and took positions on the Corn Laws. His abstraction was influential precisely because it isolated relationships, though the simplified technology, factor mobility and institutions required careful adaptation.

### 24. Jean-Baptiste Say: enterprise, production and markets

**Jean-Baptiste Say** (1767–1832) was born in Lyon into a merchant family and acquired practical experience in commerce and industry before becoming a writer and professor.

In his ***Treatise on Political Economy* (1803)**, he explored production, utility, entrepreneurs and the relationship between the supply of goods and purchasing power generated through production. The shorthand “supply creates its own demand” was developed and disputed in later literature and does not capture every nuance of Say's own arguments.

His career passed through political regimes that affected publication and business opportunities. His focus on entrepreneurial organization remained important while the Keynesian debate later challenged simplified claims that an economy cannot suffer persistent generalized underemployment.

### 25. J. C. L. de Sismondi: crises, insecure employment and social reform

**Jean Charles Léonard de Sismondi** (1773–1842) grew up in Geneva, studied and worked through political upheavals and became a historian and economist.

Initially influenced by Smith, he increasingly questioned whether rising output necessarily improved the lives of workers. In ***New Principles of Political Economy* (1819)**, he examined crises, insufficient purchasing power, inequality and the vulnerability of workers displaced by industrial change.

His writing gave an early voice to the difference between aggregate production and broad welfare. It lacked the later formalization of Keynes, but contributed to the tradition questioning automatic adjustment and the social effects of industrial growth.

### 26. Harriet Martineau: popular economic writing and the rights of excluded people

**Harriet Martineau** (1802–1876) was born in Norwich into a manufacturing and Unitarian family. She developed through extensive reading and self-education while facing the professional barriers imposed on women and living with significant hearing loss.

Her ***Illustrations of Political Economy* (1832–1834)** used stories to communicate ideas about wages, commerce, taxes and institutions to readers outside universities. She also wrote about slavery, women's rights and American society.

Her economic positions, including some arguments about poor relief, remain open to criticism. The major historical contribution was broadening who could read and debate political economy rather than merely producing academic treatises.

### 27. Frédéric Bastiat: opportunity cost and unseen consequences

**Frédéric Bastiat** (1801–1850) grew up in southwestern France and learned commerce largely outside a formal academic career. He became a political writer and legislator, criticizing monopolies, tariffs and privileged interest groups.

His essay ***What Is Seen and What Is Not Seen*** explained why evaluating a policy solely by its visible beneficiaries can miss what other uses of resources are displaced. The broken-window example illustrates that repairing destruction consumes resources that could otherwise have produced additional goods.

The argument is valuable but does not settle every question about demand shortfalls or idle capacity. Bastiat's legacy lies in clear reasoning about alternative uses of scarce resources and the incentives associated with political privileges.

### 28. Friedrich List: infant industry and national productive capabilities

**Friedrich List** (1789–1846) was born in Reutlingen, worked in government and politics and spent time in the United States during periods of conflict and exile.

In ***The National System of Political Economy* (1841)**, he argued that a developing country may need investment in infrastructure, education and temporarily protected industries to acquire productive capabilities possessed by already industrialized competitors.

Protection can foster learning if accompanied by performance demands and eventual competition; it can also entrench inefficient firms and rent-seeking. List's significance is the recognition that national development depends on building capabilities, not merely accumulating bullion or assuming that all countries begin trade with identical industrial structures.

### 29. John Stuart Mill and Harriet Taylor Mill: production, distribution and liberty

**John Stuart Mill** (1806–1873) was educated intensively by his father **James Mill**, who exposed him early to logic, classics and political economy. He worked for the East India Company and engaged in British politics.

His relationship and later marriage with **Harriet Taylor Mill** (1807–1858) involved sustained intellectual collaboration on women's autonomy, freedom and social organization. In ***Principles of Political Economy* (1848)**, Mill distinguished technical conditions of production from institutional rules governing distribution.

The distinction opened space for examining cooperatives, property, taxation and social reform without assuming every distributional outcome was a law of nature. Mill's liberalism coexisted with tensions in nineteenth-century imperial thinking, which must be examined rather than ignored.

### 30. Karl Marx: philosophy, exile and capital accumulation

**Karl Marx** (1818–1883) was born in Trier in a German family. His father **Heinrich Marx** was a lawyer; his mother was **Henriette Pressburg**. Marx studied at Bonn and Berlin and completed a philosophy doctorate at **Jena in 1841**. Journalism, censorship and political exile shaped his career. His wife **Jenny von Westphalen** and their family endured periods of serious poverty in London.

Influenced by Hegel and classical political economy, Marx developed a critique of capitalist property, labour relations and accumulation. With **Friedrich Engels**, he published the ***Communist Manifesto* (1848)**. The first volume of ***Capital* appeared in 1867**, and Engels later edited additional volumes.

Marx's analysis of value, surplus value and exploitation addressed the relationship between necessary labour, commodity production and the ownership of productive resources. It was not simply the moral statement that all profits are theft. Problems in relating labour values to production prices and heterogeneous capital remained sources of major theoretical debate.

His analysis of concentration, technological change and capitalist crises had enormous influence on economics, history and social movements. Political regimes later invoking Marx must be assessed through their actual institutions and decisions; his theoretical influence does not validate every prediction or subsequent policy carried out in his name.

### 31. Friedrich Engels: the factory experience behind socialist theory

**Friedrich Engels** (1820–1895) was born in Barmen into a family owning textile businesses. He received commercial training and worked in family operations, including in Manchester, where he observed industrial labour and urban poverty.

His ***The Condition of the Working Class in England* (1845)** documented working-class housing, health and employment conditions. Engels collaborated intellectually with Marx and used some of his business income to support the Marx household, creating a tangible contradiction between his anti-capitalist convictions and the property relations from which he obtained resources.

Following Marx's death, Engels edited further volumes of *Capital* and helped develop international socialist networks. His legacy includes empirical social observation, political theory and the transmission of Marxian ideas, rather than merely the role of a wealthy supporter.

### 32. Henry George: land rent and the unearned rise of location values

**Henry George** (1839–1897) was born in Philadelphia, worked as a sailor, printer and journalist, and developed economic ideas outside the academic profession.

In ***Progress and Poverty* (1879)**, he asked why expanding wealth and urban growth could coexist with persistent deprivation. Appreciation of land owing to population and public investment could reward owners who had made no equivalent productive improvement. He advocated a substantial tax on land value, while leaving improvements more lightly taxed.

Estimating site value separately from buildings poses practical difficulties, and transitions affect existing owners and borrowers. Nevertheless, the distinction between private investment and socially generated land rent remains influential in urban economics and municipal finance.


## IV — Marginal analysis, professional economics and the limits of equilibrium

### 33. William Stanley Jevons: mathematics and marginal utility

**William Stanley Jevons** (1835–1882) was born in Liverpool in a family connected to iron trading. He studied science at **University College London**, worked as an assayer in Australia and later developed an academic career in economics, logic and statistics.

In ***The Theory of Political Economy* (1871)**, Jevons emphasized the value of the marginal unit of a good, rather than a fixed amount of labour contained in the object. A good can be indispensable yet have low market value where its supply is abundant relative to immediate needs. Economic choice therefore concerns quantities at the margin and the alternatives forgone.

The approach increased the mathematical precision of economics but raised the problem of measuring utility, which is not an observable physical quantity. His ***The Coal Question* (1865)** also examined resource use and industrial expansion. The **Jevons paradox** describes conditions in which more efficient use of a resource lowers its cost and can stimulate increased total consumption, depending on demand responses.

Jevons died in an accidental drowning in 1882. His legacy connects mathematical choice, statistics and questions of energy consumption that remain relevant to environmental policy.

### 34. Carl Menger: subjective value and the Austrian tradition

**Carl Menger** (1840–1921) was born in the Austrian Empire into an educated family, studied law in Prague and Vienna and earned a doctorate from **Kraków University**. He worked as an economic journalist and civil servant before teaching in Vienna and tutoring **Crown Prince Rudolf**.

In ***Principles of Economics* (1871)**, he analysed how people value goods in relation to their needs and the scarcity of resources capable of satisfying those needs. Inputs and capital goods derive economic importance from their contribution to goods that consumers ultimately want. This approach challenged the notion that labour embodied in a product alone determines its exchange value.

Menger also explained how highly marketable commodities might become money through decentralized commercial processes. That account is a theoretical mechanism, not definitive evidence that every historical currency began as a barter commodity.

His dispute with **Gustav Schmoller** and the German historical school, the *Methodenstreit*, concerned the balance between theory and historically specific evidence. Later Austrian economists developed ideas that should not all be retroactively attributed to Menger.

### 35. Léon Walras: markets as an interdependent system

**Léon Walras** (1834–1910) was born in France and influenced by his father **Auguste Walras**. He followed an uneven early career in study, journalism and cooperative activity before becoming professor at the **University of Lausanne**.

In ***Elements of Pure Economics* (1874–1877)**, he formalized **general equilibrium**: prices in different markets depend on one another through purchases, production and income. A change in the price of wheat may alter wages, rents, other commodity demands and household choices. Analysing wheat in isolation can therefore miss important feedback effects.

Walras's *tâtonnement* method represented a hypothetical price-adjustment process. The existence of a set of equilibrium prices does not establish that real markets reach those prices quickly or that the allocation is fair. Later mathematical economists refined existence proofs under explicit assumptions.

Walras also considered public ownership of land, a reminder that mathematical market analysis is not synonymous with total opposition to state action. His importance lies in providing a framework for interdependence, with limits in explaining financial crises and structural change.

### 36. Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk: capital, waiting and interest

**Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk** (1851–1914) studied law and economics in the Austrian Empire, taught economics and served in public finance, including periods as finance minister.

His ***Capital and Interest*** examined why resources available now can command a different price from future resources, considering time preference, production opportunities and scarcity. Investment requires advances for labour and inputs before revenue arrives. Interest cannot be understood solely as a physical property of a particular machine or a single moral judgement.

Böhm-Bawerk challenged Marxian value theory, especially the relationship between labour values, profit rates and production prices. Marxian writers responded in different ways, and the debate over heterogeneous capital and distribution continued.

The Austrian economist's public service exposed him to the fiscal constraints that theoretical analysis alone cannot resolve. His legacy is a systematic investigation of intertemporal choice and capital structure.

### 37. Alfred Marshall: supply, demand and the professionalization of economics

**Alfred Marshall** (1842–1924) studied mathematics at **St John's College, Cambridge**, then became an economist and university teacher. His wife **Mary Paley Marshall**, an early female student at Cambridge, was an economist and coauthor of earlier work.

In ***Principles of Economics* (1890)**, Marshall connected marginal utility, production costs, competition and the timing of adjustments. Supply may be difficult to change in the short run, while in the long run firms can enter, leave or invest. The famous crossing supply and demand curves was a useful tool for analysing a specific market, not the complete description of an economy.

His concept of **price elasticity of demand** measures responsiveness of quantity to changes in prices under defined conditions. Consumer surplus, industry dynamics and external economies became important tools for policy analysis. But partial equilibrium diagrams can conceal distributional conflicts and cross-market effects.

Marshall's role in building economics as a specialized university discipline was substantial. That institutional achievement existed alongside restrictions on women's academic careers and the dominance of particular European intellectual networks.

### 38. Francis Ysidro Edgeworth: bargaining and the geometry of exchange

**Francis Ysidro Edgeworth** (1845–1926) was born in Ireland and studied at **Trinity College Dublin** and Oxford. He learned much of his mathematics independently and became an economist and statistician.

In ***Mathematical Psychics* (1881)**, Edgeworth used mathematical reasoning to analyse exchange and bargaining. The later **Edgeworth box** describes allocations between two people given their resources and preferences. When a small number of parties negotiate, there may be many outcomes acceptable to both; competition under restrictive conditions can narrow the set.

The geometry illustrates the difference between feasible exchanges and the distribution initially available to participants. An allocation can be efficient without being socially just. His research also helped develop the analytical and editorial institutions of professional economics.

### 39. Vilfredo Pareto: efficiency, income concentration and elite politics

**Vilfredo Pareto** (1848–1923) was born in Paris into an Italian family and trained as an engineer in Turin. Industrial work preceded his academic appointment at Lausanne.

The **Pareto efficiency** criterion describes an allocation in which improving one person's position requires worsening another's under a given set of feasible alternatives. It is not a measure of equality or legitimacy. A deeply unequal outcome can meet the criterion.

Pareto also studied statistical patterns in income and wealth concentration, later associated with the Pareto distribution. The popular “80/20 rule” is not an unchanging empirical law he demonstrated for every economy.

His later sociology and theory of elites intersected with controversial political contexts. The enduring value of his economic contribution is the distinction between a formal efficiency test and judgments about fairness.

### 40. Knut Wicksell: bank interest and the cumulative price process

**Knut Wicksell** (1851–1926) studied at **Uppsala University**, developed a broad interest in mathematics and political reform and taught at Lund.

In ***Interest and Prices* (1898)**, he distinguished the interest rate charged in the credit system from a hypothetical natural rate associated with real investment and saving. A persistent gap could lead to cumulative changes in demand and prices as banks expanded or restricted credit.

The natural rate cannot be observed directly and varies with economic circumstances, limiting the usefulness of treating monetary policy as an automatic adjustment of a single number. Expectations, credit constraints and real shocks also matter.

Wicksell provided foundations for later monetary theory and inflation-targeting frameworks, while also writing on taxation and public finance.

### 41. Irving Fisher: measurement, interest and debt-deflation

**Irving Fisher** (1867–1947) studied at **Yale**, earning advanced training in mathematics and economics, and pursued a career ranging from monetary analysis to public-health advocacy. He married **Margaret Hazard** and invested in businesses linked to recording systems.

Fisher developed monetary identities, theories of interest and intertemporal consumption. ***The Theory of Interest* (1930)** analysed the relationship between present and future resources. His equation of exchange was influential, but an accounting relationship does not by itself determine the causal effects of monetary changes.

Fisher was badly wrong in his optimism about stock prices immediately before the **1929 crash** and suffered major financial losses. He subsequently developed ***The Debt-Deflation Theory of Great Depressions* (1933)**, examining how falling prices raise the real burden of nominal debts, force asset sales and deepen contractions.

His mistakes as an investor did not erase the importance of his later analysis. Indeed, the experience illustrates the difference between theoretical innovation and the ability to predict turning points.

### 42. Thorstein Veblen: status, spending and institutional habits

**Thorstein Veblen** (1857–1929) was born in Wisconsin into a Norwegian immigrant farming family, studied at **Carleton**, **Johns Hopkins** and **Yale**, and faced professional conflicts over his career.

In ***The Theory of the Leisure Class* (1899)**, Veblen studied **conspicuous consumption**: expenditure may communicate wealth and social status rather than serve only direct physical needs. This does not mean that every expensive purchase is merely a status performance. It shows how comparison, imitation and hierarchy can shape demand.

His institutional approach examined habits, corporations, technology and business power. It influenced later research on social status and economic institutions, though many of his claims require more explicit empirical specification than he supplied.

### 43. John R. Commons: law, labour and collective action

**John Rogers Commons** (1862–1945) studied at **Oberlin** and **Johns Hopkins**, then became a professor and reformer, especially in Wisconsin.

Commons treated economic transactions as relationships governed by law, contracts and collective bargaining. In ***Institutional Economics* (1934)**, he described how legal rights, unions, arbitration and organizational rules affect bargaining power and distribution. Wages are not determined in a legal vacuum.

His influence on American labour and social policy reflected empirical research as well as theoretical argument. The later rise of formal neoclassical modelling reduced the prominence of older institutionalism, but problems of contract enforcement and organization returned through new methods.

### 44. Rosa Luxemburg: accumulation and imperial expansion

**Rosa Luxemburg** (1871–1919) was born in Zamość, then part of the Russian Empire, into a Jewish Polish family. She studied at **Zurich**, one of the few universities then admitting women, and earned a doctorate in **1897** on Polish industrial development.

In ***The Accumulation of Capital* (1913)**, she explored the relationship between capitalist reproduction and non-capitalist markets, arguing that imperial expansion was connected to the requirements of accumulation. Marxist critics challenged her reading of Marx's reproduction models and argued that growth was possible without the particular external markets she posited.

Luxemburg also defended democratic participation and criticized authoritarian tendencies in revolutionary politics. She was murdered amid political violence in **1919**. Her work remains influential in studies of imperialism, economic coercion and the world market, while its strongest necessity claims remain debated.

### 45. Mary Paley Marshall: education and unequal academic recognition

**Mary Paley Marshall** (1850–1944) studied at **Newnham College, Cambridge**, when women faced formal barriers to degrees and university appointments. She taught economics and coauthored ***The Economics of Industry* (1879)** with Alfred Marshall.

Marriage and scholarly collaboration took place in an institutional system that gave considerably more recognition and professional opportunity to men. This does not justify assigning all of Alfred Marshall's published work to Mary; it does show how authorship, opportunity and prestige are conditioned by university rules.

A history based only on titled professorships and prizes will systematically omit contributions made when many women were excluded from those roles.

## V — Depression, war and the invention of macroeconomics

### 46. Joseph Schumpeter: entrepreneurs and creative destruction

**Joseph Alois Schumpeter** (1883–1950) was born in the Austrian Empire, studied at the **University of Vienna**, taught economics, served as Austrian finance minister and briefly pursued commercial banking before establishing a major academic career at **Harvard**.

In ***The Theory of Economic Development* (1911)**, he placed entrepreneurial innovation at the centre of structural change. New products, business methods, markets and organizational forms could challenge incumbents, alter profits and destroy the value of established capital. Bank credit could finance an innovator before new income existed.

In ***Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy* (1942)**, he developed the idea of **creative destruction**. The mechanism explains productivity gains and business displacement but does not establish that all workers immediately benefit from innovation. Skill loss, unemployment and regional decline can persist when adaptation is difficult.

Personal financial difficulties and controversial political predictions form part of his biography. His ideas later informed evolutionary economics and modern models of innovation-led growth.

### 47. John Maynard Keynes: uncertainty and persistent unemployment

**John Maynard Keynes** (1883–1946) was born in Cambridge to the economist **John Neville Keynes** and educator **Florence Ada Brown**. He studied at **Eton** and **King's College, Cambridge**, initially focusing on mathematics and philosophy. He worked in government finance, academia and financial markets and married the dancer **Lydia Lopokova**.

Keynes criticized the economic terms associated with the First World War peace settlement in ***The Economic Consequences of the Peace* (1919)**, warning about the destructive effects of excessive reparations. His assessments have been debated, but the episode placed international production and political stability at the centre of economic policy.

In ***The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* (1936)**, Keynes challenged the idea that a market economy necessarily returns quickly to full employment. Investment depends on uncertain future revenue; households and firms may simultaneously seek liquidity, reducing aggregate spending and income. Underemployment can persist even without a single externally imposed wage restriction.

The **multiplier** describes how an initial spending change can propagate through incomes and expenditure. Its magnitude depends on taxes, imports, saving, available capacity and policy conditions. It does not allow every government project to be justified by applying one fixed coefficient.

Keynes participated in the design of postwar monetary institutions and advocated an international clearing arrangement built around the **bancor**, an idea not adopted at Bretton Woods. His legacy includes fiscal and monetary stabilization as well as an emphasis on genuine uncertainty that later simplified Keynesian models sometimes neglected.

### 48. Michał Kalecki: profits and the politics of full employment

**Michał Kalecki** (1899–1970) was born in Łódź into a family connected to industry and commerce. Financial problems affected his formal education, and he developed skills through employment, research and economic statistics.

Kalecki independently developed important ideas about profits, investment and aggregate spending. His analysis linked profits to decisions to invest and spend, with accounting relationships that depend on how income and saving are distributed. He also examined prices and **degree of monopoly**, allowing market power to affect margins and income shares.

In ***Political Aspects of Full Employment* (1943)**, he argued that employers might oppose the political and bargaining consequences of prolonged full employment even where government policy could technically sustain demand. Such opposition is conditional, not a claim that every entrepreneur resists growth.

Kalecki later worked internationally and in Poland, maintaining a critical perspective on capitalist and centrally administered economies. His achievement was connecting macroeconomic dynamics to class relations and political incentives.

### 49. Friedrich Hayek: dispersed knowledge and the limits of planning

**Friedrich August von Hayek** (1899–1992) was born in Vienna into an academic family, studied law and economics at the **University of Vienna** and pursued research on credit cycles, institutions and political philosophy.

Hayek taught at the **London School of Economics**, **University of Chicago** and other institutions. In ***The Use of Knowledge in Society* (1945)**, he argued that information about local production, preferences and resource availability is widely dispersed. Prices can communicate changes in scarcity without any central authority collecting all relevant knowledge.

His criticism of central planning focused on the difficulty of coordinating millions of decisions where information changes continuously. Socialists such as **Oskar Lange** proposed alternative market-socialist mechanisms, and the debate concerned actual information, incentives and adaptation rather than the mere presence of computers.

In ***The Road to Serfdom* (1944)**, Hayek warned about concentrating political and economic power. Some associations and comments involving authoritarian governments remain controversial, and his career should not be reduced to uncritical advocacy of every private company. He shared the **1974 economics prize** with Gunnar Myrdal.

### 50. Gunnar Myrdal: cumulative disadvantage and social institutions

**Gunnar Myrdal** (1898–1987) was born in Sweden, studied law in **Stockholm** and developed careers in economics, politics and diplomacy. His wife **Alva Myrdal** was an important public intellectual and policymaker in her own right.

Myrdal proposed **circular and cumulative causation**: poor access to education, capital and political voice can reinforce low productivity and income, while successful institutional improvements may create positive feedback. The model is not a prediction that poor countries must remain poor.

In ***An American Dilemma* (1944)**, he investigated racism and the conflict between American democratic ideals and exclusionary institutions. The project relied on wider research networks whose contributions deserve recognition. His later ***Asian Drama* (1968)** examined institutions and development in Asia, though some interpretations were challenged by subsequent growth experiences.

Myrdal's shared **1974 prize with Hayek** illustrated the broad and often conflicting traditions recognized in the discipline.

### 51. Karl Polanyi: markets and society's protective responses

**Karl Polanyi** (1886–1964) was born in Vienna and grew up in Budapest. He studied law and philosophy, worked as a journalist and researcher and experienced the economic and political crises of the interwar years.

In ***The Great Transformation* (1944)**, he argued that market economies were constructed through legal and political changes, particularly when labour, land and money were treated as if they were ordinary commodities produced for sale. He called them **fictitious commodities** because human beings, nature and monetary systems are not produced for the market in the same manner as manufactured goods.

The **double movement** described attempts to expand market coordination and responses seeking social protection. It does not imply that all regulation succeeds or that every free-market reform causes authoritarianism. The work remains influential in economic sociology and the analysis of political reactions to inequality and insecurity.

### 52. John Hicks: mathematical synthesis and the limits of IS-LM

**John Richard Hicks** (1904–1989) studied at **Balliol College, Oxford**, and taught at major British universities. His mathematical work on demand, capital and welfare helped connect neoclassical economics with the expanding Keynesian tradition.

In ***Value and Capital* (1939)**, Hicks analysed substitution and intertemporal equilibrium. His **IS-LM framework**, first published in **1937**, became a standard way to describe conditions in goods and money markets and the effects of fiscal and monetary policies.

The simplification was useful pedagogically but neglected some of Keynes's concerns about deep uncertainty and historical time. Hicks later acknowledged limitations of the framework. He shared the **1972 economics prize** with Kenneth Arrow.

### 53. Joan Robinson: imperfect competition and the capital debates

**Joan Violet Robinson** (1903–1983) studied at **Girton College, Cambridge**, and became an influential economist in a university system where women faced restrictions on professional advancement. She married the economist **Austin Robinson** and participated in the debates around Keynes, Sraffa and Kahn.

In ***The Economics of Imperfect Competition* (1933)**, she analysed firms with market power, labour pricing and the relationship between costs, output and margins. Perfect competition was not an adequate description of all industrial sectors.

Robinson later criticized the treatment of heterogeneous capital as a single measurable physical stock independent of prices and income distribution. These questions became part of the **Cambridge capital controversy**.

Her views on certain socialist regimes generated justified criticism for political judgements that underestimated repression and other harms. Her theoretical contributions and political errors can be examined separately.

### 54. Piero Sraffa: production prices and the theory of capital

**Piero Sraffa** (1898–1983) was born in Turin, studied economics and law, was close to **Antonio Gramsci** and developed an academic career at Cambridge, where he interacted with Keynes and Wittgenstein.

In ***Production of Commodities by Means of Commodities* (1960)**, he set out a mathematical system relating production techniques, prices and the distribution between wages and profits. It challenged interpretations relying on a homogeneous quantity of capital and conventional marginal productivity arguments.

Sraffa's framework is not a complete description of money, unemployment or all institutions. It is a rigorous model that exposes assumptions behind alternative theories of value and capital. His editorial work on David Ricardo also made a lasting contribution to the history of economic thought.

### 55. Hyman Minsky: stability creates incentives for instability

**Hyman P. Minsky** (1919–1996) studied mathematics at the **University of Chicago** and economics at **Harvard**, later developing a career in financial instability research.

His **financial instability hypothesis** described how prolonged stability can encourage debtors and lenders to accept greater leverage and thinner margins of safety. He distinguished **hedge finance**, where expected income covers interest and principal; **speculative finance**, where debt must be rolled over; and **Ponzi finance**, where refinancing or rising asset values are needed even to meet current commitments.

These categories depend on actual balance-sheet cash flows and financing conditions. They do not imply that every expansion inevitably produces a crash. The **2008 crisis** renewed interest in Minsky, although he had died well before it occurred.

His contribution is a dynamic view of finance in which risk can accumulate endogenously during periods that appear tranquil.


## VI — Statistics, econometrics and quantitative reconstruction

### 56. Eugen Slutsky: the mathematics of choice

**Eugen Slutsky** (1880–1948) was born in the Russian Empire and studied science and mathematics before working in statistics and economic theory amid institutional upheavals.

His **1915 demand analysis** distinguished the **substitution effect** from the **income effect** of a price change. A higher price makes substitutes relatively attractive and simultaneously changes a consumer's real purchasing power. The decomposition became fundamental to modern consumer theory, taxation and subsidy analysis.

Slutsky also showed how the sum of random disturbances can produce apparently cyclical statistical series, raising an enduring problem: apparent economic regularity may emerge without a single deterministic cycle mechanism. Recognition of his work spread slowly across academic networks separated by language and politics.

### 57. Ragnar Frisch: econometrics as an independent scientific language

**Ragnar Frisch** (1895–1973) was born in Norway in a family associated with work in precious metals and studied economics and mathematics at the **University of Oslo**. His training combined technical measurement and economic theory.

Frisch helped establish **econometrics**, linking economic models, probability and observations. His work on cycles, production and economic planning also contributed to distinguishing macroeconomic movements from individual decisions.

He shared the inaugural **1969 economics prize** with Jan Tinbergen. The prize in economic sciences had been established in 1968 by Sweden's central bank and was not one of the original categories in Alfred Nobel's will.

The enduring contribution was the demand to assess whether theoretical relationships can be confronted with evidence, while recognizing that a regression coefficient does not automatically prove causation.

### 58. Jan Tinbergen: macroeconomic models and policy instruments

**Jan Tinbergen** (1903–1994) studied physics at **Leiden University** and moved into economics and national economic planning. His scientific background encouraged the use of simultaneous equations to describe consumption, investment, income and employment.

Tinbergen developed pioneering macroeconomic models of the Netherlands and the United States. He also articulated the policy problem of matching independent objectives with sufficient policy instruments. A government seeking multiple distinct targets may be unable to reach them with a single instrument.

Keynes criticized aspects of early macroeconomic econometrics, particularly the interpretation of correlations and instability of estimated relationships. Tinbergen's long-run impact nevertheless extended to planning, forecasting and development studies.

### 59. Trygve Haavelmo: probability and causal inference

**Trygve Haavelmo** (1911–1999) was born in Norway and studied economic statistics with Frisch before working in Europe and the United States.

His ***The Probability Approach in Econometrics* (1944)** established a formal approach to statistical inference in economic systems. Rather than treating residuals as inconvenient noise attached after the theory was constructed, Haavelmo placed probability at the centre of modelling, estimation and hypothesis testing.

The framework helped address simultaneous equations and causal identification, though identification remained dependent on economically justified assumptions. He received the **1989 economics prize** for foundational econometric work.

### 60. Wassily Leontief: tracing productive interdependencies

**Wassily Leontief** (1905–1999) was born in St Petersburg and studied in Russia and Germany before working in the United States, mainly at **Harvard**.

His **input-output analysis** used matrices to represent the goods and services each industry requires from others. Vehicle production, for example, depends on metals, energy, transport and business services. A demand shock for vehicles can therefore affect suppliers far beyond the car factory.

Leontief received the **1973 economics prize**. His empirical work on international trade also raised questions about simple predictions of factor-endowment theories, producing the **Leontief paradox**.

Input-output tables are especially useful for mapping material dependencies, but fixed technical coefficients do not fully capture substitution, changing technology or price-induced adjustments.

### 61. Leonid Kantorovich and Tjalling Koopmans: optimizing scarce resources

**Leonid Kantorovich** (1912–1986) was trained as a mathematician in Leningrad and worked on resource allocation in Soviet production. **Tjalling Koopmans** (1910–1985), born in the Netherlands, studied mathematics, physics and economics and later developed his career in the United States.

Both contributed to **linear programming**, activity analysis and methods for allocating resources under production constraints. A firm or planner can specify an objective and account for limited materials, machinery, labour hours and transport capacity.

The mathematical solution depends on the accuracy of its objectives, constraints and data. It cannot establish whether the objective itself is socially desirable. The two shared the **1975 economics prize**, illustrating the relevance of optimization to private logistics and public planning alike.

### 62. Simon Kuznets: measuring growth without confusing it with welfare

**Simon Kuznets** (1901–1985) was born in the Russian Empire, moved to the United States and studied economics at **Columbia University**. His work at the **National Bureau of Economic Research** helped establish systematic measurement of national income and long-run growth.

Kuznets recognized that monetary output is not a complete measure of welfare. Domestic unpaid work, environmental damage and inequality can be hidden within a rising aggregate.

His famous **Kuznets curve** proposed that inequality might initially increase and later decline over development. The pattern is not a universal law: tax systems, education, migration, unions and political institutions strongly affect distribution.

He received the **1971 economics prize**. His legacy combines historical measurement with the warning that the meaning of an indicator depends on its construction.

## VII — Postwar macroeconomics and growth

### 63. Paul Samuelson: mathematical synthesis and economic education

**Paul Samuelson** (1915–2009) studied at the **University of Chicago** and **Harvard** and became a professor at **MIT**.

In ***Foundations of Economic Analysis* (1947)**, he established the use of mathematical optimization and equilibrium conditions across many economic problems. His ***Economics* textbook (1948)** combined neoclassical microeconomics with Keynesian macroeconomics, influencing students worldwide.

Samuelson developed theories of trade, consumer behaviour, public goods and dynamics. His condition for efficient provision of a public good requires comparing the combined marginal benefits of users with marginal social cost under stated assumptions. It does not specify a politically acceptable tax distribution by itself.

He became the first American recipient of the economics prize, in **1970**. His synthesis was challenged by inflation, monetarism and rational-expectations models, but its analytical methods persisted across schools.

### 64. Robert Solow: capital, labour and productivity

**Robert M. Solow** (1924–2023) studied at **Harvard** and became a leading economist at **MIT**.

His **1956 growth model** showed how capital accumulation, labour growth and technological progress influence output over time. With diminishing returns to capital under specified assumptions, adding machines to workers cannot indefinitely support the same rate of output growth per person without technical progress.

Growth accounting distinguishes contributions of measured labour and capital from an estimated residual. The residual is not a pure measure of invention; it can reflect errors, improved factor quality and changes in organization.

Solow received the **1987 economics prize**. The model provided a benchmark for development comparisons but does not guarantee convergence without attention to institutions, investment, technology access and population.

### 65. Nicholas Kaldor and Roy Harrod: industry and unstable growth

**Nicholas Kaldor** (1908–1986) was born in Budapest, studied at the **London School of Economics** and later worked at Cambridge. **Roy Harrod** (1900–1978), trained at **Oxford**, was close to Keynes and contributed to theories of growth.

Harrod linked saving, investment and the growth of capacity, emphasizing potential instability when actual expansion diverges from the pace needed to maintain capacity use. Kaldor studied distribution, manufacturing and cumulative growth, arguing that industrial sectors can produce productivity spillovers to other activities.

The **Kaldor laws** are empirically contingent, not guarantees that any protected factory will generate productivity gains. Their work kept demand and industrial structure in the discussion of long-run growth, challenging the view that a single aggregate production function tells the whole story.

### 66. Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz: monetary history as an argument about policy

**Milton Friedman** (1912–2006) was born in Brooklyn to an immigrant family and studied at **Rutgers**, the **University of Chicago** and **Columbia**. He married the economist **Rose Director Friedman**, who collaborated in intellectual and public projects.

**Anna Jacobson Schwartz** (1915–2012) studied at **Barnard College** and **Columbia** and developed a long career at the **National Bureau of Economic Research**. She was an influential monetary historian in her own right.

Together, Friedman and Schwartz produced ***A Monetary History of the United States, 1867–1960* (1963)**, showing the role of banking failures and monetary contraction in the severity of the Great Depression. The argument forced reassessment of monetary authorities' responsibilities, although gold-standard constraints, international trade and debt-deflation remain essential additional mechanisms.

Friedman advocated floating exchange rates, policy rules and greater reliance on markets. His claim that persistent inflation is fundamentally monetary concerns sustained nominal dynamics, not denial of all short-run supply shocks. Changes in money demand and financial innovation complicated mechanical control of monetary aggregates.

Friedman received the **1976 prize**; Schwartz did not. Her exclusion from that award should not erase her coauthorship or independent contributions.

### 67. James Tobin and Franco Modigliani: financial choices across time

**James Tobin** (1918–2002) studied at **Harvard** and taught at **Yale**, examining portfolios, money demand, investment and stabilization. The **q ratio associated with Tobin** compares market valuation of capital with replacement cost, helping frame investment incentives.

**Franco Modigliani** (1918–2003) was born in Rome, studied in Europe and immigrated to the United States amid war and persecution. His **life-cycle consumption theory** examined how individuals attempt to smooth spending across their working and retirement years.

Tobin received the **1981 prize** and Modigliani the **1985 prize**. Their frameworks describe important intertemporal decisions, but constrained households cannot always borrow or save in the way assumed by simple models. Welfare policy, wages, health and access to finance alter actual outcomes.

### 68. Robert Lucas: rational expectations and changing policy rules

**Robert E. Lucas Jr.** (1937–2023) studied at the **University of Chicago**, becoming a leading figure in the new classical macroeconomic tradition.

The **Lucas critique of 1976** argued that statistical relationships estimated under one policy regime may change when people understand and respond to new rules. An apparent link between inflation and unemployment cannot automatically be exploited again and again without altering wage and price expectations.

His approach required consistency between macroeconomic policy and individual decision-making. It did not prove that all people possess perfect information or that monetary and fiscal policy can never stabilize activity.

Lucas received the **1995 prize**. Financial crises and evidence of sticky prices, heterogeneous households and credit constraints led subsequent economists to revise and extend his framework.

### 69. Sargent, Sims, Kydland and Prescott: expectations, identification and credible commitments

**Thomas Sargent** (born 1943) studied at **Berkeley** and **Harvard** and helped build models of rational expectations and policy. **Christopher Sims** (born 1942), trained at **Harvard**, developed **vector autoregression (VAR)** and statistical tools for analysing dynamic macroeconomic relationships. They shared the **2011 prize**.

**Finn Kydland** (born 1943), trained in Norway and at **Carnegie Mellon**, and **Edward Prescott** (1940–2022), trained in mathematics and operations research, investigated real business cycles and **time inconsistency**. Policymakers may have incentives to break earlier commitments once private actors have acted on them; expectations and credibility therefore shape policy effectiveness. They shared the **2004 prize**.

These models clarify policy incentives and dynamics, but simplified versions have been criticized for omitting banking fragility, involuntary unemployment and distributional differences. Their analytical power rests in explicit assumptions, not in the claim that one representative household describes every crisis.

### 70. Robert E. Hall and Michael Woodford: the 2026 macroeconomic prize

**Robert E. Hall** (born 1943) studied at **Berkeley** and earned a doctorate at **MIT in 1967**, later becoming a leading economist at **Stanford**. His research involved labour markets, consumption, productivity and macroeconomic fluctuations.

**Michael Woodford** (born 1955) earned his doctorate at **MIT in 1983** and developed a career associated with **Columbia University**. In ***Interest and Prices* (2003)**, he developed a framework connecting interest-rate policy, sticky prices and expectations of future monetary conditions.

On **12 October 2026**, the [Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences](https://www.kva.se/en/news/the-prize-in-economic-sciences-2026/) named Hall and Woodford joint economics laureates for work on the **microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilization**. Their contributions improve analysis of how individual decisions and expectations transmit fiscal and monetary policies to output, prices and employment.

The recognition does not establish perfect forecasting or a universally optimal interest-rate rule. Policy effects depend on the structure of labour markets, credit, production and institutions.

```flow
Research on household and firm decisions → models of labour, consumption, prices and investment → aggregate dynamics and expectations → fiscal and monetary policy instruments → effects on output, inflation and employment → empirical evaluation and revision
```

## VIII — Development, colonialism and the limits of universal policy prescriptions

### 71. Raúl Prebisch: international trade and Latin America's productive structure

**Raúl Prebisch** (1901–1986) was born in Tucumán, Argentina, and studied at the **University of Buenos Aires**. He worked in statistical and monetary policy, including leadership at the **Central Bank of Argentina**, established in 1935.

The Great Depression and Latin America's reliance on primary commodity exports shaped his analysis. At the **Economic Commission for Latin America (ECLAC)**, he developed the distinction between economic **centre and periphery**, emphasizing unequal technological capabilities and asymmetry in the distribution of international growth gains.

The **Prebisch–Singer hypothesis** raised the possibility of deterioration in the long-run relative prices of some primary products. The result varies across commodities, periods and methods; it is not a universal rule governing every resource-exporting country.

Prebisch advocated industrial diversification, but the success of import substitution depends on competitive discipline, technology, infrastructure and macroeconomic conditions. His legacy is the demand to analyse the productive structure and international position of developing economies.

### 72. Hans Singer: the risks of narrow export specialization

**Hans W. Singer** (1910–2006) was born in Germany, studied economics and left amid Nazi persecution. He later worked as an academic and for international organizations.

He analysed how specialization in primary commodities could make countries vulnerable to price fluctuations and to unequal sharing of productivity gains. His work contributed to the thesis associated with Prebisch.

Singer's argument was not that all agricultural exporters must remain poor. Industrial upgrading, technology and institutions may change outcomes. Trends in prices depend on product baskets, quality adjustments and historical periods; broad claims require careful empirical specification.

### 73. Arthur Lewis: dual economies and labour migration

**W. Arthur Lewis** (1915–1991) was born in **Saint Lucia** and studied at the **London School of Economics**, later developing a global academic and policy career.

In ***Economic Development with Unlimited Supplies of Labour* (1954)**, Lewis described a developing economy with traditional and modern sectors. Capital accumulation in the modern sector could attract workers from low-productivity activities, increasing output and changing wages over time.

The model must be adapted where rural labour is highly productive, workers lack access to skills or cities expand informal employment instead of formal industrial jobs. It captures a structural transition, not the exact path of every country.

Lewis received the **1979 prize** with Theodore Schultz. His importance lies in making differences between sectors inside one economy central to growth theory.

### 74. Albert Hirschman: linkages and the politics of development

**Albert O. Hirschman** (1915–2012) was born in Berlin, studied across Europe, engaged in anti-fascist activities and emigrated to the United States.

In ***The Strategy of Economic Development* (1958)**, he argued that investments can create **backward and forward linkages**: new industries increase demand for suppliers and enable new downstream activities. Development need not start with every sector growing simultaneously; selected investments can reveal the next set of bottlenecks.

In ***Exit, Voice, and Loyalty* (1970)**, he analysed how customers and citizens respond when organizations deteriorate: they may leave, protest or remain loyal. The availability of exit and voice changes bargaining power.

His legacy is caution against grand development blueprints that assume the future can be completely specified in advance. Institutional learning often occurs through unexpected constraints and responses.

### 75. Gunnar Myrdal and Asian development: cumulative processes revisited

Myrdal's ***Asian Drama* (1968)** linked development to land institutions, education, administration and persistent social inequality. It challenged economists who assumed prices and savings alone explained poverty.

Subsequent industrialization in different Asian economies complicated some of its predictions and revealed paths that the analysis had not anticipated. This does not invalidate the concept of institutional feedback; it shows the danger of treating historically situated assessments as permanent constraints on a region.

A useful evaluation of Myrdal must combine his concern with social conditions and the contrasting evidence produced by later development trajectories.

### 76. Amartya Sen: entitlements, famine and capabilities

**Amartya Sen** (born 1933) was born in **Santiniketan**, studied in Calcutta and at **Trinity College, Cambridge**, and developed research spanning philosophy, welfare and development.

In ***Poverty and Famines* (1981)**, Sen showed why hunger may result from a collapse of people's ability to acquire food rather than only from aggregate shortages. Wages, employment, relative prices and legal entitlements determine access to available food.

The **capabilities approach** assesses whether people can actually pursue meaningful lives through health, education, safety and freedom, rather than using income as a complete measure. Public spending and growth matter because of what they enable people to achieve.

Sen received the **1998 economics prize**. His work also advanced social choice and democratic reasoning, emphasizing that welfare measures must acknowledge diverse human circumstances and rights.

### 77. Douglass North: institutions, transaction costs and history

**Douglass C. North** (1920–2015) studied at **University of California, Berkeley**, and became a leading economic historian and institutional economist.

In ***Institutions, Institutional Change and Economic Performance* (1990)**, he distinguished institutions — formal and informal rules — from organizations operating within those rules. Property law, courts, enforcement and norms influence cooperation and the costs of transactions.

His concept of **path dependence** describes how existing institutions create incentives and barriers affecting subsequent change. The past matters without determining the future mechanically.

North shared the **1993 prize** with **Robert Fogel**, whose quantitative economic history included research on railroads and slavery. The broad causal role assigned to institutions requires careful empirical evidence rather than simple rankings of countries.

### 78. Elinor Ostrom: collective resources and self-governance

**Elinor Clair Awan Ostrom** (1933–2012) was born in Los Angeles, studied political science at **UCLA**, earning a doctorate in **1965**, and worked extensively at **Indiana University**. She married political scientist **Vincent Ostrom** in 1963. The [Nobel biographical record](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2009/ostrom/facts/) documents her career.

Ostrom studied irrigation, forests, pastures and fisheries governed by their users. Her fieldwork demonstrated that common resources need not be overexploited in every circumstance. Users can establish boundaries, monitoring, sanctions and procedures to resolve disputes.

The conditions for success vary by resource, geography, community and public authority. Neither universal privatization nor centralized command provides a guaranteed solution.

Ostrom received the **2009 prize**, becoming the first woman laureate in economics. She broadened economic theory by documenting how real communities build governance institutions.

### 79. Oliver Williamson: the firm and the cost of contracts

**Oliver E. Williamson** (1932–2020) studied engineering, management and economics at **MIT**, **Stanford** and **Carnegie Mellon**, building on Ronald Coase's insights.

A firm may perform activities internally because contracting through markets requires search, negotiation, monitoring and enforcement. Highly specialized equipment or long relationships may expose parties to opportunistic renegotiation, making integration or special contractual safeguards valuable.

Internal organization also creates costs: bureaucracy, poor incentives and loss of flexibility. Williamson's framework therefore does not imply that every merger is efficient.

He shared the **2009 economics prize** with Ostrom. His work supplied practical tools for analysing outsourcing, vertical integration and governance.

### 80. Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson and James Robinson: institutions and prosperity

**Daron Acemoglu** (born 1967) was born in Istanbul, studied at **York** and earned a doctorate at the **London School of Economics**. **Simon Johnson** (born 1963) developed training in economics and politics in Britain and earned a doctorate at **MIT**. **James Robinson** (born 1960) studied economics and political history, including doctoral work at **Yale**.

Their research examined how political institutions, property rights and the distribution of power influence prosperity and innovation. Historical work on colonization investigated links between institutional arrangements and persistent economic differences.

They received the **2024 economics prize**, documented by the [Royal Swedish Academy's award release](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2024/press-release/).

The analysis has been challenged on instrumental-variable methods, historical measures and overly binary categories of inclusion and extraction. Its enduring achievement is to show that economic development cannot be understood independently of political institutions.

## IX — Contracts, information and collective choice

### 81. Ronald Coase: the company and the social cost of transactions

**Ronald H. Coase** (1910–2013) studied at the **London School of Economics** and developed his career in the United States, particularly at the **University of Chicago**.

In ***The Nature of the Firm* (1937)**, he asked why businesses organize work internally instead of purchasing every task separately through markets. The answer involved the costs of finding suppliers, writing contracts, monitoring compliance and settling disputes.

In ***The Problem of Social Cost* (1960)**, he analysed conflicting uses of resources. Under extremely restrictive assumptions of zero transaction costs and well-defined rights, bargaining may lead to efficient outcomes regardless of the initial assignment of rights, though distribution differs. In real societies, negotiation costs and unequal power make institutions essential.

Coase received the **1991 prize**. His argument was not that government is never needed; it was that real transaction costs must be included in economic reasoning.

### 82. Kenneth Arrow and Gérard Debreu: general equilibrium and social impossibility

**Kenneth Arrow** (1921–2017) studied at **City College of New York** and **Columbia**, later working at Stanford and Harvard. **Gérard Debreu** (1921–2004), born in France, trained in mathematics at the **École normale supérieure**.

Their work established conditions under which a competitive general equilibrium can exist in an abstract economy. Mathematical existence is not evidence of rapid convergence, full information or social fairness in actual markets.

Arrow also proved an **impossibility theorem** showing that certain plausible conditions for aggregating individual preferences into a collective ranking cannot all hold in general. It is a formal result about social-choice rules, not proof that democratic government itself is impossible.

Arrow shared the **1972 prize** with John Hicks; Debreu received the **1983 prize**. Their legacy is rigorous theory with the corresponding obligation to identify assumptions.

### 83. George Akerlof, Michael Spence and Joseph Stiglitz: unequal information

**George Akerlof** (born 1940) studied at **Yale** and **MIT**. In ***The Market for “Lemons”* (1970)**, he showed how uncertainty about product quality can drive good sellers from a market and reduce exchange.

**Michael Spence** (born 1943), trained at **Princeton**, **Oxford** and **Harvard**, developed models of **signalling** through education and other costly actions. **Joseph Stiglitz** (born 1943), trained at **Amherst** and **MIT**, researched incentives, screening, insurance and credit under asymmetric information.

The three shared the **2001 prize**. Their contributions changed the presumption that buyers and sellers generally know the same facts. They help explain why insurance, lending and labour markets may not clear through prices alone.

The existence of asymmetric information does not automatically determine the best regulation. Disclosure, competition, guarantees and oversight have their own costs and institutional requirements.

### 84. John Nash, John Harsanyi and Reinhard Selten: strategic interaction

**John Forbes Nash Jr.** (1928–2015) studied at **Carnegie Institute of Technology** and **Princeton**, developing the equilibrium concept bearing his name. A **Nash equilibrium** is a combination of strategies in which no participant benefits by changing their own choice while other choices remain unchanged.

**John Harsanyi** (1920–2000) helped develop games of incomplete information, and **Reinhard Selten** (1930–2016) refined equilibrium in sequential games. All three shared the **1994 economics prize**.

Game theory transformed analysis of oligopolies, negotiation, auctions and international conflict. An equilibrium need not be socially desirable: mutual non-cooperation can persist even where everyone would gain from coordinated change.

Nash's serious mental-health struggles belong to his biography but should not be romanticized as the explanation of his mathematics.

### 85. James Buchanan and Gordon Tullock: political incentives

**James M. Buchanan** (1919–2013) studied economics, including at the **University of Chicago**, and **Gordon Tullock** (1922–2014) trained in law before building an economics career.

Their ***The Calculus of Consent* (1962)** examined collective decision rules and incentives of politicians, voters and public officials. **Public choice** challenged the assumption that government always implements a perfectly defined public interest.

The concept of **rent-seeking**, developed in a related tradition including Tullock and **Anne Krueger**, analysed resources devoted to obtaining privileges rather than producing new goods. Yet private monopolies also create rent-seeking; examining political incentives does not prove privatization is always superior.

Buchanan received the **1986 economics prize**. The approach remains influential and politically contested, particularly in debates over taxation and redistribution.

### 86. Gary Becker: human capital, families and economic rationality

**Gary Becker** (1930–2014) was born in Pennsylvania, studied at **Princeton** and obtained a doctorate from the **University of Chicago**.

He extended economic analysis to education, discrimination, crime, families and other social activities. **Human capital theory** connects education and training with future productivity and earnings, while recognizing that returns depend on labour markets and individual circumstances.

His work on discrimination examined how preferences and market competition interact, but it does not establish that competition automatically removes racism or gender inequality.

Becker received the **1992 prize**. His approach widened economics while raising criticism that complex social relations were sometimes reduced to excessively narrow models of individual maximization.


### 87. Eugene Fama, Robert Shiller and Lars Hansen: asset prices and competing explanations

**Eugene F. Fama** (born 1939), trained at **Tufts** and the **University of Chicago**, developed the **efficient-market hypothesis**. Its informational interpretation suggests that widely available news is difficult to exploit systematically for abnormal profit under competitive conditions. It does not imply that every observed price is fair or that bubbles are logically impossible.

**Robert J. Shiller** (born 1946), trained at the **University of Michigan** and **MIT**, researched stock-market volatility, housing and investor expectations. His findings questioned whether some movements in prices could be explained by plausible changes in expected dividends alone. The concept of **irrational exuberance** popularized concern about speculative narratives.

**Lars Peter Hansen** (born 1952), trained at **Utah State** and **Minnesota**, developed the **generalized method of moments (GMM)**, an econometric framework widely applied to finance and macroeconomics.

The three shared the **2013 economics prize** for empirical research on asset prices. Their approaches reflect different questions: how information enters prices, when market volatility exceeds model predictions and how to estimate testable relationships. Rejecting one pricing model does not prove that every price change is irrational.

### 88. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky: decision-making under risk

**Daniel Kahneman** (1934–2024) grew up partly in Paris, survived Nazi persecution and studied psychology at the **Hebrew University of Jerusalem** and **UC Berkeley**. **Amos Tversky** (1937–1996), born in Israel, trained in psychology and mathematics and earned a doctorate at the **University of Michigan**.

Their collaboration identified **heuristics and biases** in human judgements about probability, gains and losses. ***Prospect Theory* (1979)** examined why outcomes are evaluated relative to reference points and why people may respond more strongly to losses than to otherwise comparable gains.

The results challenged simple expected-utility models but did not demonstrate that every choice is irrational or that people never learn. Experimental design, incentives, context and replication matter.

Kahneman shared the **2002 economics prize** with Vernon Smith. Tversky had died in 1996 and was not eligible for a posthumous award under the prize rules. That institutional outcome should not obscure their joint intellectual contribution.

### 89. Vernon Smith and Richard Thaler: experiments and choice architecture

**Vernon L. Smith** (born 1927) trained in electrical engineering and economics and earned a doctorate at **Harvard**. He developed **experimental economics**, creating controlled markets to examine price formation, auctions and trading behaviour.

**Richard H. Thaler** (born 1945), trained at **Case Western Reserve** and the **University of Rochester**, studied mental accounting, saving, self-control and other departures from simplified models. With collaborators including **Cass Sunstein**, he popularized **nudges**, changes in the architecture of choices that influence behaviour without formally eliminating alternatives.

Vernon Smith received the **2002 prize**, Thaler the **2017 prize**. Behavioural insights can help with retirement savings and consumer protection, but the design of default choices raises questions about transparency and who defines beneficial outcomes.

### 90. Angus Deaton and Anne Case: consumption, health and social mortality

**Angus Deaton** (born 1945) studied at **Cambridge** and became a leading economist at **Princeton**, with research on demand, household surveys, poverty and welfare measurement.

**Anne Case** (born 1958), trained at **SUNY Albany** and **Princeton**, studied health, labour and inequality. Together they investigated adverse mortality patterns among some American population groups, connecting labour-market change, substance-related deaths and social conditions.

The phrase **deaths of despair** refers to an analytical set of trends, not a single clinical diagnosis explaining every death. Access to health care, the supply of drugs, education and economic dislocation must be examined separately.

Deaton received the **2015 prize**. The partnership with Case demonstrates that economic welfare cannot be fully captured by income and consumption data without considering health and mortality.

### 91. Esther Duflo, Abhijit Banerjee and Michael Kremer: experiments in development policy

**Esther Duflo** (born 1972) studied at the **École normale supérieure** and earned an economics doctorate at **MIT**. **Abhijit Banerjee** (born 1961) studied in Calcutta, at **Jawaharlal Nehru University** and **Harvard**. Together with colleagues, they helped build the **Abdul Latif Jameel Poverty Action Lab (J-PAL)**. They are also married, but each has a distinct research contribution.

**Michael Kremer** (born 1964), trained at **Harvard**, researched education, public health and development, including field experiments in Kenya.

Their work expanded the use of **randomized evaluations** to determine how particular interventions affect people under specific conditions. Rather than assuming a school, vaccination or transfer program must work, researchers measured outcomes for comparable groups.

The three received the **2019 prize**. Experimental findings still face questions about whether results transfer to other countries, institutions or scales. Ethical safeguards and the rights of participating communities are crucial.

### 92. David Card, Joshua Angrist and Guido Imbens: causal inference without randomizing societies

**David Card** (born 1956) studied at **Queen's University** and **Princeton** and became known for empirical labour economics, including minimum wages and migration.

**Joshua Angrist** (born 1960), trained at **Oberlin** and **Princeton**, and **Guido Imbens** (born 1963), trained at **Erasmus**, **Hull** and **Brown**, developed methods for identifying causal effects in observational data.

People do not receive schooling, wages or public policies randomly in most real settings. **Natural experiments**, instruments and difference-in-differences attempt to find changes that permit better comparisons. The methods require assumptions and attention to the populations identified.

They received the [**2021 prize**](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2021/press-release/). Studies challenging simple predictions about minimum wages do not establish that every minimum-wage increase in any economy is harmless. Results depend on magnitude, labour markets and circumstances.

### 93. Claudia Goldin: recovering women's work from incomplete archives

**Claudia Goldin** (born 1946) grew up in the Bronx, studied at **Cornell** and earned an economics doctorate at the **University of Chicago**. In her [Nobel biography](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2023/goldin/biographical/), she describes her initial interest in microbiology and her turn to economic history.

Goldin reconstructed women's labour-market participation using census returns, company records and archival surveys that often overlooked married women's work. Her research demonstrated that women's participation did not follow a simple straight line upward with economic development; technologies, family norms, education and fertility choices changed the historical pattern.

With **Lawrence Katz**, her research and life partner, she studied schooling, inequality and technological change. ***The Race between Education and Technology* (2008)** examined how changes in skills and the supply of educated workers interact with wage differences.

Goldin also investigated professional careers where very long or unpredictable hours are disproportionately rewarded. Such **greedy jobs** can create earnings penalties when family responsibilities are distributed unequally. She received the **2023 economics prize** for advancing understanding of women's labour-market outcomes.

## X — Innovation and modern growth economics

### 94. Paul Romer: knowledge and endogenous growth

**Paul Romer** (born 1955) studied at **Chicago** and **Berkeley** and earned a doctorate in economics at Chicago. He developed **endogenous growth theory**, placing the production and diffusion of ideas inside the explanation of long-term growth.

Knowledge differs from many physical resources: an idea can often be used by more than one producer without being exhausted. Creating it can be costly while reproducing it is relatively cheap. These properties influence intellectual property, research subsidies, competition and innovation.

Romer's work from the **1980s and 1990s** supplied formal mechanisms linking knowledge and sustained productivity growth. He shared the **2018 economics prize** with William Nordhaus.

The argument does not validate every technology subsidy or monopoly patent. Policies must balance incentives to innovate with the social value of diffusion.

### 95. William Nordhaus: climate and the economics of long horizons

**William D. Nordhaus** (1941–2026; [Yale University obituary, 30 October 2026](https://news.yale.edu/2026/10/30/william-nordhaus-nobel-laureate-who-integrated-economics-and-climate-change-devoted-yale)) studied at **Yale** and earned his economics doctorate there, developing influential research on growth, energy and climate change.

His **DICE integrated assessment model** connected economic output, greenhouse gas emissions, temperature and damage estimates under explicit assumptions. The **social cost of carbon** seeks to evaluate the harm of an additional unit of emissions for public policy.

Estimating damage over centuries requires controversial assumptions about uncertainty, discount rates and the rights of future generations. Some critics argue that integrated models have understated extreme or irreversible risks.

Nordhaus shared the **2018 prize** with Romer. His achievement is to bring climate and economic choices into a quantitative framework without removing the underlying ethical and political decisions.

### 96. Joel Mokyr, Philippe Aghion and Peter Howitt: the 2025 economics prize

**Joel Mokyr** (born 1946 in Leiden), trained as an economic historian at **Yale**, investigated why scientific knowledge and institutions allowed sustained technological improvement after long periods of slow growth.

**Philippe Aghion** (born 1956 in Paris), an economics doctorate from **Harvard**, and **Peter Howitt** (born 1946 in Canada), an economics doctorate from **Northwestern**, developed formal theories of growth through **creative destruction**. New technologies and firms displace old ones while productivity expands.

On **13 October 2025**, they received the [economics prize for innovation-driven growth](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2025/press-release/). The award emphasized the role of openness to ideas, competition and institutional adaptation.

The models do not imply that all automation instantly improves wages or welfare. Learning, retraining, monopolization and the position of displaced workers affect who shares the gains.

## XI — Brazilian economic thought: development, inflation and the state

### 97. José da Silva Lisboa, Viscount of Cairu: trade and the opening of the ports

**José da Silva Lisboa** (1756–1835), later **Viscount of Cairu**, was born in Bahia and studied at the **University of Coimbra**, developing expertise in law, philosophy and commerce.

Influenced by Adam Smith, he wrote about trade freedom and economic institutions. His support for the opening of Brazilian ports in **1808** placed him in a decisive political-economic transformation, reducing some Portuguese colonial restrictions while increasing links with British commerce.

The opening did not dismantle slavery, unequal property or political hierarchy. The expansion of trade occurred within a society where only selected groups could exercise economic freedom. Cairu's importance lies in introducing classical political economy into the early debates of an emerging Brazilian state.

### 98. Joaquim Nabuco and André Rebouças: abolition and land ownership

**Joaquim Nabuco** (1849–1910), born in Recife, studied law in São Paulo and Recife and became an abolitionist, politician and diplomat.

**André Rebouças** (1838–1898), born in Bahia, was a Black Brazilian engineer educated through military engineering and involved in infrastructure projects and abolitionism.

Neither was an academic economist in the modern sense. Both developed important economic arguments about the effects of slavery, concentrated landownership and the failure to provide new opportunities after emancipation. Ending legal slavery without sufficient land, education, credit or protection could preserve structural dependence.

Their ideas illustrate that the economic history of Brazil cannot be reduced to commodity exports or machinery while ignoring coercion, property and the institutional legacy of slavery.

### 99. Roberto Simonsen: manufacturing as national development

**Roberto Cochrane Simonsen** (1889–1948) studied engineering at the **Polytechnic School of São Paulo** and built a career in industry, business organizations and economic writing.

In ***Economic History of Brazil* (1937)**, he examined productive structures and the obstacles to industrialization. He supported coordinated investment in manufacturing, infrastructure and domestic capabilities.

His debate with **Eugênio Gudin** during the 1940s concerned planning, economic scarcity, industry and the state's capacity to direct investment. Simonsen's significance lies in placing productive transformation at the centre of Brazilian development.

Industrial policy, however, requires performance evaluation. Protecting firms without improving capability or competition can preserve inefficiency rather than development.

### 100. Eugênio Gudin: stabilization and liberal economic reform

**Eugênio Gudin** (1886–1986) was born in Rio de Janeiro, trained as a **civil engineer** and later developed an influential career in economics, journalism, teaching and public service.

He advocated monetary stability, fiscal discipline, competition and caution about poorly evaluated public investment. In his debate with Simonsen, he challenged the assumption that government planning can always identify productive sectors better than markets.

Brazil's concentrated ownership, weak infrastructure and restricted credit posed difficulties for a simplified assumption of effective competition. Yet Gudin's concerns about inflation and political allocation of resources also addressed real institutional weaknesses.

He served as finance minister in **1954–1955**. His legacy is part of a continuing Brazilian argument over market coordination, stabilization and productive development.

### 101. Caio Prado Júnior: colonial production and historical dependency

**Caio Prado Júnior** (1907–1990) was born in São Paulo into an elite family and studied law at the **University of São Paulo's traditional law faculty**. He became a Marxist historian, publisher and political intellectual.

In ***The Formation of Contemporary Brazil* (1942)**, he argued that Portuguese colonization organized large parts of the economy to serve external commercial interests, reinforcing slave labour and concentrated production.

His emphasis on the **meaning of colonization** influenced later analyses of dependency and development. It was criticized for insufficient attention to the diversity of internal colonial markets and regional variations.

The contribution was to reconstruct Brazilian economic institutions historically instead of treating colonial society as an early version of a modern national market.

### 102. Celso Furtado: structuralism, the Northeast and economic development

**Celso Furtado** (1920–2004) was born in **Pombal, Paraíba**, studied law in Rio de Janeiro and earned a doctorate in **Paris in 1948** with a study of the Brazilian colonial economy. His formation connected history, public administration and economics.

At **ECLAC**, he worked with Raúl Prebisch and developed a historical-structural approach to development. In ***The Economic Formation of Brazil* (1959)**, he analysed export cycles, slavery, coffee, manufacturing and the building of a domestic market.

In ***Development and Underdevelopment* (1961)**, Furtado argued that underdevelopment is not simply a stage every country passes through before converging. Modern technology may coexist with low productivity, concentrated income and limited domestic markets across different sectors.

Furtado helped establish **SUDENE in 1959** as an institution for regional development in Brazil's Northeast. His public career was interrupted by the **1964 military coup**, and he continued research and teaching in exile.

His achievement was to make history, structural heterogeneity and distribution essential to understanding growth. [Research by Guido Mantega](https://www.scielo.br/j/rep/a/VFVpGYrqYjx8r5SzPMCYRHm/?format=html&ilang=pt_BR) examines his intellectual development and policy influence.

### 103. Maria da Conceição Tavares: productive structure and chronic inflation

**Maria da Conceição Tavares** (1930–2024) was born in Portugal, studied **mathematics at the University of Lisbon** and migrated to Brazil, where she developed economic training and worked with **BNDES**, **ECLAC**, **Federal University of Rio de Janeiro** and **Unicamp**.

She examined import substitution, finance, investment, distribution and Brazil's industrial structure. Her work treated inflation as a phenomenon influenced by production constraints, indexation and social conflict, not as the mechanical outcome of one monetary aggregate alone.

Drawing on Keynes, Kalecki, Marx, Schumpeter and Latin American structuralism, Tavares developed historical explanations of economic cycles and capital accumulation. She became an influential teacher and political participant.

The [Brazilian Journal of Political Economy](https://doi.org/10.1590/s1415-98482010000100009) documents her methodological range. Her legacy is a substantial research program, not merely a public image as a forceful speaker.

### 104. Ignácio Rangel: dual structures and infrastructure bottlenecks

**Ignácio de Mourão Rangel** (1914–1994) was born in Maranhão and combined political experience, self-directed study and work in public institutions with research on development.

His theory of the **basic duality of the Brazilian economy** analysed the coexistence of productive and institutional structures associated with different historical phases. Modern industry and older rural relationships could operate within the same country and influence one another.

Rangel studied investment cycles, infrastructure, agriculture and inflation. His proposals on financing public services influenced subsequent debates, though hypotheses on long cycles remain empirically disputed.

His significance lies in rejecting the assumption that every sector of a national economy changes at the same pace.

### 105. Roberto Campos: institutions, planning and liberalism

**Roberto de Oliveira Campos** (1917–2001) was born in Cuiabá, received early religious education and pursued economic and international studies, including in the United States. He became a diplomat, economic policymaker and major public intellectual.

As planning minister during Brazil's military dictatorship, Campos participated in reforms associated with the **PAEG, 1964–1967**, including monetary correction and changes to fiscal and financial institutions.

The reforms sought stabilization and increased investment capacity while operating under a regime of political repression. The economic mechanisms and the political context cannot be separated in an assessment of their social effects.

Campos later strongly criticized interventionism and advocated liberalization. His career nevertheless included substantial involvement in public planning, contradicting simplistic claims that his position was always opposition to every state economic role.

### 106. Antônio Delfim Netto: rapid growth and the costs of authoritarian economic policy

**Antônio Delfim Netto** (1928–2024) studied economics at the **University of São Paulo** and developed research on agriculture and coffee before entering senior government roles.

He served as finance minister during **1967–1974**, covering much of Brazil's period of rapid growth known as the **economic miracle**. Industrial expansion and investment were substantial, but the outcomes occurred under authoritarian rule, political repression and unequal distribution.

Later public roles confronted oil shocks, external debt and inflation. Delfim's policies and economic positions changed with circumstances, making it misleading to treat his long career as one unchanging doctrine.

His legacy combines technical economic management, major historical growth and responsibility for choices made under a dictatorship. GDP expansion alone does not measure the welfare or rights of all affected Brazilians.

### 107. Mário Henrique Simonsen: mathematical economics and indexed inflation

**Mário Henrique Simonsen** (1935–1997) studied engineering and economics, developed strong mathematical skills and taught at the **Getulio Vargas Foundation**.

His research examined inflation, growth, stabilization and indexation. Monetary correction can protect nominal contracts from price increases, but widespread automatic indexation may transmit past inflation into future prices and complicate disinflation.

Simonsen served as finance and planning minister under military governments, facing constraints involving growth, external debt and fiscal pressures. His academic contributions are distinct from the political circumstances of his appointments.

His continuing importance lies in formal and practical analysis of stabilization in an economy where inflation becomes embedded in contracts and institutions.

### 108. Luiz Carlos Bresser-Pereira: development and exchange rates

**Luiz Carlos Bresser-Pereira** (born 1934) studied law at the **University of São Paulo** and built an academic career in administration and economics, especially at the **Getulio Vargas Foundation**.

His **new developmentalism** examines exchange-rate competitiveness, industrial capabilities, public policy and how financial conditions can affect productive investment. An overvalued currency may impair internationally competing firms even when their technology is adequate.

Exchange-rate policy also has costs: depreciation can raise import prices and reduce real wages. The effectiveness of such policies depends on productivity, debt denomination, industrial structure and institutions.

As finance minister in **1987**, Bresser-Pereira was associated with the **Bresser Plan**, which did not achieve lasting disinflation. The gap between theory and policy implementation is a central part of his history.

### 109. Pérsio Arida and André Lara Resende: inflation and monetary coordination

**Pérsio Arida** (born 1952) and **André Lara Resende** (born 1951) studied economics at **PUC-Rio** and pursued advanced research in international academic settings.

During Brazil's inflation crisis in the 1980s, they developed arguments for monetary reform that would address **inertial inflation**, where prices and contracts were repeatedly adjusted according to past inflation.

The proposal associated with the name **Larida** influenced discussions of a stable unit of account and monetary transition. The eventual **Real Plan of 1994** involved many researchers and officials, not only the two economists. The **URV** was introduced in March 1994, preceding the new real on **1 July 1994**.

The achievement was not simply removing zeroes from bills. It required coordination of prices and expectations, cash logistics, monetary rules, fiscal decisions and banking arrangements. The two economists later expressed differing views on monetary and fiscal questions, showing that collaboration on one historical reform does not imply permanent intellectual agreement.

### 110. Edmar Bacha, Gustavo Franco and Pedro Malan: the institutional architecture of stabilization

**Edmar Bacha** (born 1942) earned an economics doctorate at **Yale** and worked on development, distribution and inflation. **Gustavo Franco** (born 1956) studied at **PUC-Rio** and earned a doctorate at **Harvard**, becoming a central-bank official and prominent contributor to stabilization policy. **Pedro Malan** (born 1943) studied economics in Brazil and at **Berkeley**, worked in international institutions and held key policy posts.

The transition to the real required decisions on contracts, accounting units, reserves, fiscal obligations, the exchange rate and banking supervision. No single expert could implement these components independently.

The reduction of inflation was historically significant; later high real interest rates, public debt and exchange-rate effects became subjects of debate. Evaluating the program requires distinguishing the immediate collapse of chronic inflation from the long-term consequences of different economic policies.

### 111. Fernando Henrique Cardoso and Enzo Faletto: dependency and political alliances

**Fernando Henrique Cardoso** (born 1931) trained in social sciences at the **University of São Paulo**, becoming a sociologist and researcher. **Enzo Faletto** (1935–2003), a Chilean sociologist, developed a career in Latin American academic and research institutions.

Their ***Dependency and Development in Latin America* (1969)** investigated how international capital, local elites, class relations and state policy shape different paths of growth. Dependency did not necessarily mean total stagnation; forms of **associated development** could occur through changing political coalitions.

Cardoso later became president of Brazil, but his government's policies cannot be mechanically derived from every claim made in the earlier book with Faletto. The work remains important for connecting economic development to political power and historical institutions.

### 112. Theotônio dos Santos, Ruy Mauro Marini and André Gunder Frank: radical dependency

**Theotônio dos Santos** (1936–2018), **Ruy Mauro Marini** (1932–1997) and **André Gunder Frank** (1929–2005) developed major interpretations of global capitalism and peripheral underdevelopment. Their careers intersected with Brazilian and international universities, political exile and authoritarian conflict.

Marini's theory of **superexploitation of labour** examined wages, working time and international insertion. Dos Santos analysed financial and technological dependencies, while Frank studied relations among economic centres and dependent regions.

Their work challenged the view that poor regions were simply untouched by capitalism. Critics argued that some formulations underestimated the potential for industrial transformation and domestic policy autonomy, especially in light of later East Asian growth.

Dependency theory contained rival schools, not a single doctrine. Differences among Furtado, Cardoso, Marini and Frank are essential for interpreting Latin American economic history.

## XII — Twenty-first-century inequality and economic governance

### 113. Thomas Piketty: reconstructing the history of capital and inheritance

**Thomas Piketty** (born 1971) studied at the **École normale supérieure** and earned a doctorate in economics in France, developing a career in historical inequality and the **Paris School of Economics**.

Working with **Emmanuel Saez**, **Gabriel Zucman** and other researchers, he helped reconstruct historical distributions of income and wealth from tax and related records.

In ***Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (2013)**, he examined the tendency for wealth concentration when returns on capital exceed growth for long periods under certain institutional conditions. The relationship **r > g** is not a universal law determining every distributional outcome; taxation, wars, demographics, asset ownership and institutions alter the mechanism.

His proposals for progressive wealth taxation raise practical questions about valuation, enforcement and international mobility. The lasting achievement is restoring long-run historical evidence to debates about inequality.

### 114. Gabriel Zucman and Emmanuel Saez: tax policy and hidden wealth

**Emmanuel Saez** (born 1972) studied at the **École normale supérieure** and earned a doctorate at **MIT**. **Gabriel Zucman** (born 1986) studied at the **École normale supérieure** and earned a doctorate through the **Paris School of Economics**.

Their research addresses high incomes, wealth, tax incidence and financial assets held offshore. They use tax records, national accounts and other sources to examine how legal differences and cross-border arrangements affect revenue and distribution.

Tax reform cannot be assessed only by statutory rates. Asset valuation, income shifting, avoidance, evasion, employment responses and enforcement capacity matter.

The policy implications are disputed, but their research made the treatment of highly mobile wealth an empirically grounded issue rather than an argument based only on anecdotes.

### 115. Mariana Mazzucato: the state and technological innovation

**Mariana Mazzucato** (born 1968 in Rome) grew up in the United States, studied history and economics at **Tufts** and earned a doctorate at the **New School for Social Research**. She later developed a research career in the United Kingdom.

In ***The Entrepreneurial State* (2013)**, she examined how public investment in basic research and technologies helped create opportunities that private companies commercialized. Her **mission-oriented policy** approach emphasizes measurable public purposes and coordination among research, industry and government procurement.

Public investment can support innovation where private investors cannot capture all benefits. It can also be wasted through poor incentives, political capture or weak evaluation. Her argument is not evidence that every subsidy or public company generates superior innovation.

### 116. Branko Milanović: inequality within and between countries

**Branko Milanović** (born 1953 in the former Yugoslavia) studied economics and earned a doctorate at the **University of Belgrade**, later working in international research and organizations.

He distinguishes inequality among people **within countries** from inequality caused by differences in average national incomes. Rapid growth in large Asian economies has reduced some cross-country gaps while other parts of the world experienced stagnant incomes for certain groups.

With **Christoph Lakner**, he analysed historical changes in global income distributions, represented by the well-known **elephant curve** for a specific period. Its shape is sensitive to the window, population composition and methods; it cannot be extrapolated unchanged into every decade.

His importance is treating global inequality as a separate empirical problem from any single country's Gini coefficient.

### 117. Deirdre McCloskey: statistics, rhetoric and dignity

**Deirdre Nansen McCloskey** (born 1942) studied economics and history at **Harvard** and developed careers in economic history, methodology and criticism of economic language.

She challenged the assumption that statistical significance automatically establishes economic importance, emphasizing effect sizes, historical interpretation and intellectual honesty.

Her work on **bourgeois dignity** argues that cultural acceptance of merchants and innovators contributed to the extraordinary expansion of modern prosperity. The historical proposition is contested because scientific knowledge, energy, colonial expansion, institutions and trade changed at the same time.

The enduring contribution is a reminder that language, values and institutional acceptance influence economic life and the way economists communicate empirical results.

### 118. Susan Strange: power inside international markets

**Susan Strange** (1923–1998) was a British journalist, scholar and pioneer of **international political economy**, associated with institutions including the **London School of Economics**.

In ***States and Markets* (1988)**, she examined **structural power** across finance, security, production and knowledge. Some actors influence not merely a particular bargain but the rules and opportunities within which bargains occur.

Foreign currencies, financial sanctions, corporate networks and cross-border credit cannot be examined independently of political authority and security. Her work remains relevant to reserve currencies and geopolitical competition.

### 119. Alice Amsden: late industrialization and disciplined intervention

**Alice H. Amsden** (1943–2012) studied at **Cornell** and earned a doctorate at the **London School of Economics**. Her research examined firms, technology acquisition and industrial policies in developing economies.

In ***Asia's Next Giant: South Korea and Late Industrialization* (1989)**, she analysed government credit, support for industry and the demand that favoured businesses demonstrate performance. Subsidies could help firms learn when combined with demanding export and productivity targets.

The Korean experience cannot be reproduced simply by paying subsidies to selected manufacturers. Education, administration, geopolitics, access to export markets and the ability to withdraw support matter.

Amsden's contribution is the empirical analysis of how states and firms acquire productive capabilities under conditions of technological disadvantage.


## XIII — Debates that did not disappear

| Origins of value and prices | Smith, Ricardo, Marx, Menger, Walras, Sraffa | Production, scarcity, distribution and institutions explain different dimensions |

### 120. Does value come from labour, utility or institutions?

Smith linked labour, productivity and prices through different arguments. Ricardo examined relative production costs and income distribution; Marx analysed value, labour and exploitation under capitalist property relations. Jevons, Menger and Walras reformulated individual choice around scarcity and marginal valuations. Sraffa demonstrated a model of production prices that could be constructed from techniques and distribution rather than individual utility.

These theories do not always answer the same question. A trader wants to forecast next month's market price; a historian may be interested in distribution between labour and property owners; a regulator needs evidence of market power. Marginal reasoning clarifies decisions under constraints, but the historical origins of those constraints require additional analysis.

No single school eliminated the practical significance of all its predecessors. The relevant task is to distinguish definitions, hypotheses, empirical mechanisms and claims of universal validity.

### 121. What produces growth: saving, industrial capacity, institutions or innovation?

Classical economists analysed capital and land. List emphasized national productive capabilities; Solow isolated the importance of long-term productivity growth; Schumpeter, Romer and Aghion–Howitt connected technological innovation to structural change. Prebisch, Furtado and Amsden stressed production structure and international constraints; North, Ostrom and Acemoglu emphasized institutional arrangements.

These mechanisms often complement one another. Machinery needs finance and skilled workers; education needs schools and institutions; innovation needs access to ideas and markets. Intellectual property can encourage investment while also restricting diffusion.

Industrial policy can facilitate learning under disciplined management or preserve unproductive companies when protection becomes permanent. Development cannot be reduced to the simple presence or absence of the state.

### 122. Are crises external shocks or endogenous financial failures?

Malthus and Sismondi investigated insufficient purchasing power. Keynes emphasized uncertainty and investment. Fisher described **debt-deflation**, Minsky financial fragility and Friedman–Schwartz monetary contraction. Lucas and later researchers focused on expectations and the consequences of changing policy rules.

The mechanisms can operate together. An energy shock can raise prices, deteriorate balance sheets, weaken demand and complicate monetary policy. A banking system that extends too much short-term credit can turn a modest real downturn into a financial panic.

Explaining a crisis requires reconstructing the sequence of transactions and policy decisions rather than identifying one inflation, interest-rate or output statistic as its complete cause.

### 123. Does the state correct markets or create additional costs?

Smith criticized merchant monopolies created by public privileges. List and Prebisch advocated productive transformation. Hayek examined information dispersed beyond administrative reach. Buchanan and Tullock studied public officials' incentives. Ostrom documented communities capable of designing collective rules.

Government can address public goods, information problems and missing infrastructure. It can also create privileges, corruption and inefficiency. Private ownership does not guarantee effective competition, while public ownership does not automatically ensure public purpose.

The effect of an intervention depends on its specific design, capacity for monitoring results, institutional accountability and the availability of alternatives.

### 124. What is lost when economics becomes mathematical?

Walras and Edgeworth increased analytical precision; Slutsky and Haavelmo improved models of decision and statistical inference; Arrow and Debreu demonstrated formal results under explicitly specified conditions. Mathematical structure makes hidden assumptions and logical inconsistencies easier to identify.

Problems arise when model tractability becomes more important than the economic phenomenon. A representative household may be useful for a particular exercise but cannot establish that income distribution, informal labour or financial institutions never matter. Large datasets do not solve identification or measurement problems by themselves.

Experiments and modern causal analysis provide new tools, not exemption from theory or from understanding the social institutions that generate observations.

### 125. Why the history of economics cannot be reduced to its prize

The **Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel** was established in **1968** and first awarded in **1969**. It is valuable evidence of selected contributions but cannot serve as a complete ranking of economic importance. Smith, Marx and Keynes died before it existed; Joan Robinson, Anna Schwartz, Prebisch and Celso Furtado never received it.

Scientific work is also collective. Data collectors, research assistants, statisticians, programmers, archivists, survey respondents and collaborators often make contributions that an award addressed to individual researchers cannot fully reflect.

### 126. Economics and artificial intelligence: labour, ownership and technological change

Solow's model distinguishes factor accumulation from productivity; Romer emphasizes spillovers of knowledge; Schumpeter and Aghion–Howitt examine displacement of established activities; Goldin studies how labour-market rules and family responsibilities condition the distribution of earnings; Ostrom considers the rules governing shared resources.

Artificial intelligence can make certain tasks cheaper while also concentrating revenue among owners of infrastructure, data and computing capacity. The result for employment depends on substitution and complementarity, training, competition and bargaining power. A capability demonstration does not by itself establish the net effect on national wages or productivity.

Economic policy consequently faces problems of education, research, data rights, market concentration, taxation and welfare. Historical innovations often required long institutional adaptation before gains appeared in broad productivity statistics. Neither universal unemployment nor automatic prosperity follows logically from the presence of a new technology.

## XIV — Other decisive traditions: financial crises, trade, Africa, Asia and care

### 127. John Kenneth Galbraith: corporations, advertising and affluent societies

**John Kenneth Galbraith** (1908–2006) was born in Canada, studied agriculture and economics at **UC Berkeley** and developed an academic career at Harvard alongside government and diplomatic service.

In ***The Affluent Society* (1958)**, he argued that societies could devote substantial resources to private consumption and advertising while neglecting public infrastructure and services. In ***The New Industrial State* (1967)**, he examined the power of large corporations and technical management over production.

His criticism of the image of markets as systems of tiny competitive firms was influential, while his stronger claims about corporate control of consumer preferences were contested. His legacy combines institutional economics, public communication and attention to the social allocation of investment.

### 128. Abba Lerner: functional finance and the purposes of government budgets

**Abba P. Lerner** (1903–1982) was born in what is now Moldova, grew up in London and studied at the **London School of Economics**.

His theory of **functional finance**, developed in the 1940s, proposed assessing taxes, public expenditure and government debt according to their effects on output, employment and inflation rather than treating annual budget balance as an independent moral objective.

The approach later influenced debates over monetary sovereignty and modern monetary theory. It does not remove inflation, foreign-exchange or real-resource constraints. Its central contribution is evaluating fiscal instruments by observable consequences.

### 129. Charles Kindleberger: international crises and financial leadership

**Charles P. Kindleberger** (1910–2003) studied at the **University of Pennsylvania** and **Columbia**, worked in US government and postwar European reconstruction and later taught at **MIT**.

In ***The World in Depression, 1929–1939* (1973)**, he argued that failure to supply international public goods, including liquidity and policy coordination, worsened the Depression. His work on manias and crashes examined speculative credit expansions and sudden reversals of expectations.

The hypothesis that a dominant power can stabilize the international system remains debated and does not imply that hegemony automatically produces good policy. Kindleberger connected banking stress, geopolitics and macroeconomic institutions.

### 130. Ben Bernanke, Douglas Diamond and Philip Dybvig: banking crises

**Ben S. Bernanke** (born 1953) studied at **Harvard** and earned a doctorate at **MIT**. He researched the Great Depression and later chaired the **Federal Reserve from 2006 to 2014**.

**Douglas W. Diamond** (born 1953) and **Philip H. Dybvig** (born 1955), both trained at **Yale**, developed a model showing how banks combine long-term assets with deposits that can be withdrawn quickly. This provides useful liquidity but creates the possibility of self-reinforcing runs when depositors fear other depositors will withdraw first.

All three received the [2022 economics prize](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2022/press-release/). Bernanke's policy decisions during the 2008 crisis are a separate subject from the value of his prior research. Their joint legacy is the recognition that liquidity guarantees, supervision and confidence are central to banking stability.

### 131. Paul Krugman: trade, increasing returns and economic geography

**Paul R. Krugman** (born 1953) studied at **Yale** and earned a doctorate at **MIT**, developing research in international trade and economic geography.

**New trade theory** explains commerce between countries that produce similar manufactured goods by incorporating product differentiation, economies of scale and imperfect competition. Industrial clusters can reinforce themselves as skilled workers, customers and suppliers concentrate in the same area.

Krugman received the [**2008 economics prize**](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2008/press-release/). His later public commentary and political positions must be assessed separately from theoretical results. The models explain important trade mechanisms without guaranteeing that every trade-policy change benefits every worker and region.

### 132. Jean Tirole: market power and regulation

**Jean Tirole** (born 1953) studied engineering in France and earned an economics doctorate at **MIT**, later developing a career at the **Toulouse School of Economics**.

His research examined the contracts and incentives shaping firms with market power. A telecommunications or energy network may require high investment and have limited competition; regulators must consider prices, service quality, information asymmetries and future investment.

Tirole received the [**2014 economics prize**](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2014/press-release/). The principal contribution was designing analysis appropriate to particular market structures, rather than assuming one price-control rule works for every industry.

### 133. Oliver Hart and Bengt Holmström: incomplete contracts and incentives

**Oliver Hart** (born 1948) studied mathematics at **Cambridge** and economics at **Princeton**. **Bengt Holmström** (born 1949), from Finland, trained in mathematics and operations research and received a doctorate at **Stanford**.

Hart analysed ownership and control when contracts cannot describe every future contingency. Holmström studied incentives when employees or managers affect outcomes that also depend on luck and external circumstances.

They shared the [**2016 economics prize**](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2016/press-release/). Their theories help evaluate executive pay, outsourcing and public-service contracts while demonstrating why high-powered financial incentives can also encourage excessive risk.

### 134. Paul Milgrom and Robert Wilson: designing auctions

**Paul Milgrom** (born 1948) studied mathematics and business and earned a doctorate at **Stanford**. **Robert Wilson** (born 1937) studied and developed a career at Stanford.

They advanced auction theory where bidders hold private information or seek to estimate a common but uncertain value, such as the productive value of a licence. Auction rules shape bidding, information disclosure, allocation and the winner's-curse risk.

They received the [**2020 economics prize**](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2020/press-release/). Their applied work influenced spectrum auctions. Maximizing immediate auction revenue is not always the same as maximizing social benefit if high acquisition costs reduce later investment.

### 135. B. R. Ambedkar: money, caste and emancipation in India

**Bhimrao Ramji Ambedkar** (1891–1956) was born in British India into a family facing severe caste discrimination. He studied at **Columbia University**, the **London School of Economics** and in law.

In ***The Problem of the Rupee* (1923)**, he examined Indian money and colonial currency rules. His research also covered agriculture, public finance and economic inequality. He later played a central role in drafting India's constitution and combating caste oppression.

Economic opportunity depends on legal rights, access to schooling, occupation and property. Ambedkar's lasting contribution is to join monetary analysis and political emancipation rather than treating discrimination as a non-economic issue.

### 136. Dadabhai Naoroji and Romesh Chunder Dutt: colonial economic drain

**Dadabhai Naoroji** (1825–1917) was an Indian educator and political leader active in India and Britain. **Romesh Chunder Dutt** (1848–1909) worked in the British Indian administration and wrote economic histories of colonial rule.

Their **drain theory** examined transfers of Indian resources to Britain through government payments, debt obligations, profits and other colonial channels. Historical numerical estimates were constrained by the quality of available data, but the underlying question was important: producing export revenue does not guarantee retaining its benefits domestically.

The work connected colonial taxation, agriculture and national economic sovereignty and contributed to emerging political movements in India.

### 137. Samir Amin: accumulation and unequal world systems

**Samir Amin** (1931–2018) was born in Egypt, studied in France and developed research and political engagement across African countries.

He examined global accumulation, trade and the reproduction of unequal relations between central and peripheral economies. His arguments for greater autonomy from external dependence influenced debates over development and decolonization.

Critics questioned whether proposed forms of disengagement would restrict beneficial trade or worsen domestic governance. His significance lies in analysing world capitalism from African and global South perspectives rather than treating those regions as passive recipients of European models.

### 138. Thandika Mkandawire: African state capacity and social policy

**Thandika Mkandawire** (1940–2020) was born in Malawi, studied in African and US academic settings and developed a major career in social policy and development research, including leadership of **CODESRIA**.

He criticized the assumption that African states were inherently incapable of effective industrial or welfare policy. Historical institutions, colonial legacies, administrative capabilities and political coalitions help explain why different countries achieve different outcomes.

His research challenged homogenizing accounts of Africa and the tendency to impose identical reform packages on states with widely divergent histories and productive structures.

### 139. Esther Boserup: agriculture, population and women's work

**Esther Boserup** (1910–1999) was born in Denmark, studied economics in **Copenhagen** and worked in international development.

In ***The Conditions of Agricultural Growth* (1965)**, she examined how population pressure can stimulate more intensive agriculture and technological change, challenging simple versions of Malthusian resource limits. Outcomes depend on land rights, productive capacity, institutions and environmental sustainability.

In ***Woman's Role in Economic Development* (1970)**, she documented how development policies and national statistics often ignored women's labour. Her legacy connects demographic change, agricultural technology and the distribution of unpaid and paid work.

### 140. Edith Penrose: growth through organizational capabilities

**Edith Penrose** (1914–1996) was born in the United States, trained in economics and pursued academic research in American and British institutions.

In ***The Theory of the Growth of the Firm* (1959)**, she explained how companies expand through accumulated knowledge, management skills and productive resources. Growth creates opportunities as capabilities develop, but rapid expansion can exceed managerial capacity.

Two firms with similar access to finance may perform differently because skills, organizational routines and learning cannot always be purchased immediately on open markets. Penrose influenced later resource-based and capability theories of enterprise.

### 141. Anne Krueger: rent-seeking and trade policy

**Anne O. Krueger** (born 1934) studied at **Oberlin** and the **University of Wisconsin–Madison** and developed careers in international trade, academic economics and multilateral institutions.

Her **1974 research on rent-seeking** showed how trade restrictions and licensing systems can encourage firms to spend resources obtaining privileges rather than expanding useful output. The social cost can exceed the visible difference in market prices.

Her support for liberalization and institutional reform has also been criticized where uniform prescriptions ignored productive capabilities or the social costs of transition. The contribution is a precise account of incentives created by regulatory privileges.

### 142. Dani Rodrik: globalization and democratic policy choice

**Dani Rodrik** (born 1957), originally from Turkey, studied at **Harvard** and **Princeton** and became a leading researcher in international and development economics.

His political **trilemma of the world economy** argues that very deep international integration, full national sovereignty and democratic choice cannot all be maximized simultaneously without institutional tensions.

Rodrik supports industrial strategies built around diagnosing information and coordination failures, testing results and correcting mistakes. His contribution is to focus on actual constraints and political accountability instead of assuming identical policies work everywhere.

### 143. Justin Yifu Lin: structural transformation and dynamic comparative advantage

**Justin Yifu Lin** (born 1952) earned an economics doctorate at the **University of Chicago** and developed a career at **Peking University** and the **World Bank**, where he became chief economist.

His **new structural economics** argues that development strategies should consider existing and emerging comparative advantages while using public action to address infrastructure and coordination problems.

The policy challenge is avoiding arbitrary subsidies and permanent protection. China's development involved a combination of competition, public institutions, state firms, international trade and regional experimentation that cannot be reduced to a single mechanism.

### 144. Fernando Fajnzylber: industry and equity in Latin America

**Fernando Fajnzylber** (1940–1991) was a Chilean economist associated with **ECLAC** and research on industrial policy, technology and Latin American development.

In ***The Incomplete Industrialization of Latin America* (1983)**, he examined how industrial sectors expanded without fully overcoming technological gaps and dependence. He later emphasized the possibility of productive transformation combined with social equity through education, innovation and effective institutions.

His approach challenged the view that protecting domestic markets was equivalent to developing globally competitive productive capabilities.

### 145. Nancy Folbre: the economics of care

**Nancy Folbre** (born 1952) studied economics and developed a research career in household labour, gender inequality and social policy.

Care for children, older adults and dependent people contributes to productive capacity and welfare even where no monetary transaction is recorded. The uneven distribution of this work affects time, income, career prospects and the costs of public services.

Folbre's contribution is not to assign one simplistic market wage to every household relationship but to make visible the economic activities often omitted from standard output accounts.

### 146. Raghuram Rajan: systemic incentives and financial fragility

**Raghuram G. Rajan** (born 1963) was born in India, studied at **IIT Delhi**, **IIM Ahmedabad** and **MIT Sloan**, and developed careers in academia, the **IMF** and the **Reserve Bank of India**, which he governed from **2013 to 2016**.

Rajan researched financial intermediation, incentive structures and risks generated by securitization, leverage and short-term funding. Before the **2008 crisis**, he raised concerns about the ways seemingly dispersed risks could become correlated across institutions.

He did not forecast every event or its date, but his work demonstrated why individual profits and risk models need not produce system-wide resilience. Later research also addressed institutions, inequality and the role of communities.

## XV — Economic thought as a contest of explanations and responsibilities

Ancient philosophers considered property, authority and justice. Mercantilists worked with governments competing for bullion, ships and taxes. Physiocrats described the circulation of agricultural surplus. Classical economists analysed production and distribution in industrializing capitalism. Marx located accumulation and conflict inside property and labour relations. Marginal economists developed models of preferences and prices. Econometricians tested claims against observed data. Keynes questioned how unemployment could persist; development economists challenged the presumption that the experiences of rich countries could be transferred without adaptation.

None of these transformations made every previous question obsolete. The gains from specialization remained relevant after Walras. Wicksell's banking mechanisms were not erased by Friedman. Hayek's knowledge problem did not invalidate Ostrom's evidence that communities can design institutions. Solow's growth framework did not make Furtado's structural inequalities irrelevant.

Economics advanced when investigators proposed mechanisms that could be debated, tested and revised. It became less reliable when theoretical assumptions were treated as universal facts or political preferences were presented as empirical certainty. Economic welfare depends on production, knowledge, natural resources, institutions and cooperation, but also on the distribution of gains and losses across people and places.

There is no final economic theory that can replace historical investigation, careful evidence and responsibility for the people affected by policy.
