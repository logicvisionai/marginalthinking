# AMR: national plans are expanding faster than the systems needed to measure and control resistance

*Research Report — 23 November 2026 | One Health, animal production and institutional capacity*

## The implementation gap

The latest global and European antimicrobial-resistance releases point to the same institutional problem from different directions: policy coverage has expanded, but the systems needed to detect resistance, measure antimicrobial use and finance implementation remain incomplete. The World Health Organization’s 2026 country self-assessment found that 173 countries report a national action plan, yet only 22% report both the necessary funding and full implementation. Only 36% report a national reference laboratory equipped to test the selected bacteria and fungi. [1]

The World Organisation for Animal Health’s new ANIMUSE report establishes a 2024 baseline for antimicrobial use in food-producing animals. Data from 119 participants cover 74% of estimated global animal biomass, but only five participants used farm records as a source for validated quantitative data. Most information came from sales and import records. The European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control and WHO Regional Office for Europe, meanwhile, report persistent high resistance in invasive human isolates and a 72% increase since 2019 in the EU incidence of bloodstream infections caused by carbapenem-resistant *Klebsiella pneumoniae*. [2][3]

These are not interchangeable measurements. ANIMUSE measures quantities intended for animal use, normalized by estimated biomass. ECDC and WHO report resistance in invasive human clinical isolates. TrACSS is a self-assessment of national implementation capacity. The datasets cannot establish that antimicrobial use in livestock caused the human resistance patterns observed in Europe. Their value is institutional: together they show that a One Health policy framework cannot be evaluated reliably without comparable surveillance, diagnostic capacity, field-level use data and funded national implementation.

Antimicrobial resistance is a cumulative loss of treatment effectiveness across human medicine, animal health, food production and the environment, not a single outbreak or a short-lived market shock. The 2026 Global Action Plan on AMR, adopted by the World Health Assembly in May, sets a framework through 2036. [5] The 2024 United Nations Political Declaration committed governments to stronger surveillance and diagnostic capacity and to a meaningful reduction in antimicrobial use in agrifood systems by 2030. [6] WHO estimates that AMR imposes substantial mortality and health-system costs, making implementation capacity a development and economic issue as well as a clinical one. [9] The new data show how far implementation remains from those commitments.

## 1. National plans are not the same as operating capacity

The WHO Tracking Antimicrobial Resistance Country Self-assessment Survey (TrACSS) was completed by 183 countries in 2026, representing 94% of WHO Member States. It records 173 countries with an AMR action plan, more than twice the 79 reported in 2017. The headline expansion is real, but the implementation figures are less reassuring: only 22% report both adequate funding and full implementation. [1]

The capacity gap appears at the point where a patient or an animal needs a diagnosis. Only 36% of countries report a national reference laboratory able to test the selected bacteria and fungi. The 2030 global target is 80%. Only 63% report guidelines for appropriate antimicrobial use, and just over half report surveillance systems that monitor national antibiotic consumption and resistance trends. A plan can therefore exist without the laboratories, workforce, data systems or budget required to change practice.

| National reference laboratory with selected testing capacity | 36% | Diagnostic infrastructure |

The 22% figure is not a direct audit of expenditure or service quality. TrACSS is a country self-assessment, and national definitions and reporting maturity differ. It is nevertheless a useful signal of the distance between formal commitments and reported implementation. The result also cautions against treating the number of national plans as a proxy for effective coverage.

The WHO report identifies practical tools for diagnostic testing as part of its AMR Diagnostic Initiative. Those tools address only one part of the problem. Laboratories need trained staff, quality-assurance systems, reliable specimen transport, reagents, equipment maintenance and referral networks. Surveillance requires standard case definitions and data exchange. A test result has limited policy value if it is not linked to prescribing guidance, infection prevention, procurement and follow-up.

## 2. Animal-use data have broader coverage, but remain far from farm-level measurement

WOAH’s ANIMUSE Global Report analyses 2024 data collected during its eleventh reporting round, which ran from September 2025 to March 2026. Of 148 participants, 132 supplied quantitative information and 119 were included in the global quantity analysis. Those 119 participants represented 74% of estimated global biomass for terrestrial and aquatic food-producing animals. Companion animals were excluded from the biomass-normalized global indicator. [2]

WOAH estimates that 83,254 tonnes of antimicrobial agents were reported as intended for use in animals in 2024, based on submitted data. After adjustment for estimated coverage, the total could reach 86,346 tonnes. The global indicator ranged from 87 to 90 milligrams per kilogram of estimated animal biomass, depending on the coverage adjustment. These are estimates built from participant submissions, not a census of every dose administered to every animal.

The measurement limitation is more important than the apparent precision of the global total. Only five of the 119 participants selected farm records — the source closest to the point of administration — for validated quantitative data. The report says the analysis relies primarily on sales and import records. Those records measure products entering a market or distribution chain; they do not necessarily show the quantity administered, the animal species treated, the indication, treatment duration or whether a product was used for growth promotion or veterinary treatment. [2]

| ANIMUSE 2024 measure | Reported result | Qualification |

The reporting system has improved. By April 2026, 59 Members had made at least one year of antimicrobial-use data public through ANIMUSE, five times the number recorded in December 2024. The report also finds that 88% of participants differentiated data by animal group. But public availability and category detail do not remove the gap between market-level sales and actual administration on farms. [2]

The difference affects policy. If a country records imports and sales but not use at the farm, it may be unable to distinguish therapeutic treatment from growth promotion, identify the species receiving a product or assess whether disease prevention reduced the need for medication. The same limitation makes it difficult to evaluate whether a regulation changed behaviour or merely changed the route through which products are supplied.

## 3. The trend is not yet a stable global decline

ANIMUSE reports a 3% decline in the biomass-adjusted indicator between 2019 and 2024 among 80 participants that supplied consistent data over the full period. In that cohort, the global indicator fell from 108 to 105 mg/kg. The report's foreword points to a separate analysis available through the ANIMUSE Interactive Report, not reproduced in this PDF, that shows a 9% increase between 2018 and 2024 among a different group of 74 participants. The executive summary and conclusions also cite that result. The periods and participant sets differ; the figures should not be combined into one continuous series. [2]

The contrast is not evidence that one result is false. It shows how participant composition, reporting coverage and time windows affect global aggregates. A smaller group with consistent reporting can reveal a trend within that group, while a broader or differently composed sample can produce another direction. Without stable national reporting and transparent methods, a global average may change because the sample changed rather than because use changed.

WOAH also reports that 29 of 148 participants (19%) continued to report antimicrobial use for growth promotion in the 2025 questionnaire, while ten were unsure. Seventeen of the 29 participants reporting use also had a regulatory framework governing the practice. Six of the 29 (21%) reported use of agents in the WHO highest-priority category for human health; four reported colistin. The report identifies bacitracin and tylosin among the most frequently reported growth-promoter agents, while colistin, enrofloxacin and flumequine are among the highest-priority agents of concern. These are participant reports, not an estimate that 19% of global animal production uses growth promoters. [2]

The current twelfth WOAH data-collection round began in September 2026 and introduces species-level data collection. That should improve the ability to distinguish patterns between animal sectors if participation and reporting quality are sustained. It is a new collection process, not yet a new global result.

## 4. Human clinical surveillance shows persistent resistance in invasive infections

The ECDC and WHO Regional Office for Europe report uses 2025 invasive-isolate data from two networks: EARS-Net, covering 30 EU/EEA countries, and CAESAR, covering 17 other countries in the WHO European Region. The networks use compatible methods, but national surveillance systems differ in population coverage, representativeness and laboratory practice. Cross-country comparisons therefore require caution. [3]

In 21 of 43 countries reporting the relevant combination, at least half of *K. pneumoniae* isolates were resistant to third-generation cephalosporins. In 21 of 38 countries, at least half of *Acinetobacter* isolates were resistant to carbapenems. These are proportions of countries crossing a resistance threshold for invasive isolates; they are not the proportion of all infections in the region, nor a direct measure of community prevalence. [3]

Within the EU, the estimated incidence of bloodstream infections caused by carbapenem-resistant *K. pneumoniae* reached 3.75 per 100,000 population in 2025. That was 72% above the 2019 baseline and 1.68 per 100,000 above the 2030 target of 2.07. The report describes a statistically significant increase between 2019 and 2025. It also notes that 21 of 43 countries in the wider WHO European Region reported resistance of at least 50% for third-generation cephalosporins in *K. pneumoniae*. [3]

The report identifies a broad north-to-south and west-to-east gradient for several Gram-negative pathogens, with higher resistance percentages more frequently reported in southern and eastern parts of the region. That pattern should not be converted into a country ranking without accounting for laboratory coverage, isolate representativeness and local surveillance protocols. The ECDC report also notes that 31% of countries in the wider region still collect AMR data without a standardized national system, while only 40% report funded and evaluated implementation of their national action plan. [3]

The EU-level consumption indicators improved in 2025, but remain off target. The 2025 ESAC-Net report records an EU population-weighted mean consumption of 18.9 defined daily doses per 1,000 inhabitants per day, 5% below the 2019 baseline. The EU 2030 target is 15.9, requiring a further reduction of 3.0. The EU population-weighted share of WHO AWaRe Access antibiotics reached 62.5%, up from 61.1% in 2019 but still 2.5 percentage points below the 65% target. Separately, ESAC-Net reports EU/EEA population-weighted community consumption of 17.3 defined daily doses per 1,000 inhabitants per day; this is a different indicator and should not be compared directly with the EU-level target. This is evidence of progress in consumption patterns, not proof that resistance is falling at the same rate. [4]

## 5. The data streams must not be treated as one causal chain

The three datasets answer different questions.

- **TrACSS** asks whether countries report national plans, funding, laboratories, guidelines and surveillance systems.
- **ANIMUSE** estimates antimicrobial quantities intended for animal use and normalizes them by estimated animal biomass.
- **EARS-Net and CAESAR** monitor resistance in invasive human clinical isolates.
- **ESAC-Net** measures human antimicrobial consumption in the EU/EEA.

The datasets are useful together because they expose the implementation gap across sectors. They cannot be merged to claim that a particular animal-use pattern caused a particular human resistance trend. Such an inference would require linked country-level data, species and pathogen matching, exposure pathways, antimicrobial classes, time lags and controls for human prescribing, hospital transmission, sanitation, travel and laboratory coverage.

```flow
National AMR plan
  -> budget, governance and accountability
  -> reference laboratories and standardized surveillance
  -> human clinical resistance and consumption data

Veterinary and agrifood regulation
  -> farm-level records, sales/import data and species-level reporting
  -> veterinary diagnostics, vaccination, biosecurity and husbandry
  -> animal antimicrobial-use indicators

Shared One Health analysis
  -> compare trends only where methods and units are compatible
  -> identify gaps and test transmission pathways
  -> do not infer animal-to-human causation from parallel aggregate series
```

Policy effectiveness depends on matching restrictions with disease prevention, veterinary access, diagnostic capacity and reliable records. A ban on a particular use may be necessary, but it cannot substitute for those operating capabilities. If farmers cannot diagnose disease, obtain vaccines or maintain biosecurity, poorly designed restrictions can increase treatment delays or push use outside formal reporting. Conversely, unrestricted access without stewardship can preserve incentives to use antimicrobials when better husbandry or vaccination would reduce disease incidence.

WOAH’s report emphasizes good husbandry, animal welfare, biosecurity and vaccination as ways to reduce the need for antimicrobials. The WHO Global Action Plan similarly emphasizes prevention, infection control, water and sanitation, surveillance and sustainable financing. The policy objective is not simply to reduce a national sales total. It is to reduce avoidable infections and inappropriate use while maintaining access to effective treatment when it is clinically necessary. The WOAH Code and the Quadripartite implementation framework emphasize prudent use, prevention and cross-sector accountability; WHO's updated action plan links those measures to sustainable financing and surveillance. [2][5][7][8][10]

## 6. Regional differences and the limits of comparison

The ANIMUSE 2024 biomass-adjusted estimates vary by WOAH region: approximately 143 mg/kg in Asia and the Pacific, 70 in the Americas, 38 in Europe, 34 in Africa and 11 in the Middle East. The regional sample sizes and coverage differ. The Americas had 97% estimated biomass coverage and Europe 73%, while coverage was lower in other regions; the Middle East analysis was based on only four participants. The values are not a simple ranking of stewardship quality or human-health risk. Species mix, production systems, reporting coverage and animal biomass estimates influence the result. [2]


The map combines two separate surveillance systems for orientation only. It does not imply that the regional animal-use estimates explain the human resistance gradient. A valid cross-sector analysis would need matched national datasets and compatible time periods.

## 7. Scenarios and measurable tests

**Base case — reporting expands faster than implementation.** More countries publish national plans and animal-use data, but financing, laboratories and farm-level measurement improve slowly. Global averages remain sensitive to participant composition, while resistant infections continue to impose treatment and hospital costs. The 2030 commitments become harder to verify because the underlying measurement systems remain incomplete.

**Upside case — implementation becomes measurable.** Countries fund reference laboratories, standardize surveillance, link human consumption and resistance data, and introduce species-level veterinary reporting. More participants use farm records rather than relying mainly on sales and imports. Growth-promotion practices decline, while vaccination, biosecurity and husbandry reduce avoidable treatment demand. The 2027 TrACSS and subsequent ANIMUSE rounds would show improved capacity and more stable trend cohorts.

**Downside case — plans remain administrative commitments.** Budgets are not released, laboratories cannot test priority pathogens, data remain incomplete and veterinary use is measured mainly through market supply. Growth-promotion use and high-priority agents remain in some reporting systems. Human clinical surveillance detects resistance late or in a biased sample, leaving hospitals and farms with fewer treatment options.

The base case would be weakened by a substantial increase in the share of countries reporting funded and evaluated national plans, a rise in reference-laboratory coverage towards the 80% target, and sustained improvements in farm-level animal-use records. The downside case would be weakened by stable, comparable ANIMUSE cohorts showing sustained declines and by falling incidence of resistant bloodstream infections in ECDC surveillance.

The central test is not whether countries have adopted another declaration. It is whether public budgets, diagnostic networks and data systems can make the commitments measurable. The current evidence shows a wider policy framework, better reporting coverage and some progress in antibiotic consumption, but not yet a reliable global system for tracking the use of antimicrobials at the point of administration or for linking that use to resistance outcomes across sectors.
