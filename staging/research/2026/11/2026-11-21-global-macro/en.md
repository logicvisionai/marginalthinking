# Global Macro — 21 November 2026

## Equities reprice sharply as Treasuries absorb the shock and credit risk begins to widen

*Information cutoff: 05:00 Brasília time, 21 November 2026. Market prices refer to the 20 November close unless stated otherwise.*

Friday's session produced a broad equity decline, but not a uniform liquidation across asset classes. Global shares fell, US Treasury yields dropped sharply, the dollar barely moved, gold and copper weakened, and oil rose on a geopolitical risk premium. The distinction matters: this was neither a clean inflation shock nor a straightforward recession trade. Sovereign duration attracted demand, while several assets normally treated as defensive did not.

The central question is whether the equity-led repricing remains confined to valuations or begins to affect credit intermediation, collateral and funding. The available evidence does not establish a systemic crisis. It does show a widening US high-yield spread before Friday's equity decline and vulnerabilities in non-bank finance identified by the European Central Bank (ECB). These are reasons to monitor transmission, not proof that forced selling has begun.

## 1. The new information is cross-asset transmission

The 17 November edition examined semiconductor repricing alongside Brazilian activity and inflation expectations. The 18 November report focused on European energy inflation and AI-infrastructure financing. The 19 November edition moved to energy security, Hormuz and the capital required to diversify supply. This edition changes the unit of analysis: the new information is the way the 20 November sell-off was distributed across equities, sovereign bonds, currencies, commodities and credit.

The MSCI gauge of global shares fell 3.30% on Friday and lost 8.1% for the week, its largest weekly decline since early 2020. The S&P 500 dropped 3.70%, the Nasdaq Composite 5.28% and the Dow Jones Industrial Average 1.93%. Both the S&P 500 and the Dow were more than 10% below their record closing highs. The VIX closed at its highest level since April 2025. The decline was international: the STOXX 600 fell 2.0%, emerging-market shares lost 3.10%, the Asia-Pacific index excluding Japan fell 3.27%, and the Nikkei dropped 5.06%. Taiwan's market lost about 17% over the week. US chip stocks fell 10.7% on Friday and 17.1% over the week; consumer staples were the only S&P 500 sector to finish higher. [1]

The bond response was different. The benchmark US 10-year yield fell 10.43 basis points to 4.948%, its largest daily decline since 22 October. The two-year yield fell 13.21 basis points to 4.647%, while the 30-year declined 6.81 basis points to 5.3329%. The larger move at the short end suggests that the immediate repricing concentrated on the expected policy path and near-term growth risk rather than a full collapse in long-term yields. [1]

The dollar did not deliver a broad safe-haven surge. The dollar index slipped 0.05% to 103.90 after reaching a 19-month high on Thursday. The euro eased 0.05% to $1.0961, while the dollar weakened 0.67% against the yen to 158.6. Gold fell 2.81% to $3,520.09 an ounce, silver lost 4.22% to $40.95 and copper declined 2.9% to $12,162.50 a tonne. Brent rose 0.68% to $92.61 a barrel and WTI gained 1.49% to $83.67. Reuters attributed the late-session oil move to concern about possible further attacks linked to the US-Israeli war with Iran and positioning ahead of the US Thanksgiving holiday. The price move itself does not establish that a new attack occurred. [1]

The pattern is more informative than any single price. Treasury demand increased, but the dollar and gold did not rise in parallel. Oil gained while copper fell. Investors were not simply moving from risky assets into every instrument conventionally described as safe. They were repricing exposures according to duration, liquidity, currency, commodity supply and the source of risk.

## 2. The Treasury curve still provides a hedge, but long-term funding remains expensive

The US Treasury par curve for 20 November placed the two-year yield at 4.63%, the five-year at 4.71%, the 10-year at 4.92% and the 30-year at 5.31%. The implied two-to-10-year slope was 29 basis points and the 10-to-30-year slope 39 basis points. These par-curve observations are not identical to the benchmark-yield series used in the Reuters market wrap; the small level differences reflect methodology, not conflicting quotations. [2]

The curve remains upward sloping and the long end remains above 5%, even after Friday's rally. A one-day fall in yields does not erase the cost of capital accumulated over previous months. The ECB's November Financial Stability Review notes that sovereign borrowing needs remain high in several advanced economies and that leveraged, news-sensitive investors have become more important in some government-bond markets. It also says that markets continued to function in an orderly manner. [3]

Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook said in a 20 November speech that she dissented from the October decision to hold the federal funds target range at 3.75%–4.00%, preferring another quarter-point increase. She described US growth as strong, the labour market as solid and inflation as too persistent. Her position is her own, not a statement of FOMC consensus. Cook estimated 12-month PCE inflation at 3.8% in October and core PCE at 3.2%, while citing unemployment of 4.1% and third-quarter GDP growth of 3.4%. Markets were still pricing a possible December rate increase when Treasury yields fell on Friday. [4]

In a separate comment reported by Reuters, Cook said the Federal Reserve was monitoring increasingly complex and interconnected financing arrangements supporting AI expansion. The published speech is the primary record of her policy position; the additional remarks are attributed media reporting. The signal is not that the Fed has adopted a new AI-specific rule. It is that strong aggregate growth, persistent inflation and complex investment financing can coexist with a sharp fall in technology valuations. [5]

## 3. Credit is the next transmission test

The latest ICE BofA US high-yield option-adjusted spread available at the information cutoff was 3.52% on 19 November, up from 3.23% on 13 November. The 29-basis-point widening preceded Friday's largest equity decline of the week. It indicates a deterioration in the price of lower-rated corporate risk, but the level remains far below the extremes associated with a broad credit seizure. A 20 November spread observation was not yet available at the cutoff. [6]

The ECB's November review reports that US hedge-fund repo borrowing has more than doubled since 2023. Around half of the largest euro-area high-yield bond funds hold insufficient high-quality liquid assets to cover outflows associated with a redemption shock. Redemptions from US private-credit funds remain elevated and are still being gated to avoid forced deleveraging or asset sales. The ECB also says that private credit is unlikely to pose systemic risk in isolation, while data gaps and links between banks and non-banks make total exposure difficult to assess. Banks retain strong capital, liquidity and profitability buffers. [3]

These observations do not mean Friday's equity decline caused a wave of fund redemptions. They identify where losses could travel if volatility persists: fund valuations, redemption terms, repo collateral, margin requirements and the supply of market liquidity. Evidence of a broader funding event would require persistent spread widening, tighter secured-funding terms, additional redemption restrictions, forced asset sales or a deterioration in market depth. One equity session is insufficient.

## 4. The macro backdrop is uneven, not uniformly recessionary

The US economy entered the sell-off with strong output data. The Bureau of Economic Analysis estimated third-quarter real GDP growth at a 3.4% annualized rate, compared with 2.2% in the second quarter. Real final sales to private domestic purchasers rose 4.9%. The PCE price index increased 2.1% and core PCE 2.5% in the quarter. The Bureau of Labor Statistics' October state release showed national unemployment at 4.1%; significant monthly declines occurred in the District of Columbia, Florida, New Hampshire and Pennsylvania, while Colorado recorded an increase. The October national employment report showed payroll gains of 165,000. These figures do not support a claim that the US economy had already entered a generalized contraction by Friday. They help explain why inflation-sensitive policy expectations can remain restrictive even as investors reduce equity risk. [7][8]

China's October data show a different imbalance. Industrial value added rose 4.8% year on year, manufacturing output increased 5.4% and high-tech manufacturing grew 17.6%. Retail sales rose only 0.4%, fixed-asset investment fell 8.1% in January–October and property-development investment declined 21.6%. Private fixed-asset investment was down 11.4%. Production strength is therefore not translating evenly into household demand or property investment. A strong manufacturing print alone does not prove a broad recovery in commodity demand. [9]

Japan's October consumer-price index rose 2.3% year on year; the measure excluding fresh food increased 2.2%, as did the measure excluding fresh food and energy. In the United Kingdom, retail volumes increased 0.7% in October after falling 0.6% in September; the three-month measure rose 0.4%. The same day's public-finance release showed October borrowing of £17.0 billion, debt-interest payments of £11.2 billion and public-sector net debt at 95.8% of GDP. Consumption was firmer, but the fiscal constraint remained visible. [10][11][12]

Brazil's central bank held the Selic target at 13.75% on 4 November. The Copom cited inflation expectations of 5.2% for 2026 and 4.8% for 2027, a more pronounced moderation in activity and the influence of fiscal developments on monetary policy and financial assets. B3 was closed on 20 November for the National Day of Zumbi and Black Consciousness; no Brazilian equity close should be inferred for Friday. Trading was scheduled to resume on Monday, 23 November. ## 5. Energy, commodities and capital flows

The latest US Energy Information Administration report, released on 18 November for the week ended 13 November, showed commercial crude stocks rising by 3.4 million barrels to 417.8 million, about 3% below the five-year average. Distillate stocks rose by 1.6 million barrels to 104.9 million but remained 5.6% below their level a year earlier. Gasoline stocks increased by 5.2 million barrels. These are US inventory data, not a global supply balance. They show a short-run cushion in crude and gasoline, while distillates remain tighter on a year-on-year comparison. [15]

Friday's rise in Brent and WTI therefore cannot be read as proof of a physical supply interruption. Reuters attributed the move to concern about possible further attacks related to the Iran conflict and to positioning before the US holiday. The inventory build and the oil-price rise can coexist: one describes recent US stocks; the other includes a forward-looking geopolitical premium. Gold's decline alongside a Treasury rally is also an observation, not proof of forced liquidation or a permanent change in its hedge role. [15]

At 07:57 UTC (04:57 Brasília time) on 21 November, Reuters reported that UK Maritime Trade Operations (UKMTO) had reported a tanker struck on its stern by an unidentified projectile about 11 nautical miles north of Khasab, Oman. No injuries were reported; the environmental impact was unknown. The report did not identify the vessel or the responsible actor, so it does not establish whether this was a separate incident from the tanker fire reported near Khasab on 20 November. This is an event-level maritime-security report, not proof of a sustained closure or a quantified interruption in oil flows. [17]

Separately, Reuters reported a fire at the Hartha power station north of Basra, Iraq. Citing security officials and Iraq's state news agency, INA, it said the 800 MW installed plant had been operating at about 400 MW before the fire and that operations were halted. The cause and extent of the damage had not been established. The incident indicates a local operating interruption; it does not establish an attack, a regional grid failure or a measurable loss of electricity exports. [18]

The US Treasury's September Treasury International Capital release, published on 18 November, reported a net foreign inflow of $261.0 billion. Private foreign inflows were $267.0 billion, while official institutions recorded net outflows of $6.0 billion. Foreign residents made net purchases of $186.7 billion in long-term US securities. These figures provide a baseline for foreign demand, but they refer to September, not 20 November. Treasury cautions that custodial data cannot attribute ownership precisely to individual countries. They should not be presented as evidence that investors were buying US assets during Friday's session. [14]

Fiscal constraints remain relevant to long-term pricing. UK net debt at 95.8% of GDP and monthly debt interest of £11.2 billion illustrate how higher financing costs can reduce fiscal room even when retail activity improves. The ECB warns that sovereign issuance needs and leveraged non-bank participation can make yields more sensitive to news. Brazil's Copom explicitly links fiscal developments to monetary conditions and financial assets. None of these observations proves an imminent sovereign crisis; together they explain why a temporary Treasury rally does not remove longer-term sensitivity to inflation, issuance and investor positioning.

## 7. Scenarios and falsifiers

The base case is a concentrated valuation correction. Technology and other long-duration shares remain volatile, but Treasury demand absorbs part of the shock, the dollar does not strengthen abruptly and credit spreads widen only gradually. This case would be supported by stabilization in the VIX, high-yield spreads and fund redemptions without new restrictions on market liquidity.

A more severe scenario would involve a transition from valuation losses to funding pressure. Warning signs would include sustained widening in high-yield and investment-grade spreads, tighter repo terms, higher margin requirements, additional gates on private-credit withdrawals, forced sales of liquid assets and deteriorating market depth. If these indicators appear together, the distinction between an equity correction and a broader credit event would no longer hold.

An inflationary scenario would look different. If oil's geopolitical premium persisted, inflation expectations rose and long-maturity yields reversed higher while the dollar strengthened, the Treasury hedge could become less reliable. A relief scenario would require lower geopolitical risk, evidence of easing inflation and stabilization in corporate earnings and funding conditions. These are conditional paths, not forecasts.

```flow
Technology and growth-valuation reset
  → higher equity risk premia and lower share prices
  → demand for sovereign duration and lower Treasury yields
  → valuation losses test fund liquidity, collateral and credit intermediation
Parallel energy channel: geopolitical risk
  → higher oil prices even while US inventories build
Parallel demand channel: weaker risk appetite
  → lower copper and precious-metal prices without a broad dollar surge
```

The thesis would weaken if equities recovered quickly, volatility normalized and credit spreads returned toward early-November levels without redemption pressure. It would strengthen if high-yield spreads continued to widen, private-credit gates increased, repo financing became more expensive or market depth deteriorated. One session cannot distinguish a technical correction from the beginning of a funding cycle; the next trading sessions and credit observations are decisive.

## 8. Balance of evidence

Friday's market action shows that sovereign bonds still provided a hedge against a sharp equity decline, but that hedge was not shared by the dollar, gold or industrial commodities. Oil followed a separate geopolitical channel. The macro data remain mixed: US output is strong, China combines high-tech production with weak domestic demand, Japanese inflation remains above target, the United Kingdom faces a narrow fiscal margin and Brazil maintains a restrictive policy rate.

The most defensible conclusion is higher market risk premia with an unproven transmission into systemic funding stress. Credit spreads had widened before Friday and the ECB has documented liquidity and leverage vulnerabilities, but there is no current evidence of a generalized credit freeze or broad reversal in international capital flows. The next test is not only the size of another equity decline. It is whether the cost and availability of funding change across the institutions that hold, finance and collateralize those assets.
