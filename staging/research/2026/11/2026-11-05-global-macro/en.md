# Global Macro — Oil's collapse and Treasury relief do not erase persistent inflation or restrictive monetary policy

**5 November 2026 · Early-morning edition, Brasília time · Information cutoff: 01:58 BRT (04:58 UTC).** Market prices are completed Wednesday, 4 November closes; economic releases are identified by their reference periods. The 5 November trading session had not ended and, across most of the Americas, had not yet started at the cutoff. [1][2][3]

Wednesday delivered a pronounced repricing. Brent crude futures fell **8.67% to US$94.62 per barrel**; the ten-year US Treasury constant-maturity yield declined from **4.95% to 4.90%**; the Dow Jones gained **3.03%**, while Brazil's Ibovespa rebounded **1.23% to 167,563.47 points**. This followed the Democratic Party's victory in the US midterm elections, with control of both houses of Congress shifting to the opposition. Investors reassessed the likelihood of congressional scrutiny and bargaining over the Iran conflict. A parliamentary election, however, is not itself a ceasefire, restored maritime passage or an enacted executive policy change. [1][2][3][4]

The financial move did not negate evidence of inflation pressure. Eurostat's October flash estimate put euro-area inflation at **4.0%**, versus **3.8%** in September, and the annual rate for energy at **20.8%**. US ISM services remained expansionary at **54.5**, but its prices-paid diffusion index registered **73.0**. Brazil's central bank **unanimously held its Selic rate at 13.75%**, pausing after five consecutive reductions. The regime is therefore characterized by *immediate relief in geopolitically sensitive asset prices* alongside *persistent costs, expectations and financing constraints*. [5][6][7][8]

## 2. US midterms: a legislative shift is not an automatic executive reversal

The 3 November midterms changed the balance of power in Washington. Democrats secured majorities in the House of Representatives and Senate, constraining President Donald Trump's ability to enact legislation and appropriations without negotiation. Equity and Treasury markets rallied on 4 November as investors weighed less room for unilateral legislative or fiscal initiatives and potentially stronger scrutiny of the Iran war. These are plausible mechanisms describing investors' expectations; a single session does not permit a rigorous causal attribution. [4]

Congress has powers over budgets, appropriations, legislation, hearings, confirmations and oversight. Opposition control strengthens its capacity to condition spending, investigate executive choices and demand evidence. It does not automatically transfer the conduct of diplomacy or military operations to lawmakers. Presidential veto authority, committee composition, party discipline and constitutional timing matter. Nor do election results instantly translate into enacted statutes: the composition and functioning of the new legislature must still take effect through the institutional timetable.

The oil market could discount increased political pressure for a diplomatic outcome well before actual negotiations produce a lasting arrangement. Ships, maritime insurers and refiners confront physical hazards and contractual obligations whose status cannot be verified from a stock-market rally. The most persuasive evidence of structural change would be appropriations and legal acts, shifts in war policy, dated maritime-traffic records and safer physical supply chains.

The distributional effects also matter. A prospect of smaller fiscal deficits may ease long sovereign yields, benefiting mortgage borrowers and companies refinancing long-term debt, but fiscal gridlock can also delay public investment and infrastructure approvals. Technology platforms and AI companies could face greater congressional questioning even if overall equity markets advance. Election uncertainty can diminish without removing disagreement over policy.

## 3. Oil prices: fast financial adjustment, slower physical transmission

Brent settled on 4 November at **US$94.62 per barrel**, down **8.67%**, with WTI at **US$88.12**, lower by about **8.3%**. Brent had closed at **US$103.60** the previous day; the **US$8.98** difference represents an unusually consequential repricing of geopolitical and supply-risk expectations. The immediate impetus included the US electoral result and expectations of a political push toward diplomacy. But the contracts do not demonstrate that the Strait of Hormuz has recovered its previous throughput. [1][4][9]

```chart

4 November | 94.62
```

A futures contract is distinct from the delivered price of physical crude, which varies by grade, port, vessel availability, freight, quality and risk insurance. Refined fuels introduce a further layer: refinery utilization, diesel and aviation-fuel inventories, regulatory requirements and distribution determine the price received by final customers. Retail prices may adjust with a lag, and exchange rates can offset part of a dollar-denominated fall. An importing economy can benefit from improved terms of trade while an exporter or petroleum-dependent fiscal authority loses revenue.

Brazil's equity market displayed this divergence. The broad Ibovespa advanced, but Petrobras, PRIO and other energy producers fell with crude. For an upstream producer, a lower oil benchmark can lower expected revenue per barrel; for airlines, road transport and some industrial users, cheaper final fuels can improve margins after hedges and contractual pass-through. Neither effect can be inferred solely from a firm's percentage share-price change. Corporate debt, taxes and foreign-exchange exposure can change the net result. [3]

A sound evaluation of energy normalization requires dated shipping volumes, effective delivery and insurance terms, refiner operations, inventories of crude and petroleum products, and evidence of a diplomatic settlement. Announced production requirements and actual exported barrels are separate measurements. Without physical corroboration, another futures price decline is best treated as a shift in market expectations, not as proof that disruption has ended.

```flow
US legislative shift and increased expectations of war oversight → Geopolitical risk premium declines in crude futures → Potential relief for importers and freight
Verified improvements in passage and war-risk insurance → Actual deliveries of crude and refined products → Physical prices → Consumer inflation with a lag
Lower sovereign benchmark yields → Potential easing in new issuance costs → Investment and consumption, conditional on creditworthiness
```

## 4. Euro area: October inflation reaches 4% despite falling November oil

Eurostat's preliminary October reading put euro-area harmonized consumer-price inflation at **4.0% year on year**, compared with **3.8%** in September. Energy inflation accelerated to **20.8%** from **18.9%**; services to **3.3%** from **3.2%**; food, alcohol and tobacco to **1.6%** from **1.3%**; and non-energy industrial goods to **1.2%** from **1.1%**. The measure excluding energy, food, alcohol and tobacco was **2.5%**. Component rates are not additive contributions unless weighted appropriately. [5]

The seeming paradox of expensive energy in October and plunging oil futures in early November disappears when calendar and measurement are respected. October's annual inflation compares prices with October 2025. A 4 November oil-market settlement cannot revise consumers' already observed October prices. Additionally, businesses may buy energy through long-term contracts, regulated utility tariffs may reset infrequently and labour and service prices have different adjustment cycles.

The European Central Bank must balance the risk of persistent inflation and inflation expectations against housing, industrial and financial pressure from restrictive interest rates. The direction of any future decision cannot be deduced from a single oil session. Natural gas and electricity prices, wages, exchange rates and cross-country differences in energy intensity affect transmission. A euro-area average hides significant heterogeneity: a net importer with energy-intensive industry is exposed differently from an economy dominated by services and less energy-sensitive production.

A sustained decline in physically delivered energy prices could eventually help households' purchasing power and manufacturers' margins. But if financial conditions stay restrictive, the resulting benefit may be absorbed by interest expense or credit rationing. Industrial competitiveness therefore depends on both variable input costs and the cost and availability of capital for modernization.

## 5. United States: measured job gains, expansionary services, elevated prices

ADP Research estimated **86,000 additional private-sector jobs in October**, equal to a downwardly revised **86,000** in September. Service-producing industries supplied **82,000** of the October total: trade, transportation and utilities gained **39,000**, education and healthcare **28,000**, while professional and business services lost **27,000**. ADP also reported **3.2%** median year-on-year growth in base pay versus **4.7%** in gross pay, which captures other earnings components. [7]

ADP's administrative payroll records should not be confused with the Bureau of Labor Statistics' official employment survey. Their universes, estimation procedures and revisions differ, and private employment does not automatically include public-sector jobs. The 86,000 observation supports a finding of moderate private-sector hiring, not a precise estimate of total US payrolls or the unemployment rate. The official October labour report was still forthcoming at this edition's information cutoff.

The ISM services survey indicated ongoing expansion at **54.5** in October, down from **54.9**. New orders came in at **55.9** versus **59.8** the month before; business activity at **53.8** versus **56.5**; and employment at **51.4**, up from **50.1**. Meanwhile, the prices index remained exceptionally high at **73.0**. A diffusion index at 73 means price increases are more prevalent among responding businesses; it emphatically does not mean average service prices rose 73%. Survey respondents also identified pressure in memory products and computing-related components. [6]

Moderate hiring and widespread input-price pressure can coexist. Employers can hire more slowly even when transport, insurance, technical components, contractual wages and delayed supplier pass-through remain costly. This mix is relevant to the Federal Reserve because it complicates the interpretation of labour resilience, potential demand weakness and supply-driven inflation. The ADP and ISM reports do not yield an unequivocal policy prescription; official payrolls, inflation, real wages, financial conditions and revisions remain necessary evidence. [6][7][10]

The concentration of certain critical computing inputs is also relevant to AI infrastructure investment. Even where technology company valuations rise, memory availability, power infrastructure, construction logistics and interest expense can constrain actual deployment. Large announcements of data-centre expenditure are not the same as measured productive capacity or realized returns.

## 6. Brazil: a Copom pause despite the rally in stocks and the real

On 4 November, Brazil's Monetary Policy Committee **unanimously held the Selic benchmark at 13.75% a year**, interrupting five consecutive rate cuts. Its statement described sharper moderation in cyclical economic sectors and some marginal weakening in labour indicators, but also rising inflation, higher expectations and upside-skewed risks. Focus survey expectations cited by the committee stood at **5.2% for 2026** and **4.8% for 2027**; the committee's reference-scenario projection for its relevant horizon, **the second quarter of 2028**, reached **3.5%**. A market expectation, a central bank model forecast and an already observed consumer-price index must remain analytically distinct. [8][11][12]

A decision to hold rates is not a promise to raise them, nor is it a timetable for resuming easing. If exchange-rate depreciation and fiscal uncertainty feed inflation expectations, a central bank may need to keep policy restrictive despite weaker activity. Market yields on government securities also include risk and term premia not set by the Selic alone. Lower US Treasury yields offer some external relief, but they cannot substitute for domestic fiscal credibility and the quality of government debt financing.

Before the committee's announcement, the Ibovespa had closed **1.23% higher at 167,563.47**, snapping six sessions of losses, while the commercial dollar fell about **0.64% to R$5.45**. The global market environment clearly mattered; nevertheless, the local rally is not evidence that Brazil's next fiscal decisions have already been defined or accepted. Crucially, the Brazilian stock exchange **closed before** the Copom communiqué, so the day's stock-market rise must not be attributed to a later announcement. [3]

The meeting followed the reelection of Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva amid concern over spending rules, the composition of the economic team and the trajectory of mandatory expenditure. A high domestic interest rate raises the budgetary cost of refinancing public liabilities, particularly where liabilities are linked to the Selic or repriced at market yields. Fiscal uncertainty can produce a feedback loop: a weaker currency raises imported prices, expectations become less anchored, credit remains expensive, and activity slows. It is a conditional mechanism rather than an inescapable outcome. Credible, implemented fiscal measures and sustained disinflation could weaken it. [8][9]

These effects are uneven across society. Mortgage and consumer-credit borrowers, small firms, construction companies and longer-payback infrastructure projects confront high financing costs. Commodity exporters may benefit from a weaker currency in local revenues but lose from falling global commodity prices. Low-income households remain vulnerable to food, energy and transport prices. An equity-index rebound cannot replace analysis of real disposable incomes, financing access and employment dynamics.

## 7. Geographic exposure: energy dependence meets divergent monetary regimes

```map
title: Regional exposures to the new oil, inflation and monetary-policy shock
United States | Fiscal and geopolitical repricing | Opposition controls both chambers; ten-year Treasury yield 4.90% after falling
Brazil | Restrictive rates and partial market rebound | Selic held at 13.75%; stocks rise before statement even as oil producers fall
Euro area | Persistent annual consumer inflation | October flash HICP 4.0% and annual energy inflation 20.8%
Australia | Monetary tightening | Reserve Bank lifted its policy rate to 4.85% on 3 November
Middle East | Physical energy and shipping risk | Falling futures cannot establish normalized Hormuz deliveries or insurance
Energy-importing Asia | Terms of trade and logistics | Cheaper crude may help import bills, depending on FX, insurance, inventories and demand
```

Location in the energy supply chain matters as much as the international reference price. Oil importers can benefit from lower import bills if delivered costs follow futures down. Producers and hydrocarbon-dependent fiscal authorities may suffer lower receipts. Countries borrowing in hard currency face additional exposure to US term yields even when their consumer inflation is domestic. A specific airline's economics depend on jet fuel pricing, hedging, occupancy and dollar debt, not mechanically on the Brent percentage change.

Australia offers an informative comparison: its central bank lifted rates to **4.85%** on 3 November, while Brazil paused easing on 4 November and American government bond yields fell. These movements do not imply inconsistent policy. They arise from different domestic inflation processes, labour conditions, housing cycles and currencies. The concept of a global synchronized easing cycle is therefore unsupported by these decisions. [10][11]

## 8. Three falsifiable paths for the coming weeks

**Energy relief passes through to the real economy.** If diplomatic talks lead to documented improvements in shipping, insurance, oil deliveries and refined-product costs, lower Brent could ultimately reduce imported inflation and support consumer spending. Sustained declines in long-term yields and credit spreads would strengthen that transmission. The scenario weakens if futures fall while freight costs, cargo delivery and physical inventories fail to improve.

**Financial relief without actual disinflation.** If euro-area inflation stays above target, US service providers continue reporting widespread price increases and Brazil's expectations remain elevated, central banks may preserve restrictive settings even after an equity rally. Households and companies would still face expensive debt and uneven real income gains. Sequential declines in core inflation and actual borrowing rates would be meaningful counterevidence.

**Fiscal or geopolitical risk returns.** Legislative deadlock, new hostilities or renewed supply disruption could restore risk premia to oil and sovereign markets. In the other direction, verifiable fiscal commitments in Brazil and stable legislative agreements in Washington could lower risk premia without instantly reversing past inflation or public debt accumulation. Speeches and one-day financial closes are not sufficient evidence of implementation.

The next observable tests include the scheduled official US October payroll release on 6 November, shipping flows and petroleum inventories, Eurostat revisions, Brazilian inflation and Focus expectation releases, the Treasury curve, and enacted rather than merely proposed policy decisions. Quantitative scenario probabilities would be speculative without a defensible calibrated model.

## 9. Regime assessment

On 4 November, energy futures and long-term government yields fell substantially as Western equity markets recovered. Yet economic indicators covering October and Brazil's rate decision showed how financial relief can lead changes in prices paid by households and businesses. European annual inflation accelerated, US service-provider price pressures remained broad, and Brazil stopped reducing its benchmark rate. None of these facts negates the others.

The essential distinction is time. Markets adjust probabilities within minutes; monetary authorities react to data persistence and expectations; supply chains and contracts take longer to reprice; and governments change policy through legal decisions and implementation. Treating expected peace as completed peace, or a one-day stock rebound as fiscal stabilization, would exceed the available evidence. The macro-financial environment at the opening of 5 November is one of **conditional financial decompression, not yet global disinflationary normalization**.
