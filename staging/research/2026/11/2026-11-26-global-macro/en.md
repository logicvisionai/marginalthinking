# Tariff proposals become concrete, but markets price the process—not the duty

*Global Macro — 26 November 2026 | Information cutoff: 05:05 BRT, America/Sao_Paulo*

## Executive assessment

The new information is not simply another tariff threat. On 25 November, the Office of the United States Trade Representative (USTR) made formal Section 301 determinations covering 14 economies and proposed responsive duties, opening a public-comment process. The measures are not yet effective tariffs. Mexico and Norway were not included in the determinations announced that day, and the proposals contain product exemptions, netting against specified existing duties and possible adjustments linked to negotiated commitments. [1]

Markets initially sold off, then partially recovered as investors assessed the proposal's legal and commercial scope. That response is understandable, but it does not eliminate the macroeconomic risk. A proposed duty can change sourcing, inventory and investment decisions before customs collections begin; if adopted, it can raise some landed costs while redirecting trade rather than reducing it. The distribution of the burden depends on product coverage, exemptions, supplier substitution, exchange rates and firms' ability to absorb costs in margins.

The latest US data complicate the policy response. Real GDP grew at a 4.3% annual rate in the third quarter, revised up from 3.4%, while October headline and core PCE inflation each rose 0.3% month on month and 3.8% and 3.3% year on year, respectively. Real personal consumption expenditures were essentially flat in October. Strong quarterly output therefore coexists with persistent inflation and a weaker monthly consumption pulse. [2] [3]

Observed: USTR published determinations and proposed actions; US Q3 GDP was revised higher; October PCE inflation remained elevated; equities were mixed on 25 November; Treasury yields fell; Brent rose amid attributed reports of attacks and shipping disruption. Inference: investors are distinguishing a tariff proposal from a tariff actually collected, while still demanding a premium for policy, energy and technology-financing uncertainty. Scenario, not fact: if duties are adopted broadly and retaliation follows, the combination could delay investment and keep core goods inflation firmer into 2027.

## 1. The legal status changed—and the distinction is economically material

USTR determined that practices relating to structural excess capacity and manufacturing production in China, the European Union, Singapore, Switzerland, Indonesia, Malaysia, Cambodia, Thailand, South Korea, Vietnam, Taiwan, Bangladesh, Japan and India were actionable under Section 301. The agency published economy-specific reports and proposed responsive action for public comment. It did not announce a uniform tariff already collected at customs. [1]

| Economy | Proposed additional duty | Qualification |\n|---|---:|---|\n| Bangladesh | Up to 18% | Net of existing Section 301 duties; exemptions |\n| China | Up to 20% | Product exceptions and specified existing-duty treatment |\n| European Union | 15% | Net of MFN and existing Section 301 duties; exemptions |\n| India | Up to 25% | Proposal includes possible 17% rate under a responsive agreement |\n| Malaysia | Up to 18% | Net of existing duties; exemptions |\n| Singapore | Up to 20% | Proposal contemplates 10% under a responsive agreement |\n| Vietnam | Up to 35% | Proposal contemplates 20% under a responsive agreement |

These are proposed ceilings, not final applied rates. Federal Register notices and annexes determine product scope; the USTR summary alone is not a substitute for customs classification. Comments are due 5 January 2027, and hearings are scheduled to begin on 25 January. The interval creates room for consultation, modification, litigation and negotiated commitments. [1]

The market's relief on 26 November reflects a comparison with earlier expectations, not proof that the proposal is harmless. Netting rules and exemptions may reduce the incremental burden for some products. For others, even a lower duty can be material when imported inputs are a large share of cost and supplier substitution is slow. The relevant measure is the change in the effective landed cost of a specific product, not the largest number in a press release.

## 2. The transmission starts in business decisions, before it appears in inflation

The first response is likely to be commercial rather than statistical. Importers may advance orders, hold additional inventory, renegotiate supplier prices or delay a sourcing decision until product coverage is clearer. Manufacturers may compare a tariff on the finished good with duties on intermediate components and reconsider where final assembly takes place. These actions consume working capital and can create temporary demand for freight and warehousing even when final demand has not changed.

```flow\nUSTR determination and proposed duty\n→ public comments, hearings and possible negotiation\n→ final product coverage and effective date\n→ landed costs, inventory and supplier choices\n→ margins, import prices and investment timing\n→ consumer prices, trade volumes and monetary-policy expectations\n```

The chain is conditional at every step. A proposal can be narrowed or delayed. A firm may absorb a duty in its margin rather than pass it on. A currency move can offset part of the price change. A buyer may switch to a supplier outside covered jurisdictions, changing trade routes without necessarily reducing total imports. A tariff can therefore raise prices in one sector, compress profits in another and redirect production to a third economy.

The opposing interpretation is that the market move is mainly a technology valuation and funding event, with tariffs acting as a catalyst rather than the underlying cause. That alternative is plausible: the Nasdaq and chip shares were weaker than the Dow, and S&P Global Ratings placed Nvidia's 'AA' ratings on CreditWatch negative citing rising management-and-governance risk. This is a governance review, not evidence of default or a collapse in AI demand. [4] [5]

## 3. US growth is strong in the quarter, not uniformly strong in the month

BEA's second estimate put Q3 real GDP growth at 4.3% annualized, compared with 2.2% in Q2. Real final sales to private domestic purchasers—a measure combining consumption and fixed investment—rose 5.8% annualized. Investment, consumer spending and government spending contributed; exports declined and imports increased. The revision primarily reflected higher estimates of investment and consumption. [2]

The October household release is less buoyant. Current-dollar personal income rose 0.7% and disposable income 0.8%, but real PCE was unchanged at the reported monthly precision. Headline PCE inflation was 0.3% monthly and 3.8% annually; core PCE was also 0.3% monthly and 3.3% annually. The saving rate was 4.1%. The increase in nominal income partly reflected compensation, government benefits and receipts on assets; BEA notes a one-time Medicare Improvement Fund premium rebate within social benefits. [3]

| US indicator | Latest observation | Scope |\n|---|---:|---|\n| Real GDP, Q3 | +4.3% annualized | Quarterly output, revised estimate |\n| Final sales to private domestic purchasers, Q3 | +5.8% annualized | Domestic private demand |\n| Headline PCE, October | +0.3% m/m; +3.8% y/y | PCE price index |\n| Core PCE, October | +0.3% m/m; +3.3% y/y | PCE excluding food and energy |\n| Real PCE, October | About 0.0% m/m | Inflation-adjusted household spending |\n| Personal saving rate, October | 4.1% | Saving as share of disposable income |

The combination is not contradictory. GDP is a quarterly aggregate reported at an annualized rate; real PCE is a monthly household-flow estimate. A strong investment quarter can lift GDP while household volumes pause in one month. The GDP revision also does not establish that all sectors are accelerating: imports rose, exports fell and component contributions differ from the headline rate.

For the Federal Reserve, the data reduce the case for rapid easing but do not mechanically dictate a December increase. Market pricing reported on 26 November continued to imply a high probability of a 25-basis-point hike, while speculation about a larger move receded. Pricing is a market estimate, not a policy commitment. [5]

## 4. Rates, currencies and risk assets tell a split story

On 25 November, the Dow rose 0.33%, while the S&P 500 fell 0.25% and the Nasdaq fell 0.62%. The MSCI world gauge declined about 0.6% in the session. US Treasury yields fell as investors sought safety: the 10-year yield was reported at 4.84%, down roughly 6.2 basis points. The US dollar index eased about 0.14%; the euro traded near US$1.09. These are observations from the reported session, not official closing values for 26 November, when US cash markets are closed for Thanksgiving. [5] [6]

```chart\ntype: bar\ntitle: Selected equity-index returns, 25 November 2026\nunit: percent, daily session\nDow Jones | 0.33\nS&P 500 | -0.25\nNasdaq Composite | -0.62\nMSCI world | -0.60\n```

The fall in yields alongside high GDP and inflation is important. It is consistent with demand for duration as equity risk is reduced, rather than a clean repricing toward easier monetary policy. A lower Treasury yield can coexist with a higher expected policy rate when investors seek safety or question the durability of earnings and investment. The curve should be read with equity, credit and currency moves, not in isolation.

Emerging-market flows remain a vulnerability, although the latest comparable estimate is monthly rather than intraday. The Institute of International Finance reported US$33.8 billion of non-resident portfolio outflows from emerging markets in October, after US$19.3 billion in September and against US$27.4 billion of inflows in October 2025. This was the largest monthly outflow since March, according to the IIF tracker. It does not show that every emerging market experienced an outflow or that November flows have already been measured. [7]

## 5. Europe, Asia and industrial production remain uneven

Eurostat's final October HICP put euro-area inflation at 4.0% year on year, up from 3.8% in September. Energy contributed 1.89 percentage points to the annual rate; services contributed 1.55 points. The energy contribution means that a supply shock can matter for headline inflation even if the underlying domestic price process is more moderate. It does not mean energy alone explains the full 4.0%. [8]

In Reuters' early-Asia snapshot published at 01:35 UTC on 26 November, Japan's Nikkei gained 1.6%, South Korea's main index rose 1.9%, Taiwan added 1.4% and Asia-Pacific shares excluding Japan rose 1.8%. The same report said Japan was down 8% for November, South Korea 16% and Taiwan 15%. European futures—not cash-market closes—were also higher: EURO STOXX 50 +2.3%, DAX +2.1% and FTSE +1.4%. The rebound followed the prior selloff and reflected a less severe reading of the tariff proposal; it should not be treated as a reversal of the month-to-date drawdown. [5]

Industrial data reinforce the need to avoid a single global-cycle label. worldsteel reported October crude-steel production of 142.4 million tonnes across 70 reporting economies, down 1.1% year on year. North America rose 5.6%, Africa 7.9%, the EU fell 4.0%, South America fell 4.1%, and the Middle East fell 27.7%. These are regional output observations, not a direct measure of final demand or capacity utilization. [9]

The divergence matters for the transmission mechanism. Wheat and maize eased with subdued import demand and an improving production outlook, while sugar rose for a fourth consecutive month as rain disrupted Brazil's Centre-South cane harvest and crushing and production prospects weakened in India, the EU and Thailand. Rice rose 1.6% with harvest delays and weather pressure in some exporters. FAO forecast 2026 cereal production at 2,999 million tonnes, the second-highest on record, but 2026/27 cereal trade was projected to fall 3.3% to 507.7 million tonnes; stocks-to-use was forecast at 32.4%. These are forecasts, not realized balances. FAO also estimated 41 countries or territories still required external food assistance, 31 in Africa; the country count is not a population estimate. [13]

A lower global cereal quotation can ease some import costs without improving food access everywhere. Domestic currency depreciation, transport, conflict, wages and retail margins intervene between export quotations and household baskets; the aggregate index can fall while vulnerable households face worsening affordability.

Brazil is not among the 14 economies covered by the USTR determinations announced on 25 November. The immediate direct tariff channel from this particular action is therefore not established for Brazil. Indirect effects remain possible through trade diversion, commodity prices, exchange rates and demand from affected partners. Banco Central do Brasil maintained the Selic at 13.75% in November; this restrictive rate is a domestic monetary condition, not a response to a tariff that has not yet been implemented. [10]

## 6. Energy and conflict claims add a separate inflation channel

At 07:58 GMT on 26 November (04:58 BRT), Brent was US$94.52 per barrel, down 0.1% on the day, and WTI was US$83.83, up 0.2%; WTI had no settlement scheduled because of Thanksgiving. Brent had settled 3.5% higher in the previous session and WTI 2% higher. Reuters attributed the prior session's supply premium to the Houthis' claim of an attack on Saudi Arabia's Ras Tanura refinery and Iranian reports that mines struck tankers in the Strait of Hormuz. The claims are attributed; this edition does not independently verify the incidents or assign responsibility. Reuters also reported that Middle East exports had been rising despite shipping attacks and that prices had fallen about a quarter in the three weeks before the latest rebound, a counterweight to a persistent-supply-loss interpretation. [11]

The energy channel differs from the tariff channel. A tariff affects covered goods and can be partly absorbed or rerouted. A sustained interruption to oil exports or shipping can affect a wider set of transport, petrochemical and energy costs. But a single oil-price jump after an unverified incident report is not proof of a lasting supply loss. The IEA's November outlook described rising global supply and tight buffers in refined products; that broader balance is a counterweight to a purely disruption-driven interpretation. [12]

## 7. Scenarios and falsifiers

Base case: proposed tariffs proceed through consultation and are modified by product exclusions, existing-duty netting or negotiated commitments. Firms delay some sourcing decisions but avoid a broad stop in trade. The inflation impulse is concentrated in covered goods and arrives with a lag; central banks remain cautious rather than automatically tightening in response to the announcement.

Adverse case: final measures are broad, effective dates are short and affected partners retaliate. Importers front-load purchases, then reduce orders; working-capital demand rises; margins compress; prices increase in covered categories. If energy disruptions persist at the same time, headline inflation and expectations become harder to contain, while investment and trade volumes weaken.

Relief case: consultation narrows the product list, exemptions are extensive and agreements prevent overlapping duties. The tariff risk premium fades, but technology and credit repricing remains. A market recovery would then be more durable only if earnings, cash generation and investment returns support valuations.

The preferred interpretation would weaken if final tariff coverage is narrow or delayed, effective landed costs show little change, trade volumes and capex plans remain stable, and subsequent core-goods inflation falls despite the proposal. It would also weaken if equity volatility is explained more convincingly by AI-related financing and governance concerns than by trade-policy exposure.

The next useful observations are the final product annexes, public-comment record, effective dates, customs data by product and origin, import-price indices, corporate margin disclosures, November capital-flow estimates, and the next PCE and employment releases. Until those arrive, the correct conclusion is a policy-transmission risk with a measurable legal process—not a tariff shock already fully realized.
