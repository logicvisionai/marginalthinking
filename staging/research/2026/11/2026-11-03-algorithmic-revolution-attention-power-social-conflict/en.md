# The Algorithmic Revolution: From the Quantification of War to the Struggle over Attention, Behaviour and Power

A soldier films a trench from the first-person perspective; a teenager repeats a gesture in a public square for an audience assembled by a social platform; a political movement circulates a meme that can be understood without watching a speech; a worker is evaluated by software that neither party can readily explain; an intelligence officer receives ranked information from a system whose errors may be difficult to trace. These scenes are historically distinct. Their common feature is that information about human activity passes through technical systems that classify it, predict its relevance or consequences, and redistribute it according to operational objectives.

The claim that algorithms "control society" is simultaneously provocative and imprecise. An algorithm is a specified procedure; it is not necessarily an artificial-intelligence model, a commercial recommendation system or a political actor. People continue to choose, resist, distrust, create and organise independently of platforms. Yet the distribution of choices is affected by who controls their informational environment, the criteria of selection, access to resources and opportunities, and the visibility granted to particular conduct. An algorithm does not need to issue an order to become part of social power. It may alter which people or messages are noticed, which applications are considered, which actions become worth filming, and which representations of a conflict travel farthest.

Three transformations account for the historical significance of this shift. First, the ambition to make complex processes calculable moved from statistics and administrative organisation into digital infrastructure. Second, behavioural data became a continuous input for ranking, pricing, monitoring and recommending, with effects that feed into the next round of observed data. Third, a growing share of social life acquired a prospective audience: a person may act while anticipating a video, a metric, a reaction or a possible recommendation. These transformations can improve coordination, widen access to knowledge, uncover abuses and reduce costs; they can also concentrate decision-making, reward distorted proxies, shape perceptions and redistribute risks without an equally visible system of accountability.

The central issue is not whether mathematical procedures have displaced human agency. It is how much of the environment in which human agency operates is now produced by institutions that use mathematical procedures to pursue objectives different from those of the individuals affected. No single technology, company, nation or political ideology explains the result. The evidence varies sharply between an experiment that identifies a specific effect, a study of online communities, a regulatory allegation, a military communication practice and an analogy drawn from philosophy. The distinctions are indispensable to explaining both the reach and the limits of algorithmic power.

## 1. A problem before the platforms: what can administration actually measure?

Long before a user opened a personalised feed, states used inventories, population registers, taxes, probabilities and classifications to administer territories and allocate resources. The industrial age added engineering measurements, production planning, accounting, insurance risk calculations and the systematic evaluation of labour. These methods were not a single unified ideology. They became powerful because formal measurements let organisations coordinate actions across distances, compare alternatives and document performance. They were also selective: a register records categories recognised by administrators; a productivity measure records the product that its designers decide to count.

The Second World War accelerated the development of operational research, including mathematical assessment of logistics, radar, allocation and combat decisions. Postwar computing and information theory expanded the ability to represent problems formally. Cybernetics studied communication and control in machines and organisms; statistical decision theory and operations management offered ways to make uncertainty tractable. There were important differences between Wiener, Shannon, Simon and subsequent engineers. A rigorous historical genealogy cannot convert these distinct disciplines into a direct line of descent leading inevitably to TikTok. Their shared contribution was to make prediction and feedback organisationally practical.

Quantification creates a temptation when a measure is easier to improve than the underlying objective. A company can raise the number of customer interactions without improving satisfaction. A security agency can count incidents detected without establishing whether a territory is safer. A school can optimise examination scores without measuring broader understanding. This is a general institutional vulnerability: people adapt to the measures by which they are assessed, and measurements may lose their meaning precisely because they have become targets. Whether that happens depends on governance, independent evidence and how the metric is designed.

A present-day feed produces a distinctive variant. Its measures are not only retrospective accounts. Views, skips, likes, follows and completion rates enter feedback loops that affect future exposure. People can then adapt what they produce to attract those measures. The shift is from **describing behaviour to participating in the conditions under which behaviour is rewarded**. It does not erase the earlier history of bureaucracy; it extends it into everyday interaction.

## 2. Robert McNamara and *The Fog of War*: when a metric obscures a war

Errol Morris's documentary *The Fog of War* (2003) is a useful opening to the history of the algorithmic society because it examines how a highly capable administrator confronted the limits of technical rationality in military and political decision-making. Its subject, Robert S. McNamara, had worked with quantitative management and later directed the US Department of Defense during the Kennedy and Johnson administrations. The film revisits the Second World War, the Cuban Missile Crisis and Vietnam through interviews and archival material. It is not a history of social-media algorithms, nor does it demonstrate that contemporary online systems descended directly from the Pentagon. Its relevance is the problem of treating a quantitative representation as though it were the problem itself. [1]

A study by the US research organisation CNA identifies three particularly significant systems associated with McNamara-era operations assessment: enemy body counts, the Hamlet Evaluation System and Southeast Asia Analysis Reports. The designers sought comparable indicators of counterinsurgency progress. But the data could not adequately reflect the political legitimacy, local allegiances, historical context and changing conditions that shaped the conflict. The CNA account concludes that these assessments proved unreliable as measures of progress. That is a historical finding about a particular institutional system, not a rejection of statistics in military affairs. [2]

The distinction between *measurement of an action* and *measurement of a desired outcome* is fundamental. Counting casualties can describe one military variable without answering whether a government is gaining durable legitimacy. Similarly, counting how long a video remains on screen may help predict a platform interaction without establishing whether it makes viewers better informed. A highly accurate prediction of clicking or watching is not the same as an ethically or socially satisfactory result. Nor does a failure in one dimension imply that all models are useless.

A second parallel is organisational. McNamara's systems were embedded in hierarchies that chose which information reached commanders and political leaders. Contemporary feeds and decision systems are embedded in firms and institutions that choose objectives, constraints, training data and reporting formats. In both cases, the central political question is where responsibility sits when a model appears more definite than the underlying uncertainty. The analogy is bounded: one system assesses an armed conflict within a state hierarchy; the other distributes information within commercial and social networks. Their historical causes, consequences and degrees of coercion differ radically.

The lasting problem is the seduction of an apparently complete dashboard. Its categories may be reproducible while its interpretation remains wrong. Under complex social conditions, a model can be technologically sophisticated and institutionally misleading at the same time.

## 3. Cybernetics, statistics and the migration of decision systems

The twentieth-century development of computers allowed measured events to be stored, compared and processed at scales not feasible for manual administrations. Banks used credit models, insurers calculated risks, factories scheduled production and public agencies classified populations. Search engines later made ranking central to finding information; online advertising made targeting and measurement central to the financing of internet services. Recommender systems combined these streams with models of individual interaction.

The transition was not simply from analogue repression to digital freedom or from freedom to universal surveillance. Earlier social orders already shaped information through editors, schools, churches, political parties, family networks and state broadcasting. What changed was the granularity of measurement, the speed of feedback, the capacity to update an individual's informational environment and the commercial value of knowing how that person responds. The shift must be investigated rather than treated as automatically emancipatory or oppressive.

The difference between a rule-based program and a machine-learning recommender matters. A deterministic rule may display the latest article in reverse chronological order; a learned model estimates the probability of a viewer engaging with several candidate articles. Both are algorithms, but they generate different incentives and explanations. The chronological rule is transparent at one level, although publication frequency still affects visibility. A recommendation system may discover interests more effectively, although the reasons for a particular ranking can be difficult to inspect. Neither design guarantees viewpoint diversity, accuracy, welfare or neutrality.

This development also transformed the politics of classification. When a machine uses an employment history to recommend an applicant, it inherits both the useful information and possible historic inequalities in the data. When a platform models past watch time, it may reproduce habits that a user would not deliberately choose as long-run goals. Statistical correlation becomes an input into allocation, while allocation changes the next observations. The system must therefore be evaluated as a dynamic institution, not merely a static prediction.

## 4. How recommendation systems organise the visible world

A common architecture for large recommender systems separates *candidate generation* from *ranking*. A 2016 technical paper by Google engineers Paul Covington, Jay Adams and Emre Sargin described a deep-learning YouTube recommender with these two stages. The first estimates which videos may interest the user from a very large catalogue; the second ranks the smaller candidate set. The disclosure is a documented historical architecture, not a claim that every current YouTube decision still follows exactly the 2016 design. [3]

TikTok's user documentation provides a complementary description of today's recommendation signals. The company identifies interactions such as watched, skipped, liked, shared or commented content; information about the video, including sound and hashtags; and user or device context. It says interactions and watching time are generally more influential than certain other factors for many users. This is the platform's public account, not an independent audit of its weights or business objectives. The public feed remains personalised, and an individual user's feed cannot be reduced to a fixed formula known outside the company. [4]

The economic loop begins when a user produces signals, but the system's operating goals matter. Prediction may be optimised for watch time, expected interest, relevance, safety, freshness, customer experience or a combination of metrics. The exact optimisation scheme is proprietary and changes over time. It is therefore inaccurate to say that all social platforms optimise only anger or addiction. It is equally inaccurate to pretend that the selection of measurable goals has no social effects.

The source of power is partly a selection bottleneck. An immense catalogue is practically inaccessible without intermediaries. Search rankings, subscriptions, recommendations and social ties each filter information. A designer controls not only the algorithm but also permitted content, enforcement rules, advertising policy, creator monetisation and the experimental process used to test changes. The relevant institution consists of the entire system of choices, not merely its mathematical model.

## 5. The economics of attention: what is actually being sold?

Attention is limited by waking hours, competing obligations and human capacity. Digital content, by contrast, can be copied and distributed at low marginal cost. Platforms capable of organising a large audience may sell targeted advertising, charge subscriptions, intermediate purchases or earn from associated services. Their business models differ, but the distribution of attention is economically consequential across them.

It is tempting to treat every extra minute online as profit for the platform. The real mechanism is more complex: ad load, advertising prices, advertiser demand, user retention, content cost, moderation, infrastructure, commerce and subscription choices all affect revenue. Some content that raises short-run engagement can damage advertiser confidence or regulatory standing. Some experiences that protect user trust may yield greater long-run value. The commercial incentive is a dynamic optimisation problem, not a single dial labelled outrage.

The distribution of value is uneven. A platform owns its ranking infrastructure and the rules of participation. A creator may invest time and equipment without controlling distribution. Advertisers purchase probabilistic access to audiences; consumers receive entertainment, information and services while surrendering time and sometimes personal data. An emerging creator can suddenly gain a national audience, but the same arrangement can produce dependence on unpredictable changes in the feed. These are forms of market power that can exist even when users pay no monetary subscription fee.

The concept of a *two-sided market* is useful but incomplete. Platform value often comes from several interacting groups: users, creators, advertisers, merchants, developers and payment providers. The economic question is who can switch platforms, who can take data or audiences away, whether rules are understandable and whether a creator's bargaining power survives a change in ranking. Standard competition measures such as prices alone may not capture the cost of diminished visibility or the concentration of access to audiences.

Algorithmic selection also creates an externality problem. The owner of a feed may measure clicks and sales directly while the effects on public debate, sleep or institutional trust appear elsewhere. These consequences are difficult to price and sometimes difficult to attribute. Regulation must address that evidence gap without assuming that every regrettable social event was caused by a profitable algorithm.

## 6. From long-form video to short-form cultural grammar

TikTok, Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts and comparable services made vertical, mobile-native and rapidly editable video formats central to digital culture. The transformation is not merely that videos became shorter. Music, captions, templates, remixes, repeated gestures, camera movements and immediate access to the next clip create a recognisable grammar. A segment can circulate without its original surrounding context, and the same visual joke or heroic montage can be reused across countries and political communities.

Short-form production has lowered barriers to creative expression. A small business can demonstrate a service without a production studio; an artist can reach a dispersed audience; a student can learn a technique in an accessible sequence; a civilian can document an abuse. Yet highly shareable formats also encourage compression. A twenty-second excerpt can communicate outrage or admiration efficiently while excluding qualifying evidence. That is a property of the communicative environment, not proof that every short video misleads.

The history of technology adoption is crucial. Bolsonaro's 2018 digital mobilisation cannot be described as an election driven by Instagram Reels or YouTube Shorts, which became prominent later. WhatsApp, Facebook, YouTube, images and shareable video clips were important then. The 2022 ecology was different: formats produced on TikTok or Kwai could be downloaded and redistributed inside WhatsApp groups. The same trend can therefore combine algorithmic discovery at one site with voluntary forwarding through an interpersonal messaging network elsewhere. [11][14]

Different layers of circulation have different mechanisms. A ranking system determines exposure within a feed; a group administrator may shape participation; a peer chooses to forward a message; a person with social authority may interpret it; a broadcaster may amplify the controversy. Calling the entire chain 'the algorithm' conceals the actors and causal steps that must be evaluated separately.

## 7. Dopamine, reward prediction and the scientific limits of an easy metaphor

In popular discussion, dopamine is often presented as a chemical pleasure button that short videos manipulate until the brain is damaged. This description is misleading. Neuroscientific work associated with Wolfram Schultz and colleagues examines dopamine neurons as signals related to rewards and deviations from expected rewards. Those findings are part of a larger field involving learning, motivation, cognition, context and multiple neurotransmitter systems. They cannot be translated into the claim that a particular genre of video depletes a person's dopamine or causes permanent harm. [9]

The behavioural mechanisms are nevertheless serious research questions. Novelty, variable rewards, uncertain timing, social comparison and frictionless access can encourage repeated checking. An individual's behaviour depends on stress, social ties, mood, available alternatives and pre-existing tendencies. Some users may develop problematic patterns of use; others engage intentionally and experience benefits. To establish a causal effect, researchers must separate exposure from self-selection, account for baseline difficulties and measure outcomes over an appropriate period.

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis in *Psychological Bulletin* synthesised 71 studies with 98,299 participants. It reported associations between greater short-form video use and poorer cognitive indicators, with correlations of approximately -0.38 for attention and -0.41 for inhibitory control; mental-health associations were generally weaker and heterogeneous. These are effect-size correlations across a literature with substantial methodological variation, not probabilities of mental illness, reductions in IQ or estimates of permanent damage. Many included studies were observational; reverse causality remains possible. [8]

A plausible policy concern is not the existence of dopamine but the design of an environment that makes stopping difficult, especially for vulnerable users. A service can offer user controls while implementing default experiences that remain effortless to continue. The European Commission's February 2026 *preliminary* findings against TikTok addressed infinite scroll, autoplay, notifications and personalised recommendation in relation to possible failures to assess and mitigate risks under the Digital Services Act. The findings were provisional; they do not establish a final legal judgment or a universal medical outcome. [20]

The empirical agenda should look beyond screen-time totals. It should ask what the user watched, why, with whom, at what time, with what alternatives, and with what measured effect on sleep, attention, work or relationships. 'Hours online' is a useful exposure variable, but it is not itself a diagnosis.

## 8. Music, fast cuts and cinematic intensity

A striking feature of short-form culture is the use of a rapid sequence of editing cues to give ordinary actions the expressive force of cinema: heavy bass, accelerated electronic music, phonk, funk or trap, slow motion, first-person camera, abrupt cuts, synchronized subtitles and sharply contrasting images. These choices can heighten arousal, direct attention or give a scene a particular emotional interpretation. Their effects depend on the viewer, prior knowledge, context and social meaning.

A montage of a person entering a room may portray confidence; the same gesture with different music can become comedy or menace. The edit changes the narrative frame without changing the underlying event. This is especially consequential when footage is detached from its date or location. The soundtrack can produce an impression of triumph even when the event had severe human consequences. In political conflicts, cinematic editing may make the moment of confrontation seem like the entire history of a dispute.

There is no scientific basis for claiming that one musical genre, a high beat rate or a fast cut automatically produces aggression or physical courage in young viewers. The more defensible hypothesis is that audiovisual framing can make certain performances salient, memorable and imitable under particular circumstances. The effect must be measured rather than inferred from the researcher's emotional reaction to a video.

The same techniques may perform socially beneficial functions: make complex scientific ideas accessible, allow witnesses to communicate danger, facilitate artistic experimentation, or offer humour and connection. Their dual use is precisely why banning an aesthetic style would be a poor substitute for studying the underlying behaviours and incentives.

## 9. The camera as a second audience: acting while anticipating visibility

In Erving Goffman's sociology, social life involves performances and the management of impressions before others. Cameras did not invent that human tendency. Networked platforms, however, make the audience potentially persistent, distant and difficult to predict. An individual can act before people physically present and simultaneously imagine a much larger audience that may view an edited recording later.

This changes the role of the camera. At one level it documents an action; at another it enables a person to address a later audience; at a third it may become part of the reason the action is performed. Distinguishing these situations matters. A protester may film police violence for accountability. A performer may stage a harmless gesture to entertain friends. An offender may seek notoriety. The existence of a recording alone does not establish which motivation applies.

The mechanisms are social as well as technical. Comments, view counts and imitation offer imperfect information about group esteem. A creator may anticipate future recognition and adapt a performance; viewers may reproduce a gesture because it signals membership in a community. These possibilities are best described as *audience-conditioned action* rather than a claim that a platform removes agency. Agency remains active precisely because participants interpret and use the system.

An experiment by Lauren Sherman and colleagues presented 32 adolescents with a simulated Instagram-like interface while using brain imaging. Participants were more inclined to endorse photographs shown as having many likes; the responses implicated social influence and neural networks related to reward and attention. The design supports a bounded mechanism of peer endorsement; it does not establish that social-media metrics cause violent behaviour or that the same effects hold across all ages. [10]

A useful conceptual distinction separates recognition, material reward and reputational obligation. The first is the felt approval of peers; the second may be income or commercial opportunity; the third is the pressure to maintain a public persona. These incentives may reinforce each other, but treating them as the same phenomenon makes causal testing difficult.

## 10. Aura farming: an online idiom becomes a physical gathering

The 2026 Brazilian and Latin American phenomenon known as *aura farming* provides a revealing counterexample to claims that algorithmic culture is inherently antisocial or violent. The word 'farm' comes from gaming culture, where repeating an activity can accumulate resources or points; 'aura' refers informally to perceived presence, charisma or status. Short performances, gestures and character imitations are presented as opportunities to 'gain aura'. In 2026, videos of such performances were accompanied by documented in-person gatherings and contests in public squares. [24]

The transition from an online idiom to physical competition illustrates cultural transmission. A shared vocabulary, a repeatable visual template and the prospect of recognition can coordinate people who did not know each other before. The resulting meetings may be playful, creative and community-building. They should not be rhetorically grouped with violence simply because they also involve filming, imitation or competition for attention.

The more general question concerns the *economics of symbolic status*: who recognises a gesture as impressive, what audience validates it, whether judges or peers establish rules, and how online circulation changes the scale of recognition. The case is valuable precisely because it permits observation of performative incentives in a relatively low-risk setting. It does not prove that the same mechanisms lead from a harmless dance contest to a riot or a military trench.

A hypothesis about escalation would require observations of reward-seeking in high-risk settings, evidence that participants anticipated digital recognition, comparison with similar events lacking such exposure, and examination of alternative motives. Without those elements, cinematic similarity is not causal evidence.

## 11. Adolescence, identity and the apprenticeship problem

Adolescence is a period in which peer relationships, identity and recognition often have particular salience, but young people are not a homogeneous psychological category. The social meaning of a platform can differ sharply between a teenager seeking friendship, an adult working as a creator, a student seeking information and a person experiencing social isolation. Even within the same demographic group, prior mental health, family context, school environment and available offline relationships may change outcomes.

An environment that offers continuous comparison can intensify feelings of inadequacy in some contexts, while providing minority communities or geographically isolated users with social support in others. Research quality must therefore distinguish problematic use from ordinary participation and separate self-reported symptoms from independently measured health outcomes. Aggregate correlations in the short-video literature should not be treated as clinical diagnoses of individuals. [8]

The apprenticeship question goes further than attention. People develop skills by practising, failing, receiving feedback and assuming responsibility. When recommendations are tailored to what a user already engages with, platforms may increase satisfaction while offering fewer opportunities for deliberate encounter with unfamiliar material—depending on system design. AI tutors and recommendations can also expand discovery and learning. Whether young users become more or less capable requires longitudinal educational and behavioural evidence, not an anecdote about classroom distraction.

The social dimension is as important as cognition. A person may become expert at producing a recognisable online persona while having limited opportunities to gain professional recognition, stable income or influence in formal institutions. This can be understood as a mismatch between digital prestige and material power. The mismatch may create frustration, entrepreneurship, civic participation or disengagement: no single outcome follows.

## 12. Facebook experiments: influence has been demonstrated, total control has not

The 2012 study *A 61-million-person experiment in social influence and political mobilisation* used randomised messages displayed on Facebook around the 2010 US congressional elections. The researchers found effects on political self-expression, information seeking and real-world voting; transmission through close social ties was especially significant. The experimental result demonstrates that the design of a platform message can affect specified behaviours. It does not show that a recommendation algorithm can freely choose a person's political preference or determine an election result. [5]

In a 2014 *PNAS* experiment involving 689,003 Facebook users, researchers adjusted the prevalence of positive or negative posts displayed in feeds and studied changes in subsequent emotional expression. The reported effect was evidence of emotional contagion through an online environment. The study also prompted controversy about consent and research ethics, and its effect sizes and outcome definition limit any extrapolation to clinical mental health or mass political manipulation. [6]

Those findings must be read alongside evidence that constrains stronger claims. A 2023 *Science* study experimentally assigned consenting Facebook and Instagram users to reverse-chronological feeds rather than the default algorithmic ranking during the 2020 US election campaign. Exposure and activity changed substantially, but the study did not find significant changes in central measures of political polarisation and attitudes over its three-month observation period. This does not prove that feed design has no longer-run effects; it disproves the assumption that every substantial change in exposure must rapidly transform political opinion. [7]

The research supports a nuanced conclusion: social-media design can influence attention and particular behaviours; the magnitude, persistence, direction and social consequences are highly context-dependent. Political criticism becomes more credible, not weaker, when it respects those distinctions.

## 13. The Brazilian political case: an existing social conflict meets a new communications infrastructure

The rise of Jair Bolsonaro from a long-serving federal deputy to president of Brazil in 2018 is one of the most consequential cases for studying the interaction between political grievances and networked communication. Its causes cannot be reduced to memes. Brazil entered the election after the 2013 demonstrations, economic crisis, corruption scandals, polarised debates over institutions, the impeachment of Dilma Rousseff and contestation around political representation. A study based on the 2018 Brazilian Electoral Panel Study identifies anti-Workers' Party sentiment, conservative cultural preferences, strict law-and-order views and economic liberalism among the factors associated with Bolsonaro support. The technological environment provided mechanisms of organisation and expression; it did not manufacture all of these preferences. [13]

Bolsonaro's communication was also distinctive. Supportive communities circulated ironic images, heroic representations, excerpts from interviews, denunciations of adversaries and symbolic shorthand capable of communicating political affiliation without extended argument. A short phrase or a picture could serve simultaneously as humour, persuasion and a badge of belonging. Viktor Chagas's research on memes from the early months of the administration analyses their nationalist, ideological and rhetorical characteristics. That corpus concerns a particular period and sample, not the totality of 2018 campaign material, but it documents the political functions of images circulated among supporters. [12]

The 2018 architecture was multiplatform rather than a uniquely TikTok-driven election. In a peer-reviewed study, Victor Piaia and Marcelo Alves examined **194,710 messages in 21 pro-Bolsonaro WhatsApp groups**, collected between 5 September and 11 November 2018. A relatively small number of participants accounted for a large share of posts; links to YouTube and Facebook appeared prominently; and some organised campaign activities were identifiable. The sample describes monitored groups, not every supporter, every Brazilian WhatsApp group or the probability that an individual voted because of a message. The study's greatest contribution is to show how interpersonal forwarding and public platforms operated as an interconnected communication system. [11]

There were different reasons for participation. Some users shared content as political conviction; others sought community, opposed a particular party or followed trusted family networks. An influencer might see a market for attention while a group participant regarded a forward as civic duty. Political mobilisation thus involved both genuine supporters and strategically organised activity; the two are not mutually exclusive. Evidence of some coordinated accounts does not prove that all supporters were manipulated, and evidence of genuine convictions does not exclude organised amplification.

It is tempting to state that Bolsonaro could never have reached national prominence without memes and algorithms. That is an unobserved counterfactual. A defensible conclusion is narrower and more informative: digital networks plausibly changed the **speed, coordination, reach and cost** of the mobilisation, while the exact electoral contribution cannot be isolated from simultaneous social and institutional conditions without a credible identification strategy. The comparison with political movements in other countries should therefore ask which features are specific to Brazil and which are more general properties of digital communication.

## 14. From 2018 to 2022: cross-platform video and the persistence of an identity

Between the elections of 2018 and 2022, short-form video became a more prominent part of the Brazilian communication ecology. Clips from podcasts, speeches, interviews and street encounters travelled across platforms; sound and subtitles reframed fragments for new audiences. A single recording could serve as primary political material, commentary, parody or evidence depending on who cut, titled or distributed it. The intermediary between an original event and its final audience was frequently a sequence of creators, recommenders and group members.

An investigation by Agência Pública using data from the Eleições Sem Fake project at the Federal University of Minas Gerais examined highly interactive videos circulating in pro-Bolsonaro WhatsApp groups during July and August 2022. It reported that 41.3% of the sampled high-interaction videos originated on TikTok or Kwai; of 133 such videos examined, 108 originated on TikTok, 24 on Kwai and at least one carried marks from both applications. These are **sample-dependent findings**, not percentages of all election videos, all Brazilian WhatsApp traffic or causal effects on voting. The original reporting also identified misleading claims in some items. [14]

The result reveals an important division of labour. A video can be discovered through ranking on one platform and then distributed through WhatsApp by people who know one another. That second stage may draw authority from interpersonal trust rather than from a feed's hidden formula. A shared political meme can also travel in the opposite direction, from a messaging group to a public creator's video. The resulting communication network does not have one central decision-maker.

Political movements opposed to Bolsonaro also used memes, videos, influencers and messaging networks. Their content, organisational models and audiences differed, but no claim that digital politics is exclusive to a particular ideological position is supported by the underlying technology. A comparative study would need examine matched samples from multiple camps rather than simply accumulating the most striking examples from one.

The long-run political consequence is the survival of identity outside formal election periods. A set of phrases, symbols and shared histories can persist across changes in parties and public office. This persistence may mobilise lawful participation, protest or other activities. Understanding which outcomes occurred requires attention to actual messages and institutions, not the presumption that a digital identity is inherently undemocratic or inherently emancipatory.

## 15. January 2023 in Brasília: communication, coordination and the boundary of attribution

The attacks on the institutions of Brazil's three branches of government on 8 January 2023 make it necessary to examine the passage from online claims and coordination to a physical event. The relevant mechanisms include circulation of narratives contesting the electoral result, mobilisation logistics, transport, social ties, calls for action and the decisions of particular participants. These should not be collapsed into a claim that a recommendation algorithm directly caused the attacks.

InternetLab's report on Telegram material associated with the period collected **592,644 publications between 1 and 8 January 2023**, including text, images, audio and video. The researchers analysed repeated messages, important imagery and patterns of communications and organisation. The corpus is substantial but has clear boundaries: it is not the universe of private conversations, not an individual-level map of motives, and not proof of criminal participation by anyone who was present in a monitored group. [15]

A robust causal analysis needs to distinguish at least five states: encountering a claim, endorsing it, forwarding it, coordinating an activity and engaging in that activity. Each transition requires different observations. Sharing false information is not equivalent to participating in an assault, and a large digital volume does not establish a numerical conversion rate from posts to physical participants. Nevertheless, the content of communications can be evidence about the organisation and rhetoric of a movement, especially when correlated with dated actions, transport arrangements or legal findings.

The case also reveals why content moderation is not the same as political governance. Platforms can remove messages, limit virality or close accounts, but states retain responsibility for lawful policing and institutional accountability. Aggressive moderation can limit incitement while creating risks of overreach and errors. Transparent rules, due process and independent assessment are necessary because the systems themselves hold power over what can be said and seen.

## 16. France in 2023: when events are organised, filmed and remediated

The French disturbances following the killing of Nahel Merzouk by a police officer in Nanterre on 27 June 2023 provide a distinct case. Their proximate trigger, history of police-community relations, spatial inequalities and political context cannot be replaced by a theory of TikTok or 'dopamine'. The question is how digital communication affected the **form, timing, visibility and coordination** of unrest after the event occurred.

A 2024 French Senate inquiry examined the unrest and the role of platforms. It described different roles for public networks, interpersonal messaging and location-aware functions. According to figures from a sample examined in a joint inspectorate report cited by the Senate, Snapchat was mentioned with a frequency of **36%**, compared with X at 5%, TikTok 4%, Instagram 2%, Telegram 2%, WhatsApp 1% and Facebook 0.6%. These are rates of mention in a particular sample. They are not a distribution that sums to 100%, platform market shares or the proportions of all rioters using each service. The relative prominence of Snapchat is particularly important: the most relevant logistics platform in a specific event need not be the platform whose content is most visible to distant observers. The Senate described an initial phase of circulation of video and calls to gather, followed by more varied logistical uses of messaging services, including features associated with Snapchat. Platform representatives reported different timelines of activity. The institution also considered imitation, visibility and competition for online attention, while maintaining that social circumstances and urban conditions mattered. This is much more specific than the assertion that all unrest is 'cinematic' because young people enjoy edits. [16]

The idea of performance nevertheless deserves testing. A participant may record an event to expose abuse, preserve evidence, mobilise solidarity or display daring. For a subset of activities, anticipated audience response may become part of the motive. Proving that requires interviews, communication records, sequence analysis and comparison with situations without comparable exposure. It is not enough to find a dramatic video after the fact.

The French and Brazilian cases illuminate different pathways. One concerns long-term political identity followed by an institutional assault; the other concerns a sudden protest and unrest triggered by a police killing. Both involved networked communication, but their histories and mechanisms remain distinct. Treating them as equivalent expressions of an algorithmic tendency would ignore the very social structures that an adequate explanation must recover.

## 17. International comparison: the same devices, different social mechanisms

The international comparison should not assemble striking videos into a catalogue of anxiety. A case has explanatory value when its institutional context, the source of a grievance, the relevant platform mechanisms and the observed outcome are made explicit.

```map
title: Different transmission mechanisms across researched settings
Brazil | Election-oriented memes, interpersonal forwarding, political identity and separately documented mobilisation | WhatsApp studies from 2018 and 2022; Telegram research on January 2023
France | Police killing followed by unrest, public video circulation and logistical messaging | Senate 2024 inquiry; platform-use observations have sample limitations
Ukraine | War documentation, first-person testimony, advocacy and platform-native entertainment forms | Scholarly analysis of 97 Ukrainian creator videos; no proven causal recruitment rate
United States | Randomised platform interventions and measured political or emotional responses | Facebook experiments from 2012 and 2014; 2023 chronological-feed counterevidence
```

This geographic comparison does not imply a route connecting the countries or that all citizens within a country share a single response. It shows why a useful cross-national research design must control for the type of event and the role of the platform. The same function—video sharing—can support electoral persuasion, eyewitness documentation, public accountability, fundraising or unlawful coordination, depending on the situation.

Five types of evidence should be kept separate. Documentary evidence establishes that a message or video existed. Platform data establish that particular interactions occurred, subject to access limits. Interviews describe motives but may suffer recall or social-desirability bias. Experiments can identify effects under specified interventions. Administrative or judicial records may document outcomes but do not by themselves attribute their causes to an algorithm. A powerful story built from only one of those categories is often inadequate as an explanation of large-scale social change.

## 18. Ukraine: witnessing, propaganda and the first-person perspective of war

The Russian invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 created an environment in which civilians, soldiers, journalists, institutions and propagandists produced and circulated images of conflict. Mobile recording and social platforms offered opportunities to show previously inaccessible aspects of daily survival, evacuation and combat. Those same images could be extracted from context, staged for communication, manipulated or used to construct selective narratives. Authentication and dating are central because a visceral first-person recording may still be misleading about place, timing or responsibility.

Tom Divon and Moa Eriksson Krutrök's 2025 study of **97 TikTok videos from Ukrainian creators** identifies three styles: point-of-view testimony, memes used to communicate wartime experiences, and playful political entertainment associated with engagement and fundraising. The authors examine how platform-native forms mediate conflict visibility and can support civic engagement. Their sample concerns selected creators and videos, not all wartime communication, all combatants or recruitment outcomes. [17]

Research on Ukrainian soldiers' TikTok self-representation likewise shows how soldiers perform national and gender identities through audiovisual conventions. The proximity of the mobile camera can make distant combat seem psychologically immediate, yet a clip rarely conveys the full logistical, ethical or human cost of the underlying events. A montage featuring music and a confident camera perspective may make a soldier appear heroic without measuring the viewer's subsequent willingness to enlist. [18]

The most difficult claim is that cinematic POV clips **cause young people to join the war**. Evidence of inspiration or sympathetic comments does not establish a recruitment effect. That stronger hypothesis would require recruitment data, interviews with new entrants, observed exposure histories and comparison groups, while controlling for patriotism, conscription, family links, economic pressures and military mobilisation policy. The question is legitimate; the answer is not provided by virality alone.

War communication also exposes a moral asymmetry. Some images may document atrocities or help civilians receive assistance; others can glorify harm or create operational security risks. The same distribution system that supports testimony can spread propaganda and false imagery. Treating every military clip as dangerous would undermine documentation; treating every spectacular clip as authentic would reward manipulation.

## 19. From a video of war to algorithm-assisted decisions in war

Images of trenches are only one interface between algorithms and conflict. Militaries also employ computational systems for logistics, sensor fusion, image classification, threat assessment, simulation and decision support. Their consequences differ in scale and responsibility from a social feed. A system that prioritises possible targets or signals may influence life-and-death decisions even when a human formally retains the final command.

The distinction between a tool that *claims* a capability, one that *demonstrates* it in a bounded test, one that is *deployed*, and one that is *scaled and independently effective* is indispensable. A promotional video of a drone does not establish operational autonomy or discrimination between civilian and military targets. The same caution applies to claims of artificial intelligence accurately identifying people in real-time conflict. Responsible investigation needs dated documents, independent tests, operational conditions and legal context.

The second risk is compressed decision time. Systems that deliver more alerts and faster recommendations may improve defence while increasing pressure on operators to defer to automated classifications. Imperfect training data, adversarial deception, uncertain attribution and unclear accountability can produce severe errors. Speed may be useful precisely when there is time to verify; reducing time without improving verification may worsen decisions.

This returns to McNamara without collapsing history. The Vietnam-era problem involved interpreting quantitative indicators of a broad political-military process. Current automated military systems may act within technical chains whose inputs and intermediate decisions are opaque to participants. The shared institutional vulnerability is overconfidence in a representation that does not incorporate the whole context. The technologies and legal stakes differ, but both require meaningful human responsibility and independent auditing.

## 20. Algorithms beyond the feed: work, income, credit and public administration

The most consequential algorithm in an individual's life may never recommend a video. A credit-scoring system can affect access to finance; recruitment software can filter applicants; automated scheduling can alter shifts; fraud detection can interrupt a payment; navigation software can determine which neighbourhood receives traffic. Such systems allocate opportunities, time and resources. Their impact on society may be more immediate than their cultural visibility suggests.

The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development reported in December 2025 an employer survey on algorithmic management across several advanced economies. It found at least one such tool reported by **90% of managers surveyed in the United States**, compared with an average of **79% in the four surveyed European countries** and **40% in Japan**. Those values describe firms represented by a particular employer survey and the adoption of at least one management tool. They are not the proportion of workers fully managed by autonomous AI, nor a measure of how many decisions were automated without human supervision. Respondents reported perceived benefits and concerns about accountability, transparency and worker health. [19]

The economic mechanism differs from advertising. A manager may seek more consistent allocation of tasks, fraud control or productivity. Yet a worker can face a rating whose data sources and correction routes are not visible. When employers share external assessment tools, errors or biases can spread across multiple hiring decisions. When every worker is evaluated by a narrow measure, people may focus on that measure even at the expense of service quality or safety.

The governing questions are practical: can the affected person find out that automation was used, challenge an error, obtain a human review, inspect relevant evidence and obtain a remedy? Is the model accurate across the populations it serves? Are performance gains verified or merely believed by managers? These rights and tests are often more informative than a general promise of ethical AI.

The employment and public administration cases also reveal a limit to the word *control*. A ranking system need not control someone's beliefs to influence their access to an interview, a bank account or a public service. The mechanisms of power can be distributive, procedural and material rather than psychological.

## 21. Peter Thiel, René Girard and the political theology of technological power

Peter Thiel's 2025–2026 discussions of the Antichrist belong in the intellectual history of contemporary technological power, not as evidence that he secretly coordinates the recommendations on social platforms. Reporters documented private lectures in San Francisco in 2025 and Rome in March 2026 addressing apocalyptic theology, the danger of a global political authority and fears about attempts to prevent catastrophic technologies. Contemporary reporting also records strong criticism of his interpretation by religious thinkers. These positions are attributed accounts of his statements, not independently verified prophecies or an agreed philosophical framework. [22][23]

A more fruitful connection is René Girard's account of mimetic desire and rivalry, an intellectual influence on Thiel. The theory suggests that desires and competitive relations can be mediated by models supplied by others. Applied to digital platforms, it raises questions about imitation, competition for prestige and escalation when groups share an audience. That is an interpretation, not an established causal law of algorithms. The presence of Girard's influence on a technology investor does not prove that TikTok or Facebook was engineered according to mimetic theory.

Thiel's concerns also expose a governance paradox. A rhetoric of existential danger can justify greater central control; a rhetoric of limitless innovation can justify strong private discretion. Neither position resolves who has legitimate authority, who bears risk, and what forms of evidence should constrain a decision. These questions intersect with accelerationist debates about technological speed and state power, but do not reduce to an opposition between progress and fear.

Guy Debord's concept of spectacle and Erving Goffman's work on social performance offer additional lenses. They can help interpret why a recorded conflict becomes publicly meaningful or why individuals anticipate being seen. Their theories should not be mistaken for proof that an audience's reaction caused a particular political or violent event. Empirical research must remain the authority for causal claims.

## 22. What regulators can govern without pretending to govern the mind

Regulatory choices range from transparency and independent risk assessment to rights of appeal, limits on certain profiling practices, age-appropriate design and specific obligations for very large platforms. These tools address different harms. A requirement to disclose why a person saw a recommendation does not give outsiders the ability to reproduce all ranking decisions. A restriction on autoplay may reduce frictionless consumption while affecting accessibility or user convenience. A moderation intervention against explicit incitement must be distinguished from a restriction on lawful dissent.

The European Commission's February 2026 preliminary findings about TikTok's interface referred to infinite scroll, autoplay, push notifications and high personalisation under the Digital Services Act. The document is a dated regulatory allegation with detailed risk concerns; TikTok retained the right to respond, and the announcement expressly did not prejudge the final outcome. Conflating preliminary findings with a final sanction would distort the evidence. [20]

In Brazil, Law 15,211 of 2025, the *ECA Digital*, became effective on **17 March 2026**, alongside institutional changes affecting the national data protection authority. The framework establishes obligations concerning the protection of minors in digital environments. Its legal operation, enforcement capacity and measurable effects must be assessed over time, rather than assumed from the existence of the statute. [21]

Concentration of regulatory power creates its own risks. Large corporations may be better able to finance complex compliance, while small entrants may face barriers. Governments can invoke safety to impose excessive surveillance or suppress opposition. Effective governance therefore requires independent courts, proportionate obligations, published methodologies, participation by affected groups and reviewable decisions. Auditing a system should not become a licence to monitor every private conversation.

A workable framework would separate informational autonomy, market contestability, labour rights, child protection, public accountability and operational security. The appropriate institutions and evidence differ across those domains. The public interest lies neither in a ban on calculation nor in automatic deference to private optimisation, but in making consequential choices contestable.

## 23. Three futures and the conditions that could falsify them

The next stage of algorithmic society cannot be inferred from a straight-line projection of watch time or model capability. Three interpretations deserve attention.

**Diffused assistance and pluralism.** Lower-cost models and transparent interfaces may expand access to education, public information, creative tools and productive capabilities. Users can choose different ranking methods, creators can switch distribution channels and workers can challenge automated decisions. Supporting evidence would include broad entry, affordable alternatives, measurable learning gains, lower switching costs and improvements in due process. The scenario weakens if infrastructure concentration rises and affected people cannot escape opaque allocation.

**Concentrated selection and dependency.** A small number of infrastructure providers may control important gateways to information, work and economic participation. Platform rules then become quasi-institutional conditions for participation. Evidence would include durable concentration, declining interoperability, unstable creator income, inability to challenge consequential rankings and observed reductions in independent discovery. The hypothesis weakens if open standards, market entry and effective rights of contestation become widespread.

**Fragmented and securitised information environments.** States may intensify control over data, platforms and AI systems because of military, security and sovereignty concerns. Different populations could experience separate informational environments under distinct regulatory and technical regimes. Evidence would include expanding cross-border restrictions, state-specific model access, content controls and verification burdens. The hypothesis weakens when international interoperability, accountable governance and independent exchange remain robust.

These paths can coexist: education may become more accessible while employment algorithms become more opaque; content moderation may improve safety while state surveillance grows elsewhere. No numerical probability is assigned because the relevant causal model and uncertainty ranges are not established. The future of algorithmic power is a question of institutional choices, not an inevitable consequence of faster computation.

## 24. What would count as evidence of an algorithmically governed society?

The most demanding test is not whether billions of people use software. It is whether consequential social outcomes depend on system-level decisions about measurement, ranking and allocation, and whether the affected population can inspect or challenge those decisions. A robust research agenda would examine repeated interventions, compare outcomes with appropriate counterfactuals, and separate platform effects from pre-existing social preferences.

| Mechanism | Appropriate evidence | Strongest competing explanation | Test that would weaken the claim |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Recommendation changes political attitudes | Randomised exposure with comparable groups and repeated attitude measures | Users choose content because they already hold the view | No difference after verified exposure changes over adequate periods |
| Short-form media harm attention | Longitudinal or controlled study with reliable cognitive outcomes | People with existing concentration difficulties seek more short videos | Apparent effects disappear after baseline and confounder control |
| Public filming encourages risky performance | Dated actions, testimony on anticipated audience and comparison events | Existing peer competition or local conflict drives the act | Equivalent rates when audience incentives are absent |
| Memes organise political communities | Traceable circulation, identity surveys and coordinated-action evidence | Prior political identities explain the sharing | Network changes without matching identity or mobilisation changes |
| War videos cause recruitment | Recruit-level exposure and motives linked to administrative recruitment data | Patriotism, legal mobilisation and material incentives | Recruitment unchanged among appropriately matched exposed groups |
| Automated work decisions reproduce inequality | Audited selection outcomes, error correction and group-specific outcomes | Structural inequalities existing before algorithm adoption | Equalisation of outcomes after controlled adoption and correction |

The historical continuity reaches back to the dilemma illuminated by McNamara: a system can become extraordinarily good at measuring what it has been instructed to count while being much less capable of representing the full human problem. The new difference is that metrics increasingly operate inside feedback systems that expose, reward and allocate social opportunities before the next decision is made.

Algorithmic infrastructures have not abolished politics, desire, class, institutions or culture. They provide environments through which all of these are increasingly expressed and contested. Their significance lies in the conjunction of calculation with human expectations: people know that other people, machines and markets may see their actions, and some adjust accordingly. The crucial public question is therefore **who may define the criteria by which social life becomes visible, valuable and consequential—and who may refuse or contest those criteria**.
