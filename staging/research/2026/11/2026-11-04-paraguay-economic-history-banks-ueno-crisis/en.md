# Paraguay's economic formation, banking system and the Ueno rupture of 2026

Paraguay entered November 2026 with a combination that stood out in South America: strong growth, relatively low inflation, substantial international reserves and sovereign investment-grade ratings from two major agencies. Yet on 2 November its central bank decided to resolve a major digital lender whose solvency ratio had fallen below half the legal minimum. Frozen account access, large public deposits and past commercial links between political figures and bank controllers transformed a prudential event into a financial, social and political crisis. [15][23][25][26]

The apparent contradiction has a long history: an independent state that initially controlled land and trade; devastating wars; reconstruction through concentrated property; an authoritarian regime that built infrastructure through Brazilian integration; financial opening followed by repeated bank failures in 1995–2003; and later reforms involving the guaraní, bank supervision, deposit insurance and inflation targeting. The question is not whether one unchanged economic model survived, but which vulnerabilities persisted and which institutions gained capacity.

**Information cutoff:** historical developments through October 2026 and verified events of 2–4 November 2026. Forecasts are not final results, criminal allegations are not convictions and Ueno's insolvency is not treated as proof of system-wide insolvency.

## 1. Before colonization and Guaraní economic organization

Paraguay's economy predates the national state. Guaraní communities combined horticulture, especially cassava and maize, with fishing, hunting, gathering, mobility and reciprocal exchange. Access to land was not entirely structured around individually alienable property: kinship, cultivation rights and community obligations shaped labor and food distribution. The Spanish foundation of Asunción in 1537 introduced encomienda, coercive labor relations and new demands for surplus. Without the silver deposits that made Upper Peru a major colonial revenue center, inland Paraguay had a more peripheral place in the Spanish imperial fiscal economy. Farming, ranching and commerce along the Paraguay and Paraná rivers became central.

Yerba mate, tobacco, timber, hides and cattle shaped regional trade. Producers depended on merchants, waterways, taxes and permissions rather than operating in an unrestricted market. Seventeenth- and eighteenth-century Jesuit missions organized agriculture, livestock, crafts and trade within communities governed by missionaries, combining forms of indigenous protection with discipline and subordination. The expulsion of the Jesuits in 1767 changed land and labor control but did not immediately establish a modern capitalist countryside. Rural inequality and overlapping property arrangements survived into independence. [1][2]

## 2. Independence in 1811 and José Gaspar Rodríguez de Francia's experiment

Independence in 1811 did not immediately produce an economy open to international capital. Under José Gaspar Rodríguez de Francia, who concentrated power from 1814 to 1840, Paraguay sought autonomy against Buenos Aires, Brazil and commercial powers. Restrictions on navigation and external trade, surveillance of merchants and state appropriation of property established an unusually centralized political economy. Small farming, ranching and public land remained productive anchors, while revenues and investment decisions depended on an interventionist state without a deep capital market.

Autarky did not mean modern industrial self-sufficiency. The country limited selected external financial dependencies but also restricted access to imported technology, scale and specialization. Straight comparisons with liberal commodity exporters ignore earlier demographic and geographic differences. The enduring institutional consequence was a strong executive capable of controlling land, circulation and trade but not a pluralist arrangement for checking executive decisions. [1][2][3]

## 3. Carlos Antonio López: state modernization, railways and productive capacity

After 1840, Carlos Antonio López pursued greater external trade and state-led investment. Transport works, shipyards, metallurgical facilities and the early railway symbolized modernization from above. Foreign technicians and equipment supported strategic projects. The increased capacity for construction and defense should not be mistaken for economy-wide industrialization: the domestic consumer market was small, employment remained rural and river navigation and foreign technology remained critical.

Public investment pursued sovereignty, revenue collection, transport and military supply, not simply commercial profitability. This increased selected state capabilities while concentrating investment decisions and limiting independent scrutiny. Francisco Solano López inherited those institutions amid escalating regional disputes. Claims that Paraguay was destined to become a major industrial power absent the ensuing war should distinguish documented facilities from speculative counterfactuals. [2][3][4]

## 4. 1864–1870: the Triple Alliance War destroys the economic base

The War of the Triple Alliance against Brazil, Argentina and Uruguay was a demographic, territorial, fiscal and productive break. Mobilization consumed labor, livestock, transport capacity and infrastructure; devastation cut civilian production, while deaths and displacement transformed households and labor relations. Estimates of mortality differ widely because prewar and postwar records are incomplete and contested. No single death toll is needed to establish the scale of the economic catastrophe: the state lost taxable resources, administrative reach and the means to fund reconstruction.

Explanations blaming the conflict exclusively on British interests, or exclusively on Francisco Solano López's intentions, compress border disputes, river access, state formation and competing regional military objectives into one cause. The consequences are better evidenced: destroyed assets, changed land ownership, stronger dependence on neighboring markets and lost human capital. The war ended an existing modernization process, but no source can prove the alternative peacetime development path. [2][3][4]

## 5. Reconstruction, foreign borrowing and land privatization after 1870

The postwar state needed funds to rebuild administrative capacity and honor obligations while its demographic and tax base had collapsed. External loans negotiated in the early 1870s connected Paraguay to international credit networks on unfavorable terms. Weak controls, high borrowing costs and disputes over funds actually received reduced the development benefits. Emergency taxes, monetary changes and asset sales sometimes secured immediate revenue at the expense of future capacity. [3][5]

Large sales of public land, especially in the later nineteenth century, transferred extensive areas to companies, investors and local elites. They raised state revenue and expanded private property, but did not create egalitarian agricultural ownership. Peasant families without secure title faced exclusion or unequal tenancy and labor arrangements. Timber and yerba-mate operations came to involve large estates and coercive working conditions. Land concentration became a lasting determinant of rural credit, productivity, informality and the allocation of future soybean export gains. [2][3][5]

## 6. The export economy: yerba mate, timber, cattle and dependence

By the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, primary products and trade services dominated the economy. Yerba mate, forests, hides and livestock generated tradable income, while imports of machinery and manufactured goods required foreign exchange. Weak rail and port infrastructure made the landlocked position expensive and exposed producers to navigation and transit policies outside Paraguay's borders.

Export specialization supported equipment purchases, employment and local infrastructure but left earnings sensitive to world prices and poorly diversified fiscal revenues. Concentrated ownership meant productivity gains could raise exports without proportionate income growth for rural workers. Urban merchants and freight intermediaries earned from cargo movements; small farmers faced insecure title and financing disadvantages. The distance between export success and broad fiscal-social capacity would recur in later decades. [2][3]

## 7. The Chaco War, economic nationalism and political reorganization

The Chaco War against Bolivia in 1932–1935 once more mobilized labor and public funds on a major scale. Military operations in a difficult, sparsely developed region carried high transport, supply and medical costs. Veterans returned to an unequal landholding system, intensifying demands for land and social reform. The experience strengthened the role of the armed forces and claims for more direct state intervention.

The 1930s and 1940s brought political turnover, experiments with agrarian and labor reform, and struggles over the organization of national resources. The 1947 civil war deepened divisions preceding Colorado Party dominance. Economic nationalism did not lead in a straight line to industrial takeoff: volatile institutions and scarce capital repeatedly constrained projects even as the state strengthened monetary and financial functions. [2][3]

## 8. 1943–1952: the guaraní and the Central Bank of Paraguay

Decree-Law 655 of 5 October 1943 established the guaraní as the national currency, with new notes entering circulation in 1944. The Banco del Paraguay was organized that year to combine monetary and banking functions. Decree-Law 18 of 25 March 1952 separated these roles and established the Banco Central del Paraguay (BCP), responsible for issuance, rediscounting, foreign-exchange transactions and credit policy direction. Its founding predated democracy and cannot be treated as equivalent to the current independent inflation-targeting regime. [6][7]

A small open economy's central bank must create domestic liquidity while managing exchange-rate shocks that can raise the domestic cost of imports and dollar debts. International reserves provide external insurance but are not an unlimited account for covering insolvent banks' losses. Solvency and liquidity, currency mismatch and related-party exposures became increasingly central to the BCP's institutional responsibilities. [6][8]

## 9. 1954–1989: Stroessner, public works, patronage and private capital

Alfredo Stroessner's dictatorship combined authoritarian political stability, roads and public works, agricultural expansion and stronger integration with Brazil. The economy was not purely state-run: private companies, exporters and bankers developed alongside politically connected actors. Public contracts, land, concessions and protection reinforced patronage. Roads reduced transport costs and linked the eastern agricultural frontier to Brazilian markets, while access to opportunities remained uneven.

Itaipu construction in the 1970s dramatically increased demand for labor, inputs, housing and services. The construction boom was temporary; electricity revenues and a large power system persisted. Growth cannot be credited exclusively to the regime's management: foreign engineering, binational agreements and broader economic circumstances were decisive. By the late 1980s, external pressures, foreign-exchange distortions and unequal distribution showed that large infrastructure did not remove the vulnerabilities of public finance and rural property. [2][8]

## 10. Itaipu, Yacyretá and the political economy of electricity

The 1973 Itaipu Treaty established a binational arrangement linking Brazil and Paraguay for decades. Hydropower production is not automatically equivalent to industrial electricity consumed inside Paraguay: installed capacity, actual generation, contractual shares, transferred electricity, revenue and local grid investment are separate variables. Generation began in the 1980s and reshaped Paraguay's external accounts. Yacyretá, shared with Argentina, introduced another large power asset and contractual dependency.

Abundant relatively low-cost electricity can attract power-intensive industry, data centers and manufacturing, but results depend on delivered tariffs, transmission capacity, skills, regulation and local supplier networks. If export revenues only finance current expenditure or are concentrated among narrow beneficiaries, physical energy capacity will not translate fully into domestic productivity. Itaipu tariff and Annex C negotiations are simultaneously economic and diplomatic because they affect payments, bargaining power and fiscal room. [2][21]

## 11. From the 1989 political transition to 1990s financial opening

After Stroessner fell in 1989, successive governments liberalized interest rates, exchange-rate arrangements and access to financial intermediation. These measures increased deposit-taking and credit extension. The number of domestic banks and finance companies rose sharply; IMF reconstructions describe almost one hundred financial intermediaries near the start of the crisis. Expansion looked compatible with modernization, but supervision lacked the capacity to assess loan quality and detect connections between borrowers and bank owners. [9][10]

The underlying balance-sheet mechanism is straightforward. Callable deposits can expand immediately, while longer-term loans, often concentrated among a small set of clients, may conceal risks until borrowers default. Opening banking markets without sound entry standards, governance and resolution rules can produce institutions that appear liquid during an upswing but become insolvent when confidence turns. Liberalization and prudential oversight must advance together, not sequentially. [9][10]

## 12. 1995–1998: the first major banking collapse of the democratic era

In May 1995 Bancopar and Banco General failed to meet clearing obligations. Interventions also reached Bancosur and Banco Mercantil. Another wave in 1997 involved Banco Unión, Ahorros Paraguayos and BIPSA; deterioration continued in 1998. Related-party lending, concentrated loan books, weak loan appraisal, inadequate supervision and regulatory forbearance were central causes. An IMF study recorded the failure of 13 banks and 35 finance companies during 1995–1998; other reconstructions count institutions over different periods and with different classifications. [9][10][11]

Without a formal deposit insurance regime, the state provided ad hoc protection. When owners did not recapitalize and the central bank provided liquidity to honor withdrawals, part of private losses moved to public balance sheets. Expectations of discretionary rescue also weakened discipline. IMF estimates of fiscal and quasi-fiscal costs vary by period and scope: around 10% of GDP for the initial crisis and more than 16% of GDP cumulatively through 2003. They should not be presented as interchangeable measurements of one event. [10][11]

## 13. 2002–2003: Argentine contagion, Banco Alemán and lost confidence

Argentina's 2001–2002 collapse reached Paraguay through trade, confidence and financial connections. Restrictions on Argentine bank withdrawals altered the expectations of Paraguayan depositors exposed to cross-border banking groups. In June 2002 the authorities intervened in Banco Alemán, a significant bank. Withdrawals spread, the guaraní depreciated and economic activity weakened. The 2003 intervention in Multibanco and closures of finance companies extended the episode. [9][10][11]

Unlike 1995, the 2002 crisis involved a pronounced external trigger superimposed on domestic vulnerabilities. Authorities faced a dilemma between keeping payments functioning, limiting bailouts and preventing insolvency from triggering indiscriminate panic. Emergency liquidity may support a sound bank facing temporary withdrawals; it cannot replace capital already lost through impaired assets. This distinction shaped the reforms of 2003 and remains central to the Ueno case in 2026. [9][10]

## 14. The 1995–2005 reforms: banking law, resolution and deposit insurance

Law 489/1995 established the modern central bank charter, while Law 861/1996 strengthened rules for banks, finance companies and other lenders. The legal framework clarified capital requirements, supervision and related-party exposures. Following the subsequent crisis, Law 2334/2003 introduced the Deposit Guarantee Fund (FGD) and a formal bank-resolution process intended to replace improvised rescues with predictable steps: intervention, control of records, identification of covered deposits, asset assessment and legally governed transfers or payouts. [8][10][12]

Deposit insurance can reduce small-depositor runs but does not fully protect every corporate or large deposit; it does not substitute for supervision before failure. In 2005 the Agencia Financiera de Desarrollo (AFD) was created as a second-tier development lender, channeling long-term funding through eligible financial institutions. The resulting system combines a central bank, prudential supervisor, deposit fund, commercial banks and development finance. Banco Nacional de Fomento's state-guaranteed liabilities operate under a different legal structure from private banks contributing to the FGD. [12][13]

## 15. The 2000s economy: soybeans, beef, agribusiness and migration

Soybean expansion transformed eastern Paraguay and deepened supply-chain links with Brazilian firms and global markets. Seeds, machinery, storage, crushing, logistics and exports became more capital intensive. Modernized beef production also strengthened international integration. Higher export productivity generated income, foreign exchange and some tax revenue, but coexisted with displacement of small farmers, land disputes and the need for productive nonfarm jobs. [2][14]

Urban commerce expanded through transport, construction, border trade, imports and services. Some re-export and informal activity depended on tax and currency differences with Brazil and Argentina. Migration could diversify household income through wages and remittances, but informality excluded many workers from contribution-based social protection. Successful export sectors do not automatically resolve land inequality, human-capital gaps or drought exposure. [14][15]

## 16. The modern BCP: autonomy, inflation targets and foreign exchange

The BCP operates monetary policy through its policy rate (TPM), liquidity operations and communication. Formal inflation targeting was adopted in 2011; the 2026 target was 3.5% with a tolerance band of plus or minus two percentage points. This is not a fixed exchange-rate promise. The guaraní floats, with foreign-exchange operations potentially smoothing disorderly markets, while policy aims to prevent oil, food or currency shocks from turning into persistent inflation. [7][16]

Transmission is imperfect: interbank rates react before deposit and loan prices, spending, investment and inflation. Dollar deposits and lending make exchange-rate balance sheets especially important. Depreciation increases the guaraní burden of dollar loans for borrowers earning local-currency income. The Superintendency of Banks, within the BCP, has a separate financial-integrity and stability function. Credible supervision requires independence from private interests, documented decisions and timely intervention even when headline inflation is low. [7][16][17]

## 17. Banco Nacional de Fomento: public credit, state deposits and risk

Banco Nacional de Fomento (BNF) is a state-owned bank providing deposit services, credit and support to productive sectors. It is not the central bank. Its liabilities have an explicit state guarantee under its institutional charter. Public ownership can widen services and support underserved sectors, but it also introduces the risks of politically directed lending, concentration of government deposits and future fiscal recapitalization. [13][18]

The BNF's experience during 1995–2002 illustrates those risks. An IMF study found a sharply deteriorating portfolio, with nonperforming loans reaching approximately 56% of loans in 2002, alongside strong dependence on public deposits. This is a historical observation, not a claim about its current balance sheet. A public bank still requires clear development mandates, professional credit underwriting, transparent subsidies and independent auditing. Ownership changes who may ultimately bear banking losses; it does not abolish them. [11][18]

## 18. AFD, CAH and the official development-finance institutions

The Agencia Financiera de Desarrollo (AFD) is predominantly a second-tier lender that supplies funding to eligible intermediaries for housing and longer-maturity productive investment. It is not equivalent to a retail bank holding millions of demand deposits. Crédito Agrícola de Habilitación (CAH) supports small producers. Selected Fondo Ganadero functions were incorporated into BNF under Law 7114/2023. Other sector-specific official financing entities have public budgets that should not be mistaken for commercial-bank assets. [18][19]

Development finance is beneficial where it is additional: viable projects receive longer-term or appropriately priced funds they would otherwise lack. Replacing readily available private lending merely subsidizes existing activity; shifting unprovisioned credit losses to taxpayers creates delayed costs. BCP, BNF, AFD, CAH, FGD and the social-security institute IPS have distinct legal and fiduciary responsibilities. Treating them as one state cash pool obscures the channels exposed by the current crisis. [12][18][19]

## 19. Private banks: ownership, competition, concentration and lending

Paraguay combines locally controlled banks, foreign-owned subsidiaries and international branches. An official 2025 roster listed local institutions such as Continental, Familiar, Atlas, Basa, Bancop, Interfisa, Solar, Ueno and Zeta; foreign-controlled institutions included Itaú Paraguay, Sudameris and GNB, in addition to Citibank, Banco do Brasil and Banco de la Nación Argentina branches. Mergers, such as Continental's authorized absorption of Banco Río in 2025, mean any static roster can become outdated. [13][20]

Private banks transform deposits into credit for households, traders, farmers and companies. Profit depends on underwriting, pricing, collateral, loan performance, operating costs and governance. Rapid growth may signal efficiency and inclusion, but it may also conceal underpriced risk, optimistic asset recognition or dependence on concentrated public deposits. Competition should be evaluated through effective borrowing costs, loan quality and account portability, not simply the number of mobile-app users. According to the IMF, banking assets were approximately 70% of GDP in 2025. [17][20]

## 20. Cooperatives, dollarization and financial balance-sheet exposure

Banks are not the only savings intermediaries. Credit cooperatives are important, especially for members less served by traditional banks; they are overseen by INCOOP rather than wholly by the BCP's banking supervisor. The IMF estimated cooperative assets at roughly 7% of GDP and finance-company assets at 1.5% of GDP in 2025. Separate supervisory arrangements do not imply no regulation, but they can create differences in information and intervention powers. [17]

In May 2026, the banking system's regulatory capital ratio was 17.3%, Tier 1 ratio 14%, foreign-currency deposits 43.8% of deposits and nonperforming loans 2.6% of loans. These are date-specific system aggregates, not proof that each bank was solvent or observations after the Ueno intervention. One bank can conceal overstated assets while aggregate indicators look comfortable. Dollarization adds currency mismatches: the value of assets and liabilities can adjust differently after an exchange-rate shock. [17]

## 21. The 2026 paradox: strong growth, low inflation and fiscal pressure

Paraguay entered November 2026 with comparatively strong macro indicators. The IMF reported real GDP growth of 6.6% in 2025 and projected 4.4% growth in 2026 in August, while the BCP raised its September 2026 projection to 5.0%. These are forecasts of different vintages, not final outcomes. In early November, the BCP dashboard showed October year-on-year inflation of 1.7%, a policy rate of 5.50% and net international reserves of about $11.30 billion at end-October. Moody's reaffirmed Baa3 investment grade in July, after S&P's own investment-grade upgrade in December 2025. [15][16][22]

Fiscal conditions were less comfortable. In August the IMF projected a 2026 deficit of 3.5% of GDP, partly reflecting clearance of 2023–2025 expenditure arrears and revenues weakened by guaraní appreciation. Moderate public debt does not imply unlimited fiscal room. An economy may grow quickly, maintain stable prices and still struggle with payment arrears, public services or weak supervision. A significant bank can fail without a preceding economy-wide recession. [15][22]

## 22. Public-sector deposits and the role of IPS

Public deposits in commercial banks are liabilities of those banks and financial assets of government agencies or pension funds. Their remuneration may support a public budget or fund, but investment decisions should respect safety, liquidity, diversification and specific statutory limits. Pension assets require matching investment maturities to benefit payments. Large public deposits can accelerate a bank's growth without proportionate diversification of its funding base.

Reporting following the intervention placed public money administered by Ueno at $800–850 million, including approximately $380 million linked to the social security and health fund IPS. These are reported amounts subject to balance reconciliation, classification and audit; they are not equivalent to an established final fiscal loss. Recovery depends on asset quality, claims ranking and the resolution process. The central questions are investment concentration and accountability for those who approved placements. Past political ties to bank controllers warrant conflict-of-interest investigation without converting allegations into verdicts. [23][24]

## 23. Ueno: digitalization, accelerated growth and governance risks

Ueno expanded digital accounts, cards and payments, claiming roughly 2.9–3 million customers. This is exceptional reach in a country of just over six million people, but registrations do not independently reveal average balances, loan quality or recurring profitability. Rapid transition from fintech distribution to large-scale banking requires capital proportional to risk-weighted assets and controls suited to the new size. [23][24]

Grupo Vázquez, the controller, had business ties with President Santiago Peña until March 2025, according to Reuters and AP. Carlos Carvallo, previously linked to Ueno's predecessor, later chaired the central bank. Regulators reportedly warned the bank in June 2026 about lending to related companies; the allegations and accounting adjustments triggering resolution require examination of supervisory records and court findings. Fitch revised the bank's outlook to negative in October, citing fast balance-sheet growth and substantial intangible assets. Intangibles may represent valid technology and rights, but cannot necessarily absorb liquidation losses like readily realizable liquid assets. [23][24]

## 24. 2–4 November 2026: resolution and the political crisis

On 2 November the BCP board unanimously voted to begin Ueno Bank's resolution. Announced on 3 November, the decision stated that adjustments to financial statements during a regularization process had revealed a solvency ratio below half the regulatory minimum. This is the decisive official finding: the case cannot be reduced to a temporary cash shortage or normal market-price fluctuation. The action invoked the 2003 deposit-insurance and bank-resolution law. [12][25]

AP, Reuters and EFE reported restricted account access, queues and protests as well as the detention of executives under criminal investigation. President Santiago Peña asked BCP head Carlos Carvallo to resign, and his resignation was announced on 3 November. On 4 November, 35 lawmakers filed articles seeking impeachment, which did not mean removal had been approved. Peña called for depositors' protection and accountability. Judicial findings and asset recovery were not final at the research cutoff; detention, allegations and former commercial links are not criminal convictions. [23][24][26]

## 25. Deposit protection: limits, timing and possible losses

The Deposit Guarantee Fund protects eligible balances up to a legal ceiling per depositor and participating institution. In the Ueno case the disclosed cap was approximately ₲228.3 million per depositor, roughly $38,000 at exchange rates cited during the episode. On 3 November the BCP announced an initial payout of up to ₲10 million, net of debt offsets, without issuing a full calendar in that notice. Deposit insurance does not automatically guarantee all balances or all public-sector investments. [12][25][27]

Resolution may transfer selected assets and liabilities, pay insured depositors and liquidate residual assets. Recovery depends on a verified creditor list, asset valuations and claims ranking. Pension questions require separating individual clients' accounts, benefit payments administered by IPS and the institution's own investments. Even eventual full repayment cannot erase the short-term costs of frozen access for small businesses, payrolls and households. The quality of resolution depends on speed, transparency and the continuity of essential payment functions as well as ultimate recovery rates. [23][25][27]

## 26. Bank failure or systemic crisis? The analytical distinction

The insolvency of an important bank can have systemic effects but does not make every other bank insolvent by definition. Contagion can occur through depositor withdrawals from institutions seen as similar, defensive credit contraction, public-budget exposure and higher country funding costs if supervisory credibility deteriorates. Prompt insured-deposit payments and transparent communication can interrupt or amplify these channels. [17][24]

On 4 November Reuters reported outages in some banking applications and customers transferring business elsewhere. Banking association Asoban said other institutions were operating normally while absorbing demand. Earlier system-wide capital and liquidity indicators were comfortable, but they cannot settle the post-intervention diagnosis; neither do temporary app outages prove a systemic run. Proper assessment requires bank-by-bank deposit movements, daily withdrawals, interbank funding, spreads, cross-exposures, liquidity and BCP actions over subsequent days. [17][26]

## 27. The institutional dimension: conflicts of interest and legitimacy

The crisis extends beyond Ueno's accounts because it concerns relations between government, regulator, owners and managers of public funds. A president with past investment in a related group, a central bank chief with previous employment in the supervised sector and concentrated public placements all justify scrutiny. None alone proves an offense. Accountability requires reconstructing decisions, information available at the time, statutory limits, recusals and documented supervisory warnings. [23][26]

The 1995–2003 experience underscores the problem: supervisory forbearance and implicit guarantees magnified losses. In 2026 the legal resolution framework was more developed and the macroeconomic backdrop stronger, but institutional vulnerabilities reappear when oversight and regulated interests are not credibly separate. Executive arrests alone, without assessment of fund flows, audits, supervisory decisions and IPS governance, would not prevent recurrence. [10][23][26]

## 28. Regional trade, Brazil, Argentina, Mercosur and Taiwan

Paraguay remains integrated into the River Plate basin through waterways, border trade and binational power agreements. Brazil is crucial to logistics, agribusiness and Itaipu; Argentina matters for navigation, commerce, Yacyretá and regional monetary conditions. Mercosur provides market rules while involving negotiations over external tariffs, origin rules and scale asymmetries. Paraguay's diplomatic recognition of Taiwan also matters to its position relative to mainland China. [2][14][21]

These ties expose domestic credit to external shocks. Drought reduces export output; lower dollar earnings affect agribusiness balance sheets and credit demand; weaker neighbors reduce cross-border activity; guaraní appreciation may reduce fiscal receipts translated from foreign-currency-linked flows. But these general risks are not a substitute for the specific 2026 supervisory finding that Ueno lacked capital after accounting adjustments. Geography explains transmission, not the documented proximate prudential cause. [15][25]

## 29. Labor, informality, land and income distribution

Stable prices and investment-grade ratings can coexist with limited capacity to provide education, health and social protection. The World Bank estimated that around 62% of the workforce was informal in early 2025. Informality restricts pension coverage and contribution revenue and makes it harder to document income for competitively priced bank credit. Poverty has declined over two decades, but national poverty, international purchasing-power-based poverty and multidimensional poverty measures are not interchangeable. [14][15]

Rural inequality has roots in postwar land concentration and modern export agriculture. Education and logistics affect movement from low-productivity rural work to formal services and manufacturing. A bank failure affecting many small account holders creates another distributional asymmetry: households with few liquid alternatives suffer immediately from account freezes, long before final losses are established. Resolution quality is therefore a social issue as well as an accounting issue. [14][23]

## 30. Three paths after November 2026 and the decisive indicators

An orderly resolution, with insured deposits promptly paid, efficient account transfers and losses contained to Ueno's assets, would preserve much of the remaining system's confidence. An intermediate outcome would involve delayed partial payments, litigation over public money and moderately higher funding costs, damaging investment and regulatory reputation. An adverse outcome would require additional evidence of sustained withdrawals at other banks, interbank stress or deterioration of shared assets. It cannot be declared merely from Ueno's customer count. [17][23][26]

The decisive indicators are the actual FGD payout schedule; IPS and other public recovery amounts; asset valuations and provisions; deposits by institution; interbank rates and sovereign spreads; supervisory transparency; judicial findings; fiscal contingencies; and continuity of monetary and budget policy. The long-term issue is whether digital inclusion can scale without weakening capital standards, related-party lending limits and public-deposit governance. [15][17][25][26]

## 31. Conclusion: price stability is not financial integrity

Paraguayan economic history is not a straight path from backwardness to progress. The early republic concentrated resources to preserve autonomy and build state capacity; the Triple Alliance War devastated production and transformed landholding; reconstruction strengthened large estates and external dependence; the twentieth century brought a national currency, central bank, hydropower and modern agriculture. Democratization coincided with financial liberalization that produced major banking crises before stronger regulation. The twenty-first century brought growth, lower poverty, inflation targeting, reserves and investment-grade status. [2][8][10][15]

Ueno's November 2026 failure does not erase those gains or establish that the entire banking system is insolvent. It does reveal a risk hidden by aggregates: rapid credit expansion, ownership links, public placements and weak oversight can turn one institution's losses into a payment, confidence and political legitimacy crisis. Paraguay's next economic chapter will depend on fair resolution across creditors, recovery of public resources, investigation of responsibility and credible proof that supervision applies consistently regardless of commercial or political influence. [17][23][25][26]

```flow

Frozen account access → Payment disruption → Demand for insured payouts and transfers → Potential contagion to confidence elsewhere
```
