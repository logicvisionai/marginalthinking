# Global Macro — Inflation overturns the oil relief rally as US and Brazilian policy stay restrictive

**11 November 2026 · Early Wednesday edition · Information cutoff: 00:35 Brasília time (03:35 UTC).** The most recent completed US and Brazilian equity sessions are **Tuesday, 10 November**. The October US consumer price index and the Brazilian Copom minutes were published on Tuesday and are no longer forecasts. Wednesday Asian trading and releases scheduled for later Wednesday are not represented as completed observations. [1][3][4][5][6]

A new inflation reading abruptly changed the balance between financial-market optimism and the cost of capital. US consumer prices increased **0.4% in October**, versus an approximately **0.3%** consensus, while the index excluding food and energy increased **0.3%**, versus the anticipated **0.2%**. The two-year US Treasury par yield climbed from **4.56% on 9 November to 4.73% on 10 November**; the ten-year rate advanced from **4.88% to 4.98%**. The S&P 500 fell approximately **0.4%**, and Brazil's Ibovespa declined **0.52%** to **171,336.99 points**, with the dollar around **R$5.59**. At the same time, the US Energy Information Administration published an oil outlook with substantially higher October average prices than recent daily quotes. The immediate issue is not whether the war-related oil premium can temporarily shrink, but whether a declining energy quotation can offset inflation that has spread across household expenses while monetary authorities confront slower growth. [1][2][3][4][5][6][7]

## 2. US inflation: an upside surprise with a crucial missing annual comparison

The BLS measured a **0.4%** rise in the all-items CPI in October, compared with **0.6% in September**. Slower monthly inflation than the preceding month is not the same as a favorable surprise relative to market expectations. Reuters respondents anticipated only **0.3%** in October; the realised reading was higher. Core CPI rose **0.3%**, unchanged from September and August, but above the consensus cited before publication. The result narrowed the space for expecting quick monetary easing. [1][2]

The detail is economically important. Food increased **0.5%**, including **0.4%** for food at home and **0.5%** for eating away from home. Shelter rose **0.3%** and accounted for over 30% of the monthly headline gain. Energy advanced **0.7%**, far less than its **5.2%** September increase; gasoline advanced **1.0%**, while electricity rose **0.9%** and gas service fell **0.8%**. Used cars fell **1.2%** and communication services declined. Households face uneven price movements, not an economy in which every purchase cost increases at an identical rate. [1]

A particularly consequential data limitation is frequently lost in rapid coverage: **BLS did not publish a valid October 2026 twelve-month headline or core CPI rate for affected series**. The federal funding lapse of October 2025 interrupted the prior-year price collection. Without the baseline October 2025 index, a standard twelve-month rate cannot be calculated. September's reported annual inflation rate is a September statistic; repeating it as October's annual rate would be a factual error. Neither analyst estimates nor unofficial reconstructions replace the official missing observation. [1][2]

The earlier expectation that cheaper oil alone might disinflate the economy is incomplete. A price decrease in international crude typically affects some refined-fuel prices with delays. Food, health care, rents, insurance and household services depend on additional cost structures and pricing contracts. Higher prices of basic necessities reduce discretionary purchasing power even when employment remains resilient.

## 3. Fed repricing, Treasury yields and the interest-rate channel

The official Treasury curve provides a comparable two-session measure of the shock. On **9 November**, the two-year yield was **4.56%** and the ten-year was **4.88%**. On **10 November**, they reached **4.73%** and **4.98%**, respectively. This is a **17-basis-point** increase at two years and a **10-basis-point** increase at ten years using the Treasury's published daily par-yield table. Market intraday accounts that quote 18 or 19 basis points can be consistent with different snapshots and instruments; the official daily observations are kept separate. [3]

The Federal Reserve had kept its federal funds target at **3.75–4.00%** at its **28 October** meeting, with two dissenters favoring a quarter-point increase. Since then, October payrolls added **165,000** jobs and the unemployment rate was **4.1%**. This reduces the immediacy of recession fears while providing less reason for the Fed to tolerate continued above-target inflation. It does not prove that every industry is accelerating: hiring remains concentrated and monetary policy works with long and uneven lags. [10][11]

Reuters reported that interest-rate derivatives repriced toward a December increase after the CPI and that some investors considered a larger, half-point action possible. Market probabilities are inferences from contracts and depend on time and pricing conventions; they are not commitments by the FOMC. The more durable mechanism is the change in financing costs. Higher short-term Treasury yields can raise reference rates for corporate credit, floating-rate obligations, bank funding and emerging-market portfolios. Debt maturities, spreads and credit quality determine how much reaches each borrower. [2][3]

```flow
October US CPI above consensus → higher expected Federal Reserve policy rate path → short Treasury yields rise → stronger dollar and higher global funding costs → tighter financing for importers, governments and indebted firms → lower real-investment and equity-valuation headroom
Physical oil prices falling → possible delayed fuel disinflation → partial consumer relief; services and contracts may stay elevated → monetary authorities cannot assume broad price stability
```

Neither chain is deterministic. A credible slowdown in real activity can soften rates even while inflation is high; the speed and persistence of pass-through must be observed.

## 4. Financial markets: a reversal without a confirmed economic contraction

Tuesday's US close reversed part of Monday's relief rally. The S&P 500 dropped approximately **0.4%** to **7,520.93**, the Nasdaq **0.7%** to **25,065.87**, the Dow **0.3%** to **52,231.89**, and the Russell 2000 **0.7%** to **2,821.38**. All four remained positive for the week at that moment, a reminder that a negative session does not specify the sign of a multi-day return. The tech-heavy market and smaller-company index both retreated, but for potentially different reasons: long-duration earnings sensitivity, concentrated AI valuations and funding costs for smaller firms. [4]

Price changes in outstanding stocks are valuation changes, not investment-fund cash flows or direct injections of capital into business operations. Likewise the rise in Treasury yields means the market value of outstanding fixed-rate bonds generally declines, but individual portfolio results differ by duration, hedging and maturity. The dollar's strength is particularly consequential for companies and sovereigns that receive local-currency revenue but owe dollars.

The latest available ICI money-market asset stock exceeded **US$8 trillion** in early November, while Reuters Lipper tracked substantial global money-market subscriptions. Those statistics cover different universes and distinguish position from incremental flow. They cannot be added to stock-index market capitalisation to estimate a fictional aggregate transfer of wealth. Strong demand for liquidity can coexist with active buying and selling of risky assets. ## 5. Brazil: the Copom documented a constrained policy trade-off

The Banco Central released minutes of the **282nd Copom meeting** held on **3–4 November**. The committee maintained the Selic target at **13.75%**, describing expectations above target, the effects of Middle East conflict and tighter foreign financial conditions. It also acknowledged a more pronounced deceleration in domestic activity, particularly cyclical sectors, even as employment remained comparatively resilient. Monetary restriction therefore faces two different tests: preventing additional price pressures from becoming persistent and limiting damage to productive investment and employment. [5][12]

The minutes identified **four possible supply-related pressures**: oil prices associated with Middle East hostilities; El Niño effects on agriculture and energy; labour costs associated with ending the six-day work schedule; and exchange-rate depreciation linked to domestic political uncertainty. These are committee **risk assessments and conditional channels**, not independent measurements that every effect has already materialised. In the BCB reference scenario, annual IPCA for **2026 was projected at 5.6%**, **2027 at 4.7%** and the four quarters to the **second quarter of 2028 at 3.5%**. Those are central-bank scenario projections, distinct from the Focus medians of surveyed market analysts. [5]

B3 reported the Ibovespa down **0.52%**, ending Tuesday at **171,336.99**. The dollar was near **R$5.59**, roughly six to nine centavos higher than the approximate preceding close depending on the FX quote convention. Combining a stronger dollar with imported intermediate goods, wheat, fertilizers and technology inputs raises cost sensitivity for firms without natural dollar revenue. For exporters, the weaker real can lift local-currency receipts, but dollar-denominated freight and financing can offset part of that gain. [6]

The interest-rate burden is visible in finance-sensitive projects. With a high Selic, companies financing machinery, trucks, logistics centres and inventories face higher working-capital costs. Falling demand may reduce some firms' ability to pass through input inflation, compressing margins instead. The central bank is trying to limit inflation persistence without presuming that a temporary fall in crude prices has removed energy and currency risks.

Brazil's **October IPCA is scheduled for Thursday, 12 November**, after the early-Wednesday information cutoff. Its eventual reading can change inflation expectations, but it cannot appear as an already observed statistic. [13]

## 6. Energy: an EIA forecast completed before the oil rally reversed

The EIA's **10 November Short-Term Energy Outlook** provides a structural counterweight to the day's falling oil futures. Its forecast was **completed on 5 November**, before the latest market prices. The agency estimated an **October Brent monthly average of US$124 per barrel**, a different object from the roughly US$90–92 daily futures quotes observed around 9–11 November. Its annual-average Brent forecast was **US$97 in 2026** and **US$86 in 2027**, while it projected roughly **US$77** for the fourth quarter of 2027 under the scenario used. Monthly historical averages, annual projections, quarterly projections and intraday futures should never be substituted for one another. [7][8][9]

The agency described falling global inventories, shipping disruption and tanker risk as key contributors to high October oil prices. It estimated US commercial crude stocks at **410 million barrels at the end of October**, a relatively low level for that calendar month, and projected US oil production at **14.3 million barrels per day in 2027**. Its scenario also assumed progressive recovery of production outside Iran in the Middle East next year. The assumption is conditional; renewed disruption could invalidate its inventory and price path. [7][8]

Gas markets add a different shock. The EIA raised its projected end-November US gas inventory by almost **70 billion cubic feet** relative to the prior monthly forecast after warm weather. It forecast Henry Hub natural gas prices averaging **US$2.87 per million Btu** across November–March, **16% below** the preceding forecast for that period. Oil and gas have different physical balances, infrastructure and end uses; cheaper US gas does not establish uniformly cheaper energy for European manufacturers or Asian crude-importing refiners. [9]

The direct economic transmission depends on the delivered cost of fuel. A maritime insurer's assessment, an actual tanker passage and a refinery's crude acquisition cost can move at different speeds from the Brent screen quote. Governments importing fuel may enjoy relief in trade payments only as shipments arrive, while commodity exporters lose receipts under lower prices.

## 7. China and trade: nominal export growth alongside global demand sensitivity

China's October customs exports rose approximately **35.2% year on year in nominal US dollars**, while the January–October goods surplus reached approximately **US$1.04 trillion**. The latest trade result increases the importance of external markets to Chinese industrial production and the concentration of manufacturing capacity in electronics, machinery and supply chains. It does not measure real domestic consumption or household income. A large nominal surplus can result from strong exports, restrained imports or both. [14]

China's official October manufacturing PMI was **49.3**, below the 50 expansion threshold. New orders were **49.4**. These survey readings are diffusion indices rather than percentage production changes. Foreign orders for artificial-intelligence infrastructure can coexist with weak domestic manufacturing sentiment, particularly where property-related balance sheets and household expectations remain under pressure. The strength of export demand is therefore not proof of a synchronized consumer recovery. [15]

An inflation shock in the United States creates a two-sided constraint for China: dollar strength can affect export pricing and Asian currency conditions, while higher American interest rates and weaker end demand can eventually slow orders for electronics. Chinese importers of energy might benefit from lower crude prices, but disruptions in physical shipping and currency depreciation can reduce that saving. Trade policy, tariffs and restrictions on high-performance technology remain separate political uncertainties from a single month's customs numbers.

## 8. Europe, the United Kingdom and emerging-market transmission

Eurostat's preliminary October harmonised CPI was **4.0% annually**, up from **3.8%** in September, with an energy component reported at **20.8%** year on year. This describes a regional consumer-price basket, not the same methodology as US CPI; comparison requires respecting geographical coverage and statistical definitions. Oil falling after the measurement month cannot retroactively cancel the index's October increase. [16]

The Bank of England increased Bank Rate to **4.00%** at its November meeting. With inflationary energy risks and wage-setting uncertainty, the UK faced a similar constraint to the Fed but in a different price and financial environment. The energy import burden depends on fuel composition, transmission tariffs, industrial contracts and currency movements rather than Brent alone. [17]

Emerging-market authorities confront an additional external channel. When US yields rise, domestic high-yield assets may need higher compensation to attract capital, especially where inflation forecasts remain above targets. A stronger dollar raises the local-currency cost of USD liabilities and imported machinery. The eventual effect on foreign exchange, however, depends on hedging, export receipts, reserves and country-specific institutions, not a mechanical fixed percentage.

## 9. Competing scenarios and observable tests

**Persistent inflation and higher-for-longer funding.** If US service and shelter costs remain elevated and labor demand holds up, the Fed may keep or increase policy restriction. Global rates, the dollar and real-economy refinancing costs could remain under pressure. Contrary signals would be consecutive benign core CPI readings, weakening wage-sensitive sectors and falling short-term Treasury yields.

**Physical energy normalization and gradual disinflation.** If the Middle East sees verified shipping recovery and delivered fuel becomes cheaper, transport and headline inflation could improve with lags. The EIA's November outlook would need to be revised as the actual supply path becomes clearer. Insurance spikes, renewed attacks, persistent physical market premiums and low inventories would contradict a rapid normalization.

**Disinflation through weaker growth rather than easier supply.** Tighter credit and declining consumption could slow prices, but at the cost of lower employment, capital expenditure and corporate margins. That would be different from a healthy productivity-led disinflation. Strong order books, real wages and resilient investment would weaken the hard-landing scenario.

**Regional fragmentation.** Chinese export strength may persist as US consumer purchasing power weakens, generating more trade restrictions and regional reallocation. Brazil could face both higher dollar import costs and lower domestic demand. Evidence against fragmentation would include coordinated trade liberalisation, improved industrial orders across multiple regions and lower credit spreads.
