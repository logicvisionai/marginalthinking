# Ore keeps arriving while steel cools: China shifts the adjustment to inventories and exporters

*Global Macro — 23 November 2026 | Information cut-off: 06:20 BRT*

## Central assessment

China’s iron-ore imports are still growing while its crude-steel and pig-iron output are contracting. That divergence is the most useful new physical-market signal in the current macro cycle. It does not prove that imports are excessive: ore quality, domestic mining, inventory changes, blending, shipment timing and the steel mix prevent a one-to-one comparison. It does, however, make port stocks, mill margins and the pace of new export capacity more important than headline import volumes.

The latest available customs data show Chinese iron-ore imports of 1.061 billion tonnes in January–October, up 3.1% year on year. Over the same period, crude-steel output fell 2.8%, pig iron fell 3.1% and finished-steel output fell 2.6%. In October alone, crude-steel production was 70.83 million tonnes, 1.6% below a year earlier. The adjustment is therefore not occurring only through lower imports. It is being absorbed through inventories, product mix, operating rates and margins. [1][2]

This edition changes the emphasis of the three latest available daily editions. The 22 November issue examined the distribution of AI-related productivity and the repricing of technology returns; 21 November focused on cross-asset risk and funding conditions; 19 November examined the energy and shipping shock. The present note moves from financial prices to a physical supply chain: the mismatch between ore inflows, steel production and exporter receipts. It does not repeat the AI-valuation or energy-security theses.

**Observed facts.** Imports of iron ore rose 3.1% in the first ten months; Chinese crude-steel production fell 2.8%; port inventories increased in the latest SteelHome survey; Brazilian iron-ore export value fell faster than volume; and Simandou has begun shipping while its port remains under construction. [1][3][4][5][10]

**Inference.** The market is pricing a more difficult distinction between the availability of ore and the ability of steel mills to earn a return from converting it. Import demand can remain high for inventory, blending or procurement reasons even as marginal steelmaking economics deteriorate.

**Not established.** The available data do not prove a structural surplus of iron ore, a coordinated reduction in Chinese demand, or a near-term price collapse. The latest full global steel-production comparison is still September data; October data are scheduled for 24 November. [6]

## 1. China: the input and the output are moving in different directions

The National Bureau of Statistics reported that industrial value added rose 4.8% year on year in October and 5.3% over January–October. High-technology manufacturing grew 17.6% in October. Yet the ferrous-metals smelting and rolling sector contracted 1.7%, crude-steel production fell 1.6%, pig iron fell 4.1% and rolled-steel output fell 5.1%. The aggregate industrial figure therefore conceals a sharp split between selected technology-intensive production and the steel chain. [1]

Domestic demand indicators reinforce that split. Retail sales increased only 0.4% year on year in October. Fixed-asset investment fell 8.1% over January–October, while real-estate development investment declined 21.6%. These are not direct measures of steel consumption, but they weaken the case for assuming that stronger iron-ore imports are being pulled by a broad recovery in construction demand. Manufacturing, infrastructure projects and export-oriented production can still support selected steel grades, while property-related demand remains under pressure. [2]

The customs series, as reported by the China Mining Association, records 1.0606 billion tonnes of iron-ore imports in the first ten months, 3.1% above the same period in 2025. This is a cumulative trade flow, not a measure of ore consumed by blast furnaces. Domestic ore supply, inventories at ports and mills, imported grades, pellet and concentrate use, and the timing of shipments all affect the relationship between imports and steel output. The correct interpretation is a divergence that requires an inventory bridge, not a direct conversion ratio. [3]

The divergence becomes more informative when combined with inventory and operating data. SteelHome’s 19 November survey put iron-ore stocks at 157.36 million tonnes across its monitored ports, up 1.16 million tonnes week on week. About 110 million tonnes were described as trader-held inventory. The survey is an industry estimate, not an official census of all port or mill stocks; its value lies in showing that the import flow is not being matched by a comparable increase in steel output. [4]

Reuters, citing Mysteel data, reported that only about 10% of surveyed Chinese steel mills were profitable, compared with 38% a year earlier. Daily hot-metal production had fallen for six consecutive weeks to around 2.3 million tonnes by 19 November. The most-active Dalian iron-ore contract was down 0.69% at 651.5 yuan per tonne, while the Singapore December contract was down 0.25% at US$88.25. These prices indicate pressure, not a disorderly liquidation. [5]

Procurement can remain firm for reasons that do not signal stronger final demand. A mill can maintain ore purchases to secure grade availability, replenish a depleted stock position or take advantage of a temporary price discount even when its current steel margin is poor. Traders can also hold inventory for financing, blending and delivery optionality. A rise in port stocks is therefore consistent with weaker final demand, but it is not sufficient by itself to establish excess supply. The decisive evidence would be a sustained decline in hot-metal output, falling mill and port stocks at the same time, and weaker import premiums across grades.

## 2. The global steel cycle is uneven

World Steel Association data for September show global crude-steel production of 142.4 million tonnes, down 0.2% year on year. January–September output reached 1.368 billion tonnes, 0.7% below the same period in 2025. Regional differences are material: Africa was up 9.7%, North America 4.7% and other Europe 4.1%, while Asia and Oceania were down 0.9%, the European Union 0.8%, South America 0.2% and the Middle East 9.2%. The October global release is scheduled for 24 November and is not yet available at this information cut-off. [6]

The World Steel Association’s October short-range outlook projected global steel demand to remain broadly flat in 2026 at about 1.722 billion tonnes before returning to growth in 2027. That is a forecast, not a realized demand figure. It also does not imply that demand is distributed evenly: Chinese property weakness, infrastructure spending, defence procurement, manufacturing exports and regional construction cycles produce different steel requirements. [7]

The export corridors show why a single China-demand narrative is inadequate.

Brazil’s October iron-ore exports, extracted from the Ministry of Development, Industry, Trade and Services’ Comex Stat data for NCM 2601, were approximately 36.5 million tonnes, worth about US$2.12 billion. The implied average unit value was roughly US$58.1 per tonne. Against October 2025, volume was down 8.9%, value down 27.6% and unit value down 20.5%. The volume and price changes together explain why export receipts fell more sharply than tonnage. These are product-level calculations from the official trade database, not a separate headline estimate published by the ministry. [8]

Australia’s Pilbara Ports Authority reported 48.6 million tonnes of throughput at Port Hedland in October, including 48.0 million tonnes of iron-ore exports, 4% below October 2025. The port remains a dominant export corridor, but the monthly decline shows that Australian shipments are not immune to the weaker steel cycle. Port throughput is a logistics measure; it should not be read as mine production or final Chinese consumption. [9]

Guinea adds a different supply dimension. SimFer reported 2.2 million tonnes shipped to China in the first half of 2026, including 1.6 million tonnes in the second quarter. The rail network was fully commissioned in the first quarter; mine construction was 77% complete and port infrastructure 85% complete, with port commissioning then expected in the first quarter of 2027. These are company-reported project milestones. They demonstrate an operating corridor, but not full project capacity or a completed expansion of global supply. [10]

The Guinean government’s 19 November account of the Simandou 2040 strategic committee meeting said that the committee examined the ratification process for the law establishing a sovereign fund. It did not say that the fund had been ratified, capitalized or made operational. The distinction is important: mineral output can rise before the institutions required to convert royalties, taxes and state participation into durable public investment are fully established. [11]

The supply map is therefore not simply “China versus the rest”. Brazil faces a lower unit value and weaker tonnage; Australia remains the largest established bulk corridor but reported lower October shipments; Guinea is adding a new high-grade route whose full port capacity is not yet available. For Guinea, the economic test will be the conversion of a major infrastructure project into domestic supplier development, tax receipts, skills and reliable public institutions. For established exporters, the test is whether lower realized prices force a change in capital expenditure or only compress margins.

## 3. Financial conditions: a high hurdle for marginal capacity

The latest complete US trading session was Friday, 20 November. The S&P 500 fell 3.7% and the Nasdaq 5.3%, with technology shares leading the decline. The US Treasury par curve closed around 4.63% at two years, 4.92% at ten years and 5.31% at thirty years. The curve was positive, but the absolute cost of long-duration financing remained high. The market session on 23 November was still in progress in Asia at the information cut-off; US trading had not reopened. [12][13]

The Federal Reserve’s October meeting minutes, published on 18 November, show a 10–2 vote to keep the federal-funds target range at 3.75%–4.00%. Two members preferred a 25-basis-point increase. Participants described inflation as elevated and noted that AI-related capital spending, energy prices and geopolitical uncertainty could sustain price pressure. The minutes also recorded uncertainty about the timing and distribution of productivity gains from AI. A possible December increase is a market expectation, not a decision already taken by the Committee. [14]

The European Central Bank’s November Financial Stability Review warns that high valuations, sovereign funding needs, non-bank leverage and liquidity mismatches can amplify shocks. That is relevant to mining and steel projects because the marginal cost of financing long-lived infrastructure depends on both benchmark yields and the willingness of banks, private-credit funds and bond investors to absorb duration and construction risk. The Review is a risk assessment, not evidence that a new credit event has occurred. [15]

Brazil illustrates the fiscal and monetary constraint faced by commodity exporters. The Central Bank reported a consolidated primary deficit of 0.52% of GDP over the twelve months to September, nominal interest expenditure of 8.99% of GDP and gross public debt of 82.9% of GDP. The Copom kept the Selic rate at 13.75% on 4 November. A weaker iron-ore unit value can reduce export receipts and mining cash flow, while a high domestic policy rate raises the hurdle for new logistics, processing and industrial projects. The fiscal data do not establish that mining receipts alone determine debt dynamics; they show the financing environment in which any new investment must compete. [16][17]

Credit conditions deserve a separate qualification. The ECB identifies vulnerabilities in non-bank finance and private credit, but a fully comparable same-day global credit-spread series was not available by the cut-off. The equity sell-off alone does not establish a new credit shock. The relevant question is whether a prolonged period of high long-term yields and weaker commodity receipts raises refinancing costs for marginal mines, railways, ports and steel capacity.

These are survey readings, not official output data. They suggest that European manufacturing and services are not moving in lockstep, which matters for steel demand and capital spending across the region. [18][19]

The dollar eased from a recent 19-month high during Monday’s Asian session, while gold rebounded after a sharp weekly decline. That combination is consistent with some unwinding of the most crowded dollar positions, but it is not evidence of a sustained change in the dollar cycle. ## 4. Energy and geopolitics: crude supply is recovering, product risk is not

The International Energy Agency’s November Oil Market Report estimates that global oil supply rose by 2.3 million barrels per day in October to 105.4 million barrels per day. It forecasts 2026 demand at 102.3 million barrels per day, down 2.7 million from 2025. Observed inventories increased by 52 million barrels in October, largely because oil in transit rose, but on-land stocks continued to fall and remained at their lowest level since at least 2016. Diesel prices were still about 77% above pre-war levels when the report was written. [21]

For steel producers, crude availability and refined-product availability are separate constraints. Mining, rail and shipping are energy-intensive; diesel and freight costs can remain elevated even when benchmark crude prices fall. The IEA attributes continuing pressure to attacks on energy infrastructure and tankers, disrupted product exports and high freight costs. The report does not establish responsibility for every individual maritime incident. Reuters reported Brent near US$91.97 and WTI near US$82.92 early on 23 November, with the decline in crude prices limited by the continuing conflict-related risk premium. [21][22]

The energy balance is not uniformly bearish. Higher Gulf exports and the IEA’s projected fourth-quarter surplus suggest that the crude market is rebalancing. Refined products, shipping insurance and freight remain the tighter links. A steel mill or mine may therefore face lower benchmark oil prices without receiving the same reduction in delivered diesel, power or transport costs.

## 5. Scenarios and falsifiers

**Base case — inventory-led adjustment.** Chinese steel output remains weak, imports stay supported by procurement and stock management, and port inventories remain elevated. Iron-ore prices move sideways to lower, with margins compressed for higher-cost exporters. Brazil’s export receipts remain more sensitive to unit prices than to tonnage; Australian volumes remain large but do not accelerate; Guinea’s new corridor expands gradually as the port reaches commissioning.

**Upside case — restocking follows better steel margins.** Chinese construction and infrastructure demand stabilize, hot-metal output stops falling, mill profitability improves and port stocks decline without a collapse in imports. A sustained improvement in steel margins would be more informative than one month of higher ore imports. Non-Chinese demand and the 2027 global steel-demand forecast would then provide additional support.

**Downside case — demand weakness meets new supply.** Property investment remains deeply negative, finished-steel demand and prices weaken, hot-metal output falls further and port inventories rise. The arrival of additional Simandou volumes would then increase competition among exporters before demand absorbs the new supply. Brazil and Australia would face lower realized prices; Guinea would face a more difficult ramp-up and a smaller fiscal dividend than project headlines imply.

The base case would be falsified by a sustained decline in port and mill inventories accompanied by improving steel margins and higher hot-metal output. The downside case would be weakened by a durable recovery in Chinese property completions, stronger infrastructure orders, and a broad improvement in regional steel production. The next global steel-production release, scheduled for 24 November, is the first major data point that can test whether September’s regional divergence continued into October. [6]

## 6. Transmission map

```flow
China property and investment weakness
  -> lower steel demand and weaker mill margins
  -> slower hot-metal production
  -> inventory absorption and procurement adjustment
  -> pressure on iron-ore prices and exporter unit values
  -> lower cash flow and tighter project-financing thresholds
  -> slower marginal mine, rail and port investment

Gulf shipping and refined-product disruption
  -> higher diesel, insurance and freight costs
  -> higher delivered cost for mines, mills and logistics
  -> slower pass-through from lower crude benchmarks to industrial costs
```
