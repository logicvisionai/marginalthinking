# After Accelerationism: Artificial Intelligence, Labour Income and the Struggle over the Pace of the Twenty-First Century

The dispute over technological acceleration has moved beyond philosophical claims about an increasingly machinic capitalism. In 2026, the speed at which artificial-intelligence models improve has become a practical contest over access to computation, the organisation of firms, the terms of work, the legitimacy of political institutions and the distribution of national security capabilities. A faster model can reduce the cost of one cognitive task. Whether it raises the wage of the person performing that task, eliminates an entry-level vacancy, expands a medical service or increases the profit of a dominant infrastructure provider is decided by additional economic and institutional mechanisms. This is the distinction that turns accelerationism from a description of technical speed into a research problem about power. [1][2][3][4]

A striking feature of the evidence available by 2 November 2026 is the coexistence of rapid uptake and ambiguous aggregate labour-market outcomes. Stanford's 2026 AI Index reports that 88% of surveyed organisations used AI in 2025, against 55% in 2023; generative AI was used in at least one business function by 70%, while deployment of agents remained in the single digits across almost every business function. The International Labour Organization's June 2026 review finds real but uneven productivity gains and still limited large-scale job displacement. Anthropic's March 2026 analysis does not find a systematic increase in unemployment among the most exposed workers since late 2022, but identifies suggestive evidence of slower hiring for young workers in exposed occupations. None of those observations alone resolves the long-run question. They do change what an intellectually serious version of the debate may claim today. [1][2][3]

The central proposition is conditional: **technical capability may accelerate faster than the capacity of labour markets, distribution systems, infrastructure and political institutions to convert it into widely shared welfare**. This is not a claim that collapse, universal abundance, unemployment or authoritarianism must follow. Three rival paths remain credible: diffusion through competition and complementary work; concentrated returns to scarce assets; and fragmentation under physical, regulatory and geopolitical constraints. The decisive evidence will concern realised productivity, occupational transitions, household incomes, access to essential services, competition and institutional accountability—not the number of impressive demonstrations.

## 1. What changed in 2026: capability, adoption and the missing economic link

An AI capability benchmark measures performance under a specified evaluation. A survey of firms measures reported use. A productivity experiment measures outcomes in a bounded workflow. An employment series measures jobs under many simultaneous macroeconomic forces. These indicators sit at different stages of the economic process and cannot be substituted for one another. The 2026 Stanford index finds substantial improvements in coding and other benchmarks, while still identifying weaknesses in the deployment of more autonomous systems and responsibility assessments. It also reports that the leading model-performance gap between the United States and China has narrowed sharply. Neither an apparent frontier convergence nor a record benchmark supplies a forecast of payroll employment. [1]

The adoption numbers reveal a further distinction. An organisation can report AI use because one team uses a chatbot; that classification does not mean the firm's principal processes were reorganised. An agent can complete a demonstration yet fail in production because of permission boundaries, accountability, data quality, integration, error recovery and the need for human supervision. The relevant economic unit is the **end-to-end process with its verification cost**, not the isolated answer the model generates. A model that drafts a contract in minutes may save little time if lawyers must reconstruct facts and liabilities from the beginning. Conversely, a thoroughly integrated system can have large effects without a spectacular public model announcement.


Source: Stanford HAI, 2026 AI Index, Economy chapter [1]. Survey-reported adoption is neither intensity of use nor measured causal output growth; the two observations are from the survey series, not a continuous extrapolation.

The question after a sharp adoption increase is therefore not whether AI is present, but where savings emerge in accounts and where they are distributed. Are firms completing more customer transactions per hour? Are prices falling? Are productivity gains retained as operating margins or passed to consumers? Are new jobs created elsewhere in the supply chain? Do younger workers retain a path into complex tasks? These questions are empirically separable, and each admits contrary evidence.

## 2. Accelerationism is not one policy programme

The philosophical history of accelerationism has already been examined in Marginal Thinking's work on its intellectual genealogy, Nick Land and Curtis Yarvin. Those studies differentiate the Marxian question of productive forces from Land's account of runaway capitalist and cybernetic dynamics, left-accelerationist projects for collective institutions, neo-reactionary designs of sovereignty, and contemporary effective-accelerationist culture. The intellectual families share an interest in speed and technological transformation; they do not share a single theory of property, political legitimacy or desirable social outcome. [14][15][16]

The present dispute begins where those earlier accounts leave off: when someone calls for faster AI, **which constraint should be relaxed, for whose benefit and with what accountability**? Public funding for electricity grids, a reduction in anticompetitive behaviour, faster medical trials, permission to deploy less-tested military autonomy and the removal of civil-service checks are distinct interventions. Treating them as expressions of one coherent ideology conflates social objectives with instruments. Inversely, treating a request for safety evaluation as a rejection of innovation conceals the economic question of which actors bear the costs of a failure.

A programme of accelerated progress that assumes competition will distribute the benefits must explain how the relevant markets stay contestable. A programme of restraint that assumes safety can be supervised centrally must explain how regulation avoids making large incumbents the only firms able to comply. A programme for public ownership must explain financing, procurement competence, democratic oversight and the possibility of poor state execution. The ideological label does not settle any of these institutional tests.

## 3. The September 2026 argument over pace is an institutional dispute

In his September 2026 essay, *We Must Pace the Frontier*, Anthropic chief executive Dario Amodei defended deliberate pacing and stronger assessment of increasingly capable systems, including proposals for independent evaluation. He described potential scientific and economic gains together with risks from loss of control, misuse and economic upheaval. These are attributed forecasts and proposals by a leading developer, not independently established forecasts of disease eradication or economic growth. A large incumbent's argument for safety can be sincere and also be economically relevant to competition; investigating one possibility does not disprove the other. [8]

On 23 September, OpenAI chief executive Sam Altman addressed the United Nations Security Council. He warned both of a loss of human control and of the concentration of AI power in too few hands, arguing for oversight, incident reporting, complementary international standards and democratic decision-making. The UN record confirms that the intervention occurred. His statement is a primary source for OpenAI's public position; implementation and the distribution of benefits require separate documentary evidence. [9][10]

| Actor and date | Documented position | Mechanism proposed | What remains unproven |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Dario Amodei, September 2026 | Deliberately pace frontier development and strengthen independent evaluation | Reduce unobserved risk before deploying more autonomous capability | Whether the proposed controls are sufficient, enforceable and competitively neutral |
| Sam Altman, 23 September 2026 | Human control, international standards and limits on concentration of power | Shared safety evidence and democratic oversight | Whether business and regulatory decisions deliver the stated distributional and safety outcomes |
| International Labour Organization, 1 June 2026 | Uneven productivity gains and limited aggregate displacement so far | Examine actual workplaces, task reorganisation and job quality | Long-term employment equilibrium following broad deployment |
| IMF analytical work, 2024 and 2026 | Large cross-country exposure, heterogeneous complementarity and new-skill demand | Labour markets transmit adoption through wages, tasks and investment | Which exposures ultimately become permanent displacement |

These positions should not be flattened into an epic of two camps. A firm can seek faster innovation in medicine and stricter constraints in autonomous weapons. A state can favour open research while limiting exports of particular hardware. A worker can value AI assistance and oppose being evaluated by an opaque algorithm. The meaningful divide is frequently between forms of governance and ownership, rather than enthusiasm and fear.

## 4. How model capability becomes—or fails to become—productivity

A workflow begins with inputs, decisions, validation, execution and delivery. Models alter particular operations within it. The realised productivity effect depends on task share, reliability, supervision, integration, the cost of computing, how output is used and the ability to change the surrounding organisation. A tenfold improvement in the speed of drafting text does not produce a tenfold increase in completed legal cases when court schedules, testimony, approval and client acquisition are unchanged. This is a general bottleneck argument, not a claim that legal applications have no value.

Consider a software team. An assistant may generate boilerplate, tests and first drafts of documentation. Its contribution depends on integration into repositories, maintainable code, review practices, security and the ability of engineers to understand systems. A manager can use the saving to release better software sooner, expand the backlog or reduce junior hiring. In the first case, existing workers may gain influence and firm revenue rises; in the second, work expands while headcount stabilises; in the third, the company's payroll share may fall. All three can arise from similar improvements in code generation, making technical performance insufficient to establish a labour result.

```flow
Capability on a bounded task → Integration into a complete workflow → Verified change in cost, quality or throughput → Price, margin and demand response → Firm investment and hiring → Wages, profits and public revenues → Household welfare
Cheap model access → Additional computing and energy demand → Electricity and network constraints → Deployment costs → Uneven geographic diffusion
```

The flow contains conditional transitions, not a claim that every link has already occurred. Higher productivity can create additional work if lower prices expand demand enough; it can also reduce labour demand if the process becomes much less labour-intensive and the market is saturated. The sign and size of the employment response require sector-specific demand elasticities, capital constraints and time lags.

## 5. Labour exposure is not the unemployment rate

An IMF analysis published in 2024 estimated that nearly 40% of world employment was exposed to AI, with an estimated 60% in advanced economies, 40% in emerging markets and 26% in low-income countries. These figures are indicators of potential task exposure, not shares of jobs forecast to disappear. The distribution is plausibly higher in wealthier economies partly because professional cognitive tasks occupy a larger employment share; the ability to benefit from AI is also greater where education, connectivity and infrastructure are adequate. Differences between historical and newer IMF estimates must preserve method and denominator before comparison. [4][5]

```chart

Emerging market economies | 40
Low-income countries | 26
```

Source: IMF Staff Discussion Note, January 2024 [4]. Different groups and definitions should not be added together or read as a 2026 employment forecast.

The ILO's June 2026 synthesis analyses experiments, firm evidence, worker surveys and platform studies, finding productivity improvements that have not uniformly translated into measured output, earnings or jobs. It particularly highlights the possibility that hiring for younger workers becomes more difficult while existing workers continue to perform their tasks with AI. Anthropic's observed-exposure study reaches a compatible but narrower conclusion: no systematic unemployment rise in its high-exposure groups since late 2022, alongside suggestive slowing in early-career hiring. The result comes from a model developer's own empirical research, a reason to compare methods and data, not to dismiss the evidence automatically. [2][3]

The mechanism matters because occupational adjustment is asymmetric. An experienced professional can combine AI output with organisational context, client trust and final accountability. A novice may historically have obtained precisely that experience by preparing the draft that AI now produces. If a firm saves costs by removing training opportunities without changing senior responsibilities, it can temporarily improve margins while reducing its future pool of qualified professionals. Alternative outcomes are possible: cheap assistance may let smaller firms employ more new workers and broaden access to expertise. The falsifying comparison is whether entry employment and eventual advancement recover as diffusion deepens.

| Indicator | What it measures | What it cannot establish alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Potential exposure | Tasks that current systems could affect | Actual adoption or lost jobs |
| Observed AI use | Interaction with deployed products | Net productivity or causal substitution |
| Output per hour | Production relative to labour time | Who receives the gains |
| Vacancies and hiring by seniority | Demand for entrants and experienced workers | Technology as the unique cause |
| Wage distribution | Income outcomes across workers | The effect of AI without a comparison group |
| Capital and operating margins | Financial consequences for firms | Final societal welfare or monopoly causation |

There is also a time problem. Firms can delay formal layoffs, reassign workers or cut new vacancies before aggregate unemployment changes. Macroeconomic growth or recession can obscure the effect. Policy assessment needs occupational cohorts, comparable firms, wage records, hours and repeated measurement—not a single headline about layoffs or a national unemployment figure. The hypothesis of entry-level erosion could prove stronger than the hypothesis of immediate general unemployment, but it remains to be established across countries and occupations.

## 6. Who owns the new productive capacity?

The emerging AI economy is not only a market for model subscriptions. It comprises chips, semiconductor manufacturing capacity, design software, networking, cloud contracts, data centres, electricity, cooling, models, proprietary datasets, distribution channels and specialised human services. A reduction in inference cost can lower an application's price while increasing total demand for computation. The beneficiary may be the application developer, an electricity supplier, a cloud operator, customers, employees or shareholders in multiple locations.

Financial accounting must distinguish an announced investment from a finished asset, a paid contract from revenue recognition, a company's market capitalisation from its cash flow, and a productivity promise from observed value added. A reported investment boom raises questions about utilisation, debt service, depreciation, competition and the risk of stranded infrastructure. The 2026 AI Index reports intense industrial concentration in development of notable models, while also documenting a rapidly changing international competitive frontier. It does not prove that current industry leaders will retain rents indefinitely. [1]

Competition can reduce margins at the model layer while scarce chip fabrication, electricity-grid connections or enterprise switching costs sustain scarcity rents elsewhere. Conversely, open models, on-device processing, standardised interfaces and improved efficiency can weaken incumbent control. Treating the whole system as either fully monopolised or frictionlessly competitive hides where market power actually arises. The comparative test is firm entry, effective switching costs, costs per verified task, concentration at each industrial stage and the portion of productivity growth paid to labour and consumers.

The already published Marginal Thinking study on technofeudalism provides the relevant conceptual dispute: when do platform tolls and private control over digital markets signify a different economic system, and when are they contemporary forms of capitalist rent and market power? The present evidence does not settle that controversy. It makes measurement of ownership, price-setting power and contestability indispensable. [16]

## 7. Electricity is not a metaphor

The economic politics of AI are constrained by physical systems. In its 2025 *Energy and AI* report, the International Energy Agency estimated that data centres used about 415 TWh of electricity worldwide in 2024, around 1.5% of global electricity demand. Its Base Case projects about 945 TWh in 2030, with AI as an important driver alongside other digital services. The 2030 figure is a conditional projection, not measured demand and not electricity exclusively attributable to AI. [6]


Source: IEA, *Energy and AI*, executive summary [6]. The two bars have different evidentiary status. Future realised demand can differ with equipment efficiency, workloads, construction, grid access and power prices.

The same IEA report estimated that in 2024 the United States accounted for 45% of world data-centre electricity use, China for 25%, and Europe for 15%. These are geographical shares of data-centre electricity, not percentages of AI compute capability, model ownership or political influence. Regional energy systems, construction lead times and industrial policy affect the cost of deployment. Local congestion may be severe even while the global share of electricity remains relatively modest.

```map
title: Data-centre electricity demand and geographic constraints

China | 25% of global data-centre electricity in 2024 | IEA estimate; industrial and grid conditions differ
Europe | 15% of global data-centre electricity in 2024 | IEA regional aggregate; heterogeneous member-state systems
Brazil | Potential energy and data-centre investment opportunity | No comparable Brazil share established by this study
```

A technically superior model can be unavailable to users because a grid connection is delayed or an enterprise cannot finance integration. A country's hydropower resources do not automatically ensure suitable energy delivered at the right location and time. Governments that seek to become AI hubs must assess grid reinforcement, industrial demand, water, land rights, tax privileges, foreign-exchange exposure and the social value of alternative energy uses.

## 8. Universal basic income: distribution is a design problem

If machines can complete more paid tasks, one political response is to reduce the dependence of income on continuous employment. Universal basic income is a recurring, individual, unconditional payment broadly available irrespective of labour status. It differs from a means-tested minimum-income programme, a negative income tax, temporary unemployment insurance, universal public services, social wealth funds, or worker ownership of productive assets. These instruments distribute benefits and risks differently; calling all of them UBI obscures budgets and eligibility.

The OpenResearch unconditional cash study provides a useful empirical warning against easy conclusions. A randomised US experiment studied transfers of $1,000 per month to 1,000 lower-income adults over three years, compared with 2,000 controls receiving $50. The NBER's paper, originally released in July 2024 and revised in August 2026, reports reductions in labour supply and labour-market participation in that specific setting. This is evidence about a defined transfer experiment and population; it is neither a definitive argument against income security nor a prediction of a national permanent programme funded by automation profits. The revised paper should take precedence over older summaries when reporting effect magnitudes. [7]

A distributional comparison must identify who receives a transfer, who pays tax, the price level, existing benefits displaced, housing and healthcare supply, the response of the labour market and the durability of the financing source. An income guarantee can increase an individual's bargaining power even when they choose fewer paid hours, but its welfare value cannot be inferred from hours alone. Reduced hours can reflect valued time for care or education; it can also accompany lower earnings without improvement in job quality. Evaluation therefore needs consumption, time use, health, debt, employment quality and intergenerational opportunity.

| Instrument | Transfer or public asset | Primary economic question | Principal implementation risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Universal basic income | Broad individual cash entitlement | Can a recurring net fiscal transfer raise security without destabilising supply? | Scale of net funding, inflation in constrained services, displacement of existing benefits |
| Targeted minimum income | Payment dependent on income or vulnerability | Does targeting reach households in need with low exclusion? | Administrative errors and high effective marginal tax rates |
| Social wealth dividend | Share of returns from publicly held productive assets | Can public ownership obtain durable, diversified returns? | Fiscal concentration and governance of public investments |
| Universal basic services | Public provision of health, education, mobility or care | Does supply-side access relieve basic cost constraints? | Capacity, service quality and uneven regional implementation |
| Wage insurance and retraining | Income replacement and worker adjustment | Can displaced workers enter sustainable occupations? | Insufficient job demand or unsuitable training |
| Working-time reduction | Redistribution of hours | Can verified productivity gains finance fewer hours without wage collapse? | Uneven coverage and sector-specific production constraints |

A tax on robots is often presented as a simple financing solution. Yet the tax base is difficult to define: a spreadsheet, a robotic arm and a model-powered software service may all replace some labour but differ economically and legally. Taxing gross automation use may discourage beneficial investment. Profit-based taxes and broad capital taxation can be administratively clearer but face avoidance, cross-border coordination and cyclical profitability. No financing mechanism can be assumed before specifying its legal base and incidence.

## 9. Brazil: a social and industrial test, not a miniature Silicon Valley

Brazil's stakes differ from those of the economies that develop most frontier models. Many Brazilian workers are in services, commerce, logistics, agriculture, industry, public administration and informal activities whose tasks, connectivity and capital intensity vary widely. A cheap Portuguese-language assistant may improve small-business administration or access to public information without replacing a full worker. At the same time, professional services, software entry roles and outsourced business processes may face greater direct competition from automation.

The IBGE's PNAD Contínua recorded a 5.2% unemployment rate in the moving quarter ending September 2026, released on 30 October. That is a national labour-market observation, not a measure of AI displacement. To investigate a Brazilian AI effect, researchers would need to combine PNAD, formal-employment and wage microdata, sectoral adoption, enterprise surveys and occupational task changes, while controlling for interest rates, regional trends, labour demand and the business cycle. Announcing a national AI unemployment crisis from one aggregate rate would be as unjustified as using low unemployment to dismiss a possible loss of junior opportunities. [11]

Brazil also has policy options broader than subsidising foreign-owned data centres. It can evaluate the return on digital infrastructure, technical education, independent research, open language resources, public procurement, SME adoption and the extension of productive technology to agribusiness and manufacturing. Each option has a different route into wages and productive capacity. For social protection, reforms must start with the existing budget, eligibility systems, tax incidence and constraints of municipal service provision. It is not analytically defensible to finance a hypothetical universal grant with a hypothetical future tax on models whose taxable profit has not yet appeared in Brazil.

A further risk is becoming a consumer market that exports payments for compute and intellectual property while importing equipment, with limited local productive learning. The opposite scenario is feasible if domestic firms use affordable models to raise competitiveness and invest in complementary skills and products. Which path occurs depends on contracts, knowledge transfer, financing, grid infrastructure and market access—not national enthusiasm for accelerationist rhetoric.

## 10. Security: technological speed can shorten the interval for human judgment

The same software techniques that support industrial robots and predictive maintenance can alter military sensing, logistics, electronic warfare and targeting. NATO's July 2026 innovation policy explicitly identifies AI, drones and autonomous systems as changing the character of conflict, including work to test, procure and scale new technologies. The document confirms institutional priorities and some programmes; it does not establish that every announced system is operationally successful or that all systems autonomously choose lethal targets. [12]

In August 2026, the UN Secretary-General and the President of the International Committee of the Red Cross jointly appealed for binding rules on autonomous weapons, warning that faster technological development was increasing pressure on meaningful human control and civilian protection. These are international institutional positions on law and ethics, not proof that a new treaty had been adopted. The distinction between a decision aid, a remotely operated weapon and a system that selects and engages targets without further human intervention is essential. [13]

The strategic mechanism is not simply that military machines become more intelligent. Shorter sensor-to-decision intervals can improve defence and generate pressure to delegate decisions when humans are overwhelmed; electronic countermeasures, imperfect identification and adversarial manipulation can also make the speed of action more dangerous. A procurement contract proves an intent to acquire; a test demonstrates a bounded capability; repeated battlefield use may establish deployment; verified consequences require independent evidence. Neither an official release nor a dramatic video is enough on its own.

These risks connect to the economics of acceleration. High defence spending can build industrial capacity, but also redirect engineering talent, semiconductor demand and public finance. Competition between states may encourage investment that civilian markets would not finance, while export controls can increase the cost of scientific collaboration. In a security race, the optimal private speed for a company can differ from the socially desirable speed when failures impose costs on civilians, allies and competitors.

## 11. Psychology and the shrinking apprenticeship ladder

Economic welfare is not only the number of tasks completed. People acquire competence by making decisions, encountering errors, receiving feedback and eventually carrying responsibility. An assistant can widen access to expertise, but reliance without verification may impede learning. Whether it does so depends on the design of teaching and work. Students who must defend a solution independently may benefit from AI feedback; those whose evaluation rewards only polished output may have incentives to outsource the thinking itself. The same ambiguity holds for professional judgment.

Automation can also affect identity. Employment supplies income but may also offer routine, recognition, social networks and an account of one's contribution. If experienced staff remain in charge while routine training tasks disappear, firms must redesign mentoring deliberately. If workers are monitored and rated by opaque systems, loss of autonomy may matter even when output improves. Claims about a universal cognitive decline, mass psychiatric harms or inevitable liberation from work require longitudinal, population-specific evidence and are not established by anecdotes.

This dimension changes the distribution question. A basic income can reduce acute income insecurity yet leave unresolved who controls scheduling, knowledge, platform access, housing or the institutions in which people participate. Conversely, productivity gains accompanied by shorter hours and high-quality public services may improve welfare without every household owning a model company. Freedom has material, political and cognitive components that do not move in lockstep.

## 12. Three distinct international transmission paths

The United States' position combines model development, capital markets, cloud infrastructure, corporate demand and geopolitical influence, but depends on international semiconductor supply chains. China's position combines industrial manufacturing and model development with a different political and regulatory system. European countries face heterogeneous energy and industrial conditions and a distinct debate over rights and regulatory capacity. Emerging economies may face lower immediate occupational exposure yet have less infrastructure to capture benefits. The IMF's estimates highlight the asymmetry without deciding a single future ranking. [1][4]

A policy that encourages low-cost model access may expand adoption in countries with constrained capital, but it does not automatically give them bargaining power over the cloud bill, training data, chip supply or cross-border rules. A restriction justified by safety may reduce a particular risk while strengthening vendors able to pay the compliance cost. An export control may limit one buyer's access while encouraging substitution and new geopolitical dependency elsewhere. These are contingent effects requiring observed trade, pricing, investment and procurement evidence.

| System | Relative opportunity | Main constraint | Distributional question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| United States | Frontier R&D, finance and infrastructure | Market concentration and physical expansion costs | Do gains reach workers and consumers? |
| China | Industrial scale and competitive research | Technology access, energy and regulatory choices | How are industrial gains and public control balanced? |
| Europe | Research capacity, institutions and industrial markets | Heterogeneous energy and investment conditions | Can regulation protect rights without blocking diffusion? |
| Brazil and similar economies | Broad service and industrial adoption potential | Skills, capital, language, grid and foreign-service dependence | Does adoption build domestic capability or deepen imported-service dependence? |
| Low-income economies | Access to low-cost knowledge tools | Connectivity, electricity, education and fiscal space | Are productivity benefits accessible outside digitally connected elites? |

The rows describe mechanisms, not an exhaustive country ranking. Particular countries within a group can substantially differ from its general characteristics. Cross-country comparisons must preserve purchasing-power, labour-market and electricity definitions.

## 13. Four observable interpretations for 2027–2030

**Diffused complementarity.** Model costs decline and AI becomes reliable in complete workflows. Small and medium firms adopt it alongside human skills; competition transfers part of the saving into prices and real wages. Evidence would include broad productivity gains, real income increases across the distribution, resilient early-career hiring, easier supplier switching and wider service access. It would be weakened by persistent labour-share decline and concentration without consumer benefits.

**Concentrated automation.** AI raises output in selected firms, but chip capacity, cloud distribution, capital and enterprise integration determine who obtains the return. Senior workers may capture complementary wages while junior entrants lose opportunities. Evidence would include higher measured value added with weak median wages, reduced entry hiring, larger markups and persistent switching costs. This interpretation would weaken if competition quickly dissipated margins and occupational entry recovered.

**Constrained and securitised deployment.** Technical progress remains strong but energy, physical infrastructure, financing, security incidents and geopolitical controls slow deployment. Benefits become geographically uneven; military procurement competes with civilian uses. Evidence would include stranded or delayed projects, divergent national access costs, trade restrictions, verified security incidents and operational bottlenecks. The scenario weakens if efficiency gains, diversified production and reliable safeguards remove the constraints.

**Institutional adaptation.** Governments and organisations alter training, competition rules, procurement, insurance, taxes and working-time arrangements faster than initially expected. This can overlap the first three scenarios. Its test is whether measurable outcomes improve without unbounded fiscal obligations, suppressed entry or loss of democratic accountability. Institutional design, not the word acceleration, is the independent variable of interest.

No numerical probabilities are assigned: the required causal model and confidence intervals are not established. The three principal paths can coexist across industries and countries. Changes in the rate of technical progress need not be mirrored in the rate of social or political transformation.

## 14. Evidence that would change the assessment

The most consequential near-term evidence is likely to be operational rather than spectacular: a robust multi-country study of adoption matched to firm output; an independent panel of entry-level hiring in exposed occupations; audited financial results establishing where value is captured; evidence of reduced cost per verified task; regional data-centre capacity actually connected to power; and institutional assessments of AI safety procedures and autonomous systems.

A serious research programme must also retain falsifiers for the fashionable narratives. A general unemployment surge would contradict claims that task recomposition remains sufficient; persistent hiring gains and wage growth in exposed occupations would weaken an immediate-displacement thesis. Improved model benchmarks with stagnant aggregate productivity would weaken the inference that capability alone brings abundance. Lower cloud concentration, open interoperability and falling enterprise switching costs would weaken strong monopoly narratives. A measurable social benefit from safety regulation without reduced entry would challenge the view that all safety governance is regulatory capture.

The correct editorial position is neither to celebrate frictionlessness nor to romanticise stagnation. Technical progress expands the feasible set of actions. Property, coordination, political rights, infrastructure and economic institutions determine which of those actions become durable social achievements. In 2026, the evidence establishes a rapidly expanding technological frontier and a still-unsettled distributional result. The most important accelerationist question is therefore who can govern the passage from capability to welfare, and whose interests that passage serves.
