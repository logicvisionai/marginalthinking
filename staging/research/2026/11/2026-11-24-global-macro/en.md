# Global Macro — 24 November 2026

## Global growth is holding up, but industrial output is diverging

*Information cutoff: 20:45, 24 November 2026, America/Sao_Paulo. Market observations use the session stated by each source.*

## Executive assessment

The latest business surveys and industrial-output data do not describe a synchronized global contraction. They show an expansion whose geography and sectoral composition are becoming less even. India and Germany retain strong survey readings, while France is contracting. The October crude-steel release adds a harder, sector-specific comparison: output across 70 reporting economies fell 1.1% year on year, but North America and Africa expanded while the European Union, South America and the Middle East contracted. [1–7, 28–29]

Financial conditions are not tightening uniformly. The Chicago Fed’s National Financial Conditions Index (NFCI) stood at −0.478 for the week ending 13 November, the latest observation available by this edition’s 20:45 BRT cutoff. Negative values indicate looser-than-average US financial conditions; the index is a broad composite, not a corporate credit spread. That reading contrasts with the Institute of International Finance’s estimate of US$33.8 billion in non-resident portfolio outflows from emerging markets in October. The two indicators cover different geographies and financial channels. [15–16, 23–24, 30]

Energy remains volatile rather than resolved. Brent fell 3.46% to US$89.37 per barrel on 23 November as Middle Eastern flows increased and Libya’s Sharara pipeline restarted, then rose 2.27% to US$91.40 in the latest session reported on 24 November amid reports of attacks on vessels around the Strait of Hormuz. Reuters did not quantify a sustained loss of oil output from those reports. The IEA estimates that global oil supply rose by 2.3 million barrels per day in October to 105.4 million barrels per day, while warning that refined-product markets and onshore inventory buffers remain tight. [17–18, 30]

The evidence points to a fragmented expansion rather than an imminent global recession. Surveys, steel output and business sentiment show that the recovery differs by region and sector; financial conditions and delivered energy costs add further differences. The next test is whether this dispersion appears in national-accounts output, employment and investment, not whether one headline index remains above its expansion threshold.

## 1. Activity: a positive aggregate conceals a wider regional gap

The November flash composite PMIs show the dispersion directly.

The indices are diffusion measures from business surveys, not growth rates. A reading of 55 does not mean that GDP grew by 5%. The surveys are useful for identifying direction, breadth and changes in momentum; they cannot substitute for national-accounts releases or establish the size of an output gap.

In the United States, the composite index fell to 54.6 from 55.9 in October, a four-month low. Manufacturing registered 54.5 and services 54.7. Both remained in expansion, but the survey described weaker demand in services, broadly unchanged manufacturing orders and persistent input-cost pressure. S&P Global’s estimate of roughly 3% annualised fourth-quarter GDP growth is a survey-based nowcast, not an official GDP estimate. It should be read alongside the slowdown in the composite index rather than as proof that the economy has accelerated. [1]

The euro-area composite remained at 51.7, but the national split widened. Germany rose to 55.0, its strongest reading since March 2022, with manufacturing at 55.0 and services at 54.0. Export orders supported manufacturers, while domestic services also improved. France moved in the opposite direction: its composite fell to 46.2, manufacturing to 47.9 and manufacturing output to 44.5. French firms reported a seventh consecutive survey period of job cuts and unusually weak confidence. The possibility of a technical recession in France was raised by S&P Global’s economist; it is a conditional assessment, not an observed fourth-quarter GDP result. [2–4]

The euro-area headline consequently masks a material internal divergence. Germany’s strength is not evidence that all member states are recovering. France’s weakness is not evidence that the entire currency area is contracting. The aggregate remains above 50, but the distribution matters for the ECB: a common policy rate is being transmitted to economies with different demand, pricing and labour-market conditions.

The United Kingdom’s composite PMI eased to 52.3 from 52.9. Services fell to 52.0, while manufacturing output rose to 54.2 even though the manufacturing headline slipped to 52.7. Survey respondents cited data-centre construction, defence spending and food and drink as supports for manufacturing. Services export orders weakened, and hiring fell as firms sought to contain costs. S&P Global’s estimate of 0.6% fourth-quarter GDP growth is a survey interpretation; the more useful signal is that domestic industrial demand is stronger than some export-facing services. [5]

Japan’s composite eased to 51.5 from 52.1, a six-month low. Manufacturing improved to 53.7, helped by semiconductor and electronics demand and faster export orders. Services slowed to 50.5, close to stagnation. Manufacturing is expanding while consumer-facing services are close to stagnation, amid labour shortages, higher costs and concern about household spending. [6]

India remained the strongest large emerging-market reading in this group. Its composite PMI edged up to 56.3 from 56.2, and manufacturing reached a nine-month high of 56.0. Services slowed to 55.3, however, and employment moved in opposite directions: factories hired at the fastest pace in seven months while service firms reduced headcount. The data indicate a manufacturing-led expansion, not an evenly distributed acceleration across the economy. [7]

China’s official November manufacturing PMI was not available at the information cutoff. The October industrial and investment data discussed in the 23 November edition remain the latest official baseline; no new Chinese activity observation is inferred here.

The World Steel Association reported 142.4 million tonnes of crude-steel production in October across 70 reporting economies, down 1.1% year on year. Those economies represented approximately 98% of world crude-steel production in 2025. Output from January to October reached 1,509.3 million tonnes, 0.8% below the same period in 2025. The monthly decline was not uniform: North America grew 5.6% and Africa 7.9%, while the EU fell 4.0%, South America 4.1% and the Middle East 27.7%. Asia and Oceania were flat. The year-to-date figures are less severe in some regions, which is why the monthly and cumulative series should not be conflated. [28]

The country data show a similar split. October output rose 3.6% in India, 8.4% in the United States (an estimate) and 28.5% in Viet Nam. It fell 9.6% in Germany, 5.4% in Brazil and 1.6% in China. Germany’s January–October output was still 2.5% above a year earlier, while Brazil’s was 2.3% lower and China’s 2.8% lower. Steel production is not a direct measure of final demand: operating schedules, product mix, exports and inventories also matter. [28]

Germany’s Ifo Business Climate Index rose to 90.6 in November from 90.1 in October. Current conditions increased to 89.4 and expectations to 91.9. The aggregate improvement was not broad-based: manufacturing confidence declined after six consecutive increases and its expectations returned to negative territory, while services and trade improved and construction edged lower. The result qualifies the strong German flash PMI. A positive composite survey can coexist with weakness in a particular industrial branch, just as a monthly decline in steel output does not establish a country-wide contraction. [29]

The latest policy decisions show why similar PMI readings do not imply similar financing conditions.

These are not identical instruments and should not be compared as if they were a single global rate. They nevertheless show the range of nominal policy settings faced by borrowers. The Federal Reserve held its target range at 3.75%–4.00% in October, but the 10–2 vote included two members who preferred a 25 basis-point increase. The ECB’s deposit rate is 2.50%. The Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee meeting ended on 4 November; its decision to raise Bank Rate to 4.00% was published on 5 November. The RBA raised its cash-rate target to 4.85% on 3 November. Brazil held the Selic at 13.75%. The Bank of Japan’s 18 September decision set the overnight guideline at around 1.25%, effective 24 September. [8–13, 27]

The divergence is not simply a matter of central banks choosing different speeds of easing. In Australia and the United Kingdom, policymakers cited broad domestic inflation pressure and the risk that higher energy costs would pass through to other prices. In the United States, the October FOMC statement described resilient activity and elevated inflation; the dissenting votes were in favour of tightening, not easing. In Brazil, the nominal policy rate remains high even as the domestic economy slows. Each central bank is balancing domestic capacity, imported energy, wages and exchange rates; a PMI above 50 does not resolve that trade-off.

Brazil’s November Macrofiscal Bulletin adds a new fiscal and forecast signal. The Ministry of Finance’s parameter grid revised 2027 GDP growth to 1.8% from 2.3%, IPCA inflation to 4.5% from 3.8%, the accumulated Selic rate to 12.93% from 12.67%, the average exchange rate to R$5.34 per US dollar from R$5.23, and the average Brent price to US$95.20 per barrel from US$79.50. These are the Ministry’s macroeconomic parameters, comparing the September and November forecast vintages; they are not realised outcomes or a market-consensus survey. The exchange-rate and Brent figures are annual averages, not year-end or spot prices. The Selic figure is an accumulated rate: the grid derives it from the real rate implicit in the Focus median and converts it to nominal terms using the SPE’s inflation projection. It is therefore distinct from the Focus median for the terminal Selic rate at end-2027, which stood at 13.00% in the 19 November 2026 survey, up from 12.75% one week earlier and 11.75% four weeks earlier. The November grid was closed on 6 November, so it should not be read as incorporating every subsequent market move. [20]

The same bulletin provides a countervailing fiscal observation. The central-government primary deficit for January–September was R$89.7 billion, compared with R$103.2 billion in the corresponding 2025 period in the bulletin’s comparison. Real revenue growth of 6.5% exceeded real expenditure growth of 5.4%. The deficit remains a deficit, but the year-to-date comparison does not support a simple claim that the primary balance deteriorated in every dimension. The more difficult combination is slower projected growth, higher projected inflation and a high policy rate, which limits the room for demand support without worsening the inflation outlook.

The 19 November Focus report, summarized by B3 on 23 November, raised the market median for the 2026 year-end exchange rate from R$5.40 to R$5.55 per US dollar, lifted the IPCA projection from 5.11% to 5.15%, and reduced the GDP-growth estimate from 1.72% to 1.70%. These are market medians for 2026, not the Ministry’s 2027 parameter grid. The Focus report records market expectations; the Macrofiscal Bulletin records the Ministry’s own forecast parameters. ## 3. Markets: equities reverse part of the rebound while credit remains expensive

US equities rose sharply on 23 November: the S&P 500 gained 4.7%, the Nasdaq 6.5%, the Dow 3.0% and the Russell 2000 3.4%. The move ended a seven-session losing streak and recovered just over half of the preceding week’s losses. The rebound was led by technology and AI-linked shares; the Associated Press described the move as a sharp reversal after a week of losses. AP reported that Nvidia’s board reinstated CEO Jensen Huang amid an investigation into possible foreign-spy and national-security concerns; Nvidia shares rose 31.5%. The company-specific event helps explain the concentration of the rebound in AI-linked shares, but it is not evidence of a broad improvement in macroeconomic or financing conditions. A one-day change in equity prices cannot establish that earnings, financing or productivity expectations have been repaired. [23]

The reversal continued in the latest US session described by Reuters on 24 November. The Nasdaq fell 1.4% and the S&P 500 lost 0.50%, while the Dow gained 0.85%. Nvidia shares dropped more than 6% after the previous session’s 31.5% surge, and Anthropic fell more than 6% to below its US$83 IPO price. The report described profit-taking and a rotation toward defensive and traditional sectors as investors awaited US PCE inflation data. The dollar fell 0.44% from a 20-month high; the ten-year Treasury yield was 4.928%, little changed from 4.92% late on Monday. These figures describe the reported session and should not be treated as evidence of a persistent market trend. [30]

The official US Treasury par curve closed on 23 November at 4.63% for two years, 4.92% for ten years and 5.29% for thirty years. The 2s10s spread was therefore 29 basis points and the 10s30s spread 37 basis points. The curve is upward sloping, but long nominal yields remain high. The ten-year yield is not a pure measure of expected policy rates: it also reflects expected inflation, real rates and term premium. [14]

The Chicago Fed’s NFCI was −0.478 for the week ending 13 November, according to the release published on 18 November. The index aggregates 105 indicators spanning US money, debt and equity markets and traditional and shadow banking. It is standardized around a long-run mean of zero; negative readings have historically indicated looser-than-average financial conditions. This is a broad US measure, not a gauge of every borrower or of funding conditions in emerging markets. The Chicago Fed’s calendar scheduled the next weekly release for 25 November, after this edition’s cutoff. [15]

The equity rebound and the NFCI describe different dimensions of financial conditions. Equity prices can recover quickly when a concentrated source of uncertainty is removed, while the NFCI summarizes a wider set of money-, debt- and equity-market signals. Its negative reading indicates that US conditions remained looser than their historical average in the latest published week. It does not rule out elevated long-term borrowing costs, stress for individual borrowers or tighter financing access in emerging markets.

The WSJ Dollar Index rose 0.27% to 99.02 on 23 November, its largest daily gain since 11 November and its tenth advance in thirteen sessions. This is the WSJ index, not the ICE DXY measure. In Brazil, the Central Bank’s PTAX dollar selling rate was R$5.9708 on 23 November. The Ibovespa fell 0.95% to 164,796.05 points, diverging from the US equity rebound as domestic caution and the fall in oil weighed on local shares. These observations show that the global relief rally did not translate into uniform risk appetite across markets. [21–24]

The Institute of International Finance estimated US$33.8 billion of non-resident portfolio outflows from emerging markets in October, after US$19.3 billion of outflows in September and US$27.4 billion of inflows in October 2025. The October figure was the largest monthly outflow since March. It is a monthly estimate, not a daily measure of 23 November trading. It nevertheless shows that the financing environment for emerging markets was already under pressure before the latest US equity rebound. [16]

## 4. Energy and commodities: cheaper crude, persistent product and logistics risk

Brent futures for January delivery settled at US$89.37 per barrel on 23 November, down US$3.20 or 3.46%. WTI settled at US$80.17, down US$3.24 or 3.88%. Reuters attributed the fall to increased Middle Eastern supply and the reopening of Libya’s Sharara pipeline. The market close was lower even though Reuters also reported shipping incidents and claims of attacks in the Gulf. The Houthi movement’s statement about an attack on a Saudi refinery, the UK Maritime Trade Operations’ reports of attacks on shipping, and the different accounts given by Bahrain and Iran must remain attributed to those actors and reporting institutions. The available evidence does not quantify a new, sustained loss of regional oil output from those claims. [18]

The next reported session reversed part of the decline: January Brent futures settled at US$91.40 per barrel on 24 November, up US$2.03 or 2.27%. Reuters linked the move to new reports of attacks on vessels in and around the Strait of Hormuz. The report does not establish a measured loss of crude production or a sustained interruption to exports. The price change therefore records a higher short-term risk premium, not proof that physical supply has already fallen. [30]

The IEA’s November Oil Market Report estimates that global oil supply rose by 2.3 million barrels per day in October to 105.4 million barrels per day. Global observed inventories increased by 52 million barrels during the month. The report also warns that onshore stocks are at their lowest level since at least 2016 and that refined-product markets remain tight. European diesel prices were still 77% above their pre-war level. These observations are compatible: crude supply can recover while product inventories, refining capacity, freight and insurance remain constrained. [17]

A lower Brent benchmark therefore does not translate one-for-one into lower delivered costs for airlines, trucking, agriculture, mining or households. Diesel, jet fuel and marine fuel have their own refining margins and logistics. A renewed interruption around the Strait of Hormuz could raise delivered costs even if the headline crude benchmark initially appears well supplied. Conversely, continued recovery in Middle Eastern exports and the Libyan pipeline restart would reduce the risk premium if the additional barrels reach buyers reliably.

The Indian market for Russian Urals crude illustrates how those flows are being reallocated. Reuters, citing three traders, reported that late-December and January cargoes offered to India were at a US$2–3 per barrel discount to dated Brent on a delivered basis, compared with US$1–2 a few weeks earlier. The traders cited competition from West African and Middle Eastern crude, high shipping costs and export delays. Reliance Industries’ Russian imports rose 9.6% month on month in October to 393,500 barrels per day, according to industry and trade data cited by Reuters. These are trade-source estimates, not a comprehensive customs series. They indicate a change in marginal pricing and procurement, not the disappearance of Russian crude from India’s supply mix. [19]

Food commodities are also diverging. The FAO Food Price Index averaged 133.9 points in October, down 1.2% from September. The cereal index fell 4.5%, while the sugar index rose 6.6% to its highest level since December 2024. FAO linked the sugar increase partly to rainfall-related disruption of sugarcane harvesting and crushing in Brazil’s Centre-South region, as well as concerns about production in India, the European Union and Thailand. The aggregate food index is an international commodity basket, not a measure of retail food prices or household affordability. The meat component also uses a mixture of projected and observed prices in the latest month and can be revised. [25]

The Bank of England’s 5 November Monetary Policy Report, using futures and storage information available in late October, had already noted elevated diesel and jet-fuel crack spreads and European gas storage below its 2018–25 average. Those are not 23 November inventory observations, but they reinforce why the crude benchmark alone is an incomplete measure of delivered energy costs. OPEC+ ministers are due to discuss 2027 output policy on 29 November. [10, 18]

## 5. Transmission: why the same global cycle produces different outcomes

```flow
Regional PMI divergence
  -> different revenue, hiring and inventory paths
  -> different domestic inflation and capacity pressures
  -> divergent central-bank decisions
  -> different yield, currency and refinancing conditions
  -> unequal capital flows and investment capacity

Crude supply recovery
  -> lower benchmark oil prices
  -> partial relief to headline inflation
  -> but diesel, refining, shipping and insurance remain costly
  -> delivered-cost pressure persists in exposed sectors

Broad US financial conditions and emerging-market outflows
  -> higher refinancing and foreign-currency funding costs
  -> weaker investment by marginal borrowers
  -> more selective hiring and procurement
  -> wider regional differences in growth
```

```map
Euro area | PMI 51.7; Germany 55.0; France 46.2; EU steel output −4.0% y/y in Oct | One monetary area contains divergent national cycles; steel output is sectoral, not total industrial demand
Germany | Ifo 90.6; October steel output −9.6% y/y | Services and trade sentiment improved while manufacturing confidence weakened; Jan–Oct steel output remained +2.5%
India | PMI 56.3; October steel output +3.6% y/y | Manufacturing is expanding, but sector output and household demand are not interchangeable
Brazil | October steel output −5.4%; 2027 GDP assumption 1.8% | Industrial production, fiscal expectations and commodity receipts; the Ministry forecast vintage closed on 6 Nov
Gulf and Libya | Brent US$91.40 on 24 Nov | Export flows, shipping security and refined-product costs; reported attacks do not quantify lost output
```

The base case is continued global expansion with wider differences between countries and sectors. The United States, euro area, United Kingdom, Japan and India remain above the PMI threshold, but the latest readings do not show a common acceleration. Germany and India provide stronger industrial signals; France and the Japanese service sector are weaker. Lower crude prices provide some relief to headline inflation, while refined products, credit and exchange-rate channels remain restrictive.

An upside scenario would require the recovery in oil exports and Libya’s pipeline flows to persist, refined-product premiums to narrow, US financial conditions to remain looser than average and the November PMI divergence to become less pronounced. Under those conditions, lower energy costs could support real household income while financing conditions remain broadly accommodative. This remains conditional on observed inventories and delivered prices, not on the Brent benchmark alone.

A downside scenario would combine renewed shipping disruption in the Gulf, persistent diesel and freight costs, a move in the NFCI toward or above zero, and continued portfolio outflows from emerging markets. If a broad tightening in US conditions coincided with further deterioration in French, Japanese service-sector or US new-order indicators, the expansion could weaken beyond the current regional pockets. A lower oil price would not necessarily protect importers if freight, insurance or product availability deteriorated.

The central thesis would be weakened by a sustained narrowing of the PMI gap between Germany and France, the NFCI remaining below zero, renewed positive non-resident flows to emerging markets, and a broad decline in diesel and other refined-product premiums. It would also be weakened if subsequent national-accounts data showed that the flash PMI divergence did not translate into meaningful differences in output, employment or investment.

## Conclusion

The latest data support a fragmented expansion, not a synchronised recession. The October steel release shows a modest global decline alongside clear regional gains and losses; Germany’s business climate improved without a broad manufacturing recovery. The reversal in US equities and the rebound in Brent add to the dispersion in market signals. Monetary policy is not converging toward a single easing path, and credit conditions for emerging-market borrowers remain less settled than headline equity indices suggest.

The relevant risk is a widening gap between economies and borrowers that can finance investment at current rates and those that cannot. That gap can increase before aggregate global GDP turns negative. It is the combination of regional activity, industrial production, credit pricing, currency conditions and delivered energy costs—not any single equity index or oil benchmark—that should determine whether the current expansion is durable.
