# The world history of money: debt, metal, sovereignty and the architecture of trust

Money was not born on a precise date, invented by one people, or fixed in one definitive form. Before struck coins, complex societies measured debts, recorded deliveries, established equivalences, collected obligations and settled payments. After coined metal appeared, people continued to use credit, grain, shells, textiles, written instruments and private promises. In the twenty-first century, a worker's bank deposit, the reserves that bank holds at a central bank, cash and a private digital token may all be described in the same unit even though they embody different claims, debtors and risks.

The evolution of money is inseparable from production and property, but equally from war, taxation, social hierarchy, coerced labour, legal systems, imperial expansion and the ability to accept a stranger's promise. The decisive transformations were not simply substitutions of one material for another. They arose when an individual obligation became acceptable to third parties, when an authority standardized its unit, when payments networks reached beyond local communities and when institutions succeeded — or failed — in maintaining equivalence among different forms of monetary claims.

The long history exposes a persistent tension: the more money expands a society's capacity to finance trade, credit, investment and government, the greater its need to restrain abuses of that power. Excessively rigid systems can strangle activity during crises; systems without credible limits can destroy trust in prices, wages and contracts. Gold did not eliminate financial crises, and fiat issuance does not make such crises inevitable. The quality of a monetary arrangement depends on its rules, productive capacity, balance sheets, legitimacy and the distribution of adjustment costs.

## I. What constitutes money — and what does not

Six functions usually share the same everyday name. A **unit of account** expresses prices, wages, fines and taxes. A **means of payment** is accepted to discharge an obligation. A **medium of exchange** circulates among buyers and sellers. A **store of value** transfers purchasing power through time without guaranteeing its preservation. A **final settlement asset** extinguishes obligations between institutions. **Collateral** makes it possible to obtain finance subject to margin, valuation and risk constraints. An object can perform some of these functions without performing the others.

Divisibility, durability, portability, verifiability, relative scarcity and liquidity lower the costs of using money. None is sufficient by itself to create money. Gold in a territory without reliable markets or authority can be simply a commodity; a non-physical bank entry can pay millions of suppliers. Acceptance depends on a network, contracts and expectations of future acceptance. An asset described as a store of value may still lose purchasing power: stability of denomination does not imply stable purchasing power.

Three monetary domains coexist: **legal money**, recognized by law for specified obligations; **economic money**, actually accepted by economic agents; and **international money**, used for contracts, funding and reserves beyond its original jurisdiction. They can diverge. A state may require national currency for taxes while real estate is informally priced in dollars and importers must settle external liabilities in another currency. Partial dollarisation arises from the gap between legal acceptance, domestic confidence and global liquidity.

Every monetary transaction raises four concrete questions: *who owes? in what unit? through which instrument? when is the obligation finally settled?* A transfer between customers of one bank may be an internal adjustment of balances; a transfer across banks requires interbank settlement. A stablecoin is a private issuer's liability, unlike central-bank cash; by design, bitcoin is not a redeemable claim at par against a particular institution. This hierarchy of monetary promises underlies the entire chronology.

| Instrument | Obligation or anchor | Main acceptance mechanism | Typical vulnerability |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grain or weighed metal | Physical commodity, quality and weight | Usefulness, recognition, markets and standards | Physical costs, adulteration, unstable supply |

## II. Before coins: kinship, reciprocity and accounting

The textbook sequence in which every society starts with barter, moves to commodity money and ends with paper is a theoretical narrative, not a demonstrated universal historical chronology. In *The Wealth of Nations* (1776), Adam Smith explained how the double coincidence of wants complicates direct exchange. In “On the Origin of Money” (1892), Carl Menger modelled the spontaneous emergence of more marketable commodities. The mechanism is coherent: a good accepted by more buyers reduces search costs. But it does not establish that entire early communities operated through pure barter.

Caroline Humphrey and Stephen Hugh-Jones, in anthropological work on barter and value, described large known economies as mixtures of reciprocity, redistribution, credit, direct exchange and authority. Gifts established relationships and obligations; debts could exist without interest and without standardized circulating objects; settlements could involve work, food or services. Barter occurred in particular circumstances, including transactions among strangers or where customary money broke down. Replacing an exclusive metallic origin myth with an exclusive credit origin myth would be no better: both mechanisms could emerge, overlap and change.

Near Eastern clay tokens, some predating writing by millennia, provide evidence of resource counting and record-keeping. They do not, on their own, establish the presence of modern banks or a universal currency. They show why storage, obligations and administration needed quantitative memory. As societies produced surplus and gathered grain stores, herds and workshops in larger organizations, dependence on individual recollection became increasingly costly. Recording *who delivered what, to whom, and when* became an infrastructure of power.

The history of money therefore does not begin with the history of coinage. A common unit could organize a temple's holdings even if workers never received discs or bars of the material in question. Nor should the earliest records be presumed to document symmetrical freedom of contract: dependent labour, punishment, taxation, slavery and social prestige coexisted with commerce and agreements. Accounting made an obligation visible to administrators; it did not necessarily protect the debtor.

## III. Mesopotamia: barley, silver and the recorded obligation

In Mesopotamian records from the third and second millennia BCE, barley and silver played distinct but overlapping roles. Barley could be delivered for subsistence and payment; silver offered high value per unit of weight and a convenient standard for evaluating other goods. A *shekel* initially denoted a weight measure before later identifying particular coins in other settings. The metal's convenience did not remove the need for legal institutions: recognized weights, witnesses, archives, debt collection and interest conventions mattered as much as its physical substance.

Surviving documents attest loans, land sales, labour agreements and obligations expressed in monetary standards. One qualification is essential. Specialist research into archaic Uruk texts does not find sound evidence that silver was already a generalized means of payment at that stage; more secure evidence of silver payments appears in later documents. A notation in silver cannot automatically be treated as a physical transfer. It may represent valuation, debt or an obligation eventually discharged in another commodity.

Urban specialization created interdependent monetary needs. Farmers faced levies and deliveries; craftspeople depended on provisions; merchants financed journeys and extended credit; authorities collected surplus and distributed rations. Ledgers, contracts and standards enlarged coordination but also enabled creditors to accumulate claims against land and households. Emergency loans following poor harvests could become mechanisms through which control of assets shifted, strengthening material inequality.

Ancient financial institutions should not be projected into a fully integrated modern payment system. The existence of deposits and payment orders is not evidence, by itself, of interbank book transfers. Safekeeping other people's property, financing merchants, establishing a unit of value and running transferable balances across a banking network are separate capabilities. They evolved discontinuously and through different arrangements.

Early credit exposes a crucial proposition: **physical scarcity does not guarantee solvency**. A debt denominated in silver can bankrupt a farmer even if silver retains its market value. Nominal interest, harvests, productive conditions, deadlines, enforcement and restructuring determine whether a claim is payable. Monetary history was a history of balance-sheet crises from an early stage.

## IV. Money beyond precious metals: African, Asian and American plurality

Cowrie shells, uncoined metals, salt, cloth, ceremonial objects and agricultural units performed monetary functions in societies across the world. There was no inevitable ladder leading toward European silver. The usefulness of each form depended on commercial networks, accessible supply, divisibility, political recognition and local conventions. An imported shell could be valuable where obtaining it required long-distance transport; discovering new supplies or expanding imports could change its purchasing power.

In West Africa, cowries supplied, among other places, from the Maldives circulated alongside other money forms. Smithsonian collections document their role in both small transactions and large exchanges, including their connection to the transatlantic trade in enslaved people. This complicates any romantic history of monetary expansion. A payment network can facilitate everyday commerce and agricultural markets while also expanding the capacity to buy human beings. Technical efficiency is not morally neutral.

In Mesoamerica, cacao beans, cotton cloth and other goods played commercial and tributary roles in particular contexts. In the Andes, organized labour obligations, storage and state redistribution had central importance without necessarily reproducing Mediterranean coinage. In the Pacific, difficult-to-transport objects could convey recognized social value even if the object did not physically move with each transfer. Transfer of recognized ownership and physical portability need not coincide.

Colonization transformed monetary systems in Africa and the Americas through levies payable in colonial units, paid or coerced labour, control of ports and mines and the imposition of metropolitan standards. When taxes had to be paid in money, households were compelled to sell crops or labour power to obtain it. Monetary integration thus became entangled with labour discipline and the formation of export-dependent economic structures.

## V. Lydia and the major innovation of coin striking

Electrum pieces — made from a naturally occurring gold-silver alloy — struck in Lydia, in Anatolia, around the second half of the seventh century BCE are among the earliest documented coinages. They followed weight standards and displayed marks associated with an issuing authority. The subsequent striking of gold and silver separately reduced an additional uncertainty: electrum's variable alloy composition made the intrinsic precious-metal content of individual pieces difficult to assess.

A stamped seal did not invent the metal's value; it changed the costs of verifying quantity, legitimacy and acceptance. Instead of weighing and testing an irregular piece during every transaction, the recipient could rely — provisionally — on an established standard. Where coins circulated sufficiently, this could reduce time, disputes and the cost of small payments. But the certifying authority also acquired the ability to reduce metallic content, recall older coins, demand taxes and collect seigniorage.

Coinage did not diffuse instantly or uniformly. Greek cities adopted different standards that connected money to civic identity and fiscal capacity; the Persian Empire developed its own arrangements; many users continued to weigh metals. A trusted coin could travel beyond the territory of its issuer when merchants understood its quality and liquidity. Political credibility and information about metal composition reinforced one another, yet remained analytically distinct.

Three explanations compete over why the earliest coins were produced: facilitating trade; paying and taxing through the state, including military expenses; and expressing authority or prestige. Evidence does not justify reducing every first issue to a single motive. Variation across cities and fiscal systems requires case-specific explanation.

## VI. Classical empires: tribute, soldiers, inflation and territorial integration

In the Greek and Roman worlds, coined money became an instrument of market integration, military pay, tax collection and political legitimacy. Army expansion generated demand for a recognizable payment unit; taxes paid in money and public expenditure increased its circulation; mines, levies and conquest supplied metal. Conquest, revenue, wages and provisioning could form a reinforcing system centred on imperial power.

The Roman denarius, different gold coinages and the late Roman solidus did not form one unchanging monetary regime. Weights, precious-metal content, tax arrangements and remuneration all changed. Debasement could temporarily increase the government's nominal spending power, but the response of prices depended on real supply, confidence, circulation, taxation and preferences for better coins. The claim that prices always rise in the same proportion as removed metal is too mechanical.

Distinguishing monetary inflation from collapsing production, territorial losses, civil war and disrupted logistics is essential to interpreting Roman crises. Unsafe routes impair supply; fragmentation undermines fiscal capacity; collection of goods in kind may intensify when coin use becomes less dependable. Monetary disorder can be both a consequence of declining state capacity and a force accelerating it.

Distribution also mattered. Large landholders, military suppliers and well-connected creditors might benefit from imperial circulation, while conquered provinces, enslaved populations and producers exposed to heavy levies bore unequal costs. Greater monetary integration did not settle control over land, military force or access to finance.

## VII. China, India and Japan: monetary histories independent of Europe

China developed an extensive history of bronze coinage, state monetary standards, merchant networks and eventually paper money. Dynasties and regions combined metal circulation, fiscal obligations and credit instruments in different ways. Standardizing coinage during unification under Qin was a political-administrative undertaking. Under the Song dynasty, urban growth and trade increased demand for instruments easier to transport than large volumes of metal coin.

Around 1010, merchants in Sichuan issued *jiaozi*, initially convertible into iron coins; the state later assumed issuance. This was not an exact historical predecessor of today's central bank. Redemption promises, maturity, circulation and fiscal management differed. Under the Yuan, paper money attained imperial scale but excessive issuance, confidence problems and political shocks contributed to its deterioration. The lesson is not that paper invariably fails. Monetary durability depends on fiscal revenues, control of issuance, the credibility of promised redemption and continuing demand.

India's varied traditions, including punch-marked coins and regional monetary systems, evolved alongside trade networks and different political formations. Indian Ocean trade connected money, bills, merchants and intermediaries across diverse legal and religious traditions. Commerce did not require a single universally accepted standard: specialized actors provided conversion services, issued and honoured promises and bore distance and counterparty risk.

Japan shows just how uneven monetary development could be. The Bank of Japan's Currency Museum documents coins predating the Wado Kaichin of 708, including *Fuhon-sen*. It also records periods when minting declined and rice, silk and cloth served as commodity money, as well as credit instruments linked to warehouses. In the Edo period, gold, silver and copper coins coexisted with market exchange rates. In the nineteenth century, the yen and the Bank of Japan, which began operations in 1882, accompanied fiscal reform, industrialization and external integration. None of these histories fits an inevitable universal progression.

## VIII. The medieval Islamic world, the Mediterranean and long-distance credit

Gold dinars and silver dirhams circulated across large Islamic political and commercial spaces. Their usefulness depended on institutions and routes capable of recognizing weights, reputations and contractual claims, not merely on metal content. Merchants operated between Mediterranean ports, Levantine cities, trans-Saharan caravan routes and Indian Ocean markets. Families, courts, trading knowledge and financial partnerships reduced transaction costs without a single monetary sovereign.

Transfer instruments and bills helped reduce the physical risk of carrying value across distances. Shared etymologies between historical instruments and modern banking words must not be mistaken for identical legal institutions. Their practical operation depended on who honoured an obligation, how settlement was confirmed and what happened if an intermediary failed. Contractual security depended on jurisdiction and enforcement.

Medieval Europe relied on silver coins, periodically scarce specie, trade fairs, merchant houses, bills of exchange and deposit banks in Italian cities. Commercial accounting grew more complex as credit, insurance, exchange and partnership finance linked merchants separated by long journeys. The decisive innovation was not merely to manufacture better coins, but to reduce the frequency with which coins had to travel physically.

Financial networks were marked by inequality and legal distinctions. Lenders, merchant dynasties, governments and religious authorities operated within different rules; minorities could be excluded or pushed into particular financial roles. Expulsions, confiscation and sovereign debt repudiation demonstrate that political and legal discrimination belongs in the history of finance alongside contractual creativity.

## IX. American silver, conquest and the first ocean-spanning monetary medium

European conquest of the Americas reorganized the global geography of metals, work and finance. Mines such as Potosí and minting centres in New Spain supplied Atlantic and trans-Pacific networks. The Spanish piece of eight became recognizable across many jurisdictions, including Asian trade. Its acceptance derived from standardization, available supply and usefulness in settling transactions across political boundaries, not from a perfectly unified global monetary order.

The silver flows were not simply the discovery of new wealth. Territorial conquest, several forms of coerced Indigenous labour, enslavement, taxation, merchant intermediation, ocean shipping and imperial competition organized their extraction. Silver was a monetary asset, but its production required energy, tools, land and human bodies. The emergence of a widely circulating international coin was also a history of colonial domination.

The relationship between newly mined metals and Europe's so-called Price Revolution was important but not exclusive. Demographic change, market recovery, shifts in supply and credit mechanisms also affected prices. Early recipients of new money could purchase goods before all prices adjusted; benefits and costs followed expenditure channels and contract structures rather than being distributed proportionally. The later expression “Cantillon effect” captures a distributive intuition, without turning centuries of change into a single-cause explanation.

The link between the Americas and China illustrates a further transformation. Trade imbalances and Asian demand for silver could attract metal extracted through coercion in the Americas toward distant markets. Routes through Manila and the Atlantic made a minted standard a balance-of-payments instrument for regions with different monetary institutions and preferences. International money long predated modern central banks and floating exchange rates.

## X. From public banks to private notes: transferable liabilities

The establishment of the Bank of Amsterdam in 1609 responded to the confusion of heterogeneous coins and inconsistent weights. Its accounts allowed merchants to settle obligations in a recognized bank unit. The gain did not arise from turning metal into wealth by magic; it came from creating a more reliable settlement asset and common measure. Problems of lending practices and fiscal support later weakened confidence in the late eighteenth century, exposing how much sound monetary infrastructure depends on governance.

The Bank of England was founded in 1694, when war financing against France created an urgent demand for public funds. Its emergence connected government debt, tax capacity and commercial banking. State securities entered private portfolios; financial institutions transformed promises into payment instruments; the government received immediate resources against future revenue. A creditor's confidence increasingly depended on enduring institutions capable of taxation and debt administration, rather than the personal word of a monarch.

Commercial banks, discount houses and clearing systems developed transferable liabilities. Deposits were no longer just physical sacks of coins held for safekeeping: they became claims against an institution, subject to rules for withdrawal and payment. This allowed payment capacity to expand without a proportionate increase in physical stocks of gold and silver. It also created maturity mismatch between immediately redeemable liabilities and assets that came due much later.

A bank can be solvent over time but lack cash tomorrow; it can be liquid for the moment while having insufficient net worth. Different mechanisms are needed for these problems. A fractional-reserve system cannot make all deposits simultaneously redeemable in cash during a generalized run without external support or asset liquidation; lending indefinitely to insolvent borrowers destroys prudential incentives. Clearing, supervision, guarantees, resolution and the lender of last resort developed to manage this tension, never to remove it completely.

## XI. Revolutions, paper currency and national-market construction

The eighteenth and nineteenth centuries saw repeated experiments in paper issuance, depreciation, fiscal reform and banking integration. Paper can reduce transport costs and improve transactions, but public acceptance depends on issuance discipline and credible supporting institutions. Episodes such as the French *assignats* reveal the risks of financing public expenditure beyond what trust, productive capacity and taxation can sustain. They do not show that all state money without a metal promise must fail.

Industrialization increased demand for working capital, wage advances, railway finance, international trade funding and long-term credit. Factories cannot operate if every payment requires physical movement of gold from one producer to another. Banks and securities markets expanded the capacity to convert anticipated future revenue into current spending. This supported investment but also amplified crises when expectations of profit and asset values failed.

Nation-states simultaneously moved to establish common monetary standards and curtail competing issues. A uniform unit simplified taxes, contracts, statistics, public debt and procurement. It could also suppress regional autonomy and older payment practices. Monetary standardization was both infrastructure for a larger market and an assertion of sovereignty.

Central banks appeared on different timelines. Sweden developed pioneering institutions for note issuance and public banking; the Bank of England gradually acquired public roles; the Bank of Japan opened in 1882; the US Federal Reserve was only established in 1913. No single day marks the invention of the fully modern central bank. Price stability, bank liquidity support, prudential supervision and payment-system provision accumulated through repeated crises and reforms.

## XII. Gold, silver and the hidden cost of a fixed anchor

The classical gold standard, broadly from the 1870s to 1914, tied national currencies to defined quantities of gold. With convertibility, movement of metal and the defense of parity, exchange rates among members remained relatively stable. This reduced uncertainty for international contracts but imposed constraints: persistent balance-of-payments deficits put reserves under pressure and required adjustments in rates, credit, wages, prices or capital flows.

The choice between gold and silver was also a distributive conflict. Moving toward predominantly gold-based monetary systems changed relative metal prices, financing conditions and the position of debtors and creditors. Where agricultural prices and wages fell amid deflation, fixed nominal debts became more burdensome in real terms. Exchange stability among financial centres did not imply stable employment and incomes in peripheral economies.

Adjustment rarely followed a perfectly automatic textbook rule. Central banks sterilized flows, governments restricted convertibility, markets anticipated official interventions and empires transferred costs to colonies. Gold in a vault could not manufacture political independence, legal certainty or physical production. A fixed anchor might strengthen long-term creditor confidence while worsening recessions when liquidity demand rose.

The outbreak of war in 1914 exposed the institutional character of the arrangement. Countries suspended or restricted convertibility to finance and manage conflict. Attempts to restore prewar parities later confronted economies with altered debt burdens, prices and productive structures. Defending an inherited exchange rate could require unemployment and deflation increasingly incompatible with the political demands of mass societies.

## XIII. The Depression, hyperinflation and managed money

Germany's hyperinflation of 1923 illustrated how debt, fiscal breakdown, lost production, political confrontation and monetary expansion can reinforce depreciation expectations. Households reduce their holdings of domestic currency, increasing spending velocity and demand for alternative assets; the declining willingness to hold money further undermines fiscal and exchange-rate stability. The German case cannot support the claim that every central-bank balance-sheet expansion produces hyperinflation. Institutional and economic conditions differ fundamentally.

The Great Depression exposed the opposite extreme. Bank failures, credit contraction, deflation and declining demand impaired the capacity to service debts. The gold standard constrained policy responses in important countries as authorities sought to defend parity. Exiting gold on different dates and reforming banking systems were not costless solutions, but changed the available policy space. Barry Eichengreen's *Golden Fetters* (1992) linked international gold rigidity to the severity and transmission of the Depression; Friedman and Schwartz's *A Monetary History of the United States* (1963) stressed monetary contraction and banking-system failures.

The United States established the Federal Reserve in 1913 following decades of panics, especially in 1907. The system was not originally designed primarily as a modern inflation-targeting institution. Its early purpose included elastic currency, banking stability and liquidity. The later evolution of the Fed's powers illustrates how monetary institutions respond to specific historical failures rather than implement an immutable theory.

Monetary policy became inseparable from deposit insurance, prudential regulation, public debt management and Treasury capacity. A state may abandon gold convertibility yet remain constrained by inflation, exchange rates, confidence and access to real resources. Leaving metal changes the nature of the constraint; it does not abolish it.

## XIV. Bretton Woods: the dollar, gold and the global liquidity contradiction

In July 1944, representatives of 44 countries at Bretton Woods designed an international monetary system based on adjustable parities, financial cooperation and multilateral institutions. The US dollar became central, with official gold convertibility at $35 per ounce, while other currencies maintained exchange-rate parities against the dollar. The system sought to avoid the competitive devaluations, protectionism and financial disruption of the interwar years while preserving some policy autonomy.

A structural tension remained. As international trade expanded, the rest of the world needed increasing quantities of dollar-denominated liquid assets. Supplying those assets depended in part on the United States issuing liabilities in its own currency. As foreign obligations grew relative to US gold, the pledge to exchange dollars for gold at a fixed rate became less credible. The dilemma associated with Robert Triffin captured the conflict between world liquidity provision and confidence in redemption.

The system unravelled in stages. On 15 August 1971, the Nixon administration suspended the dollar's gold convertibility for foreign monetary authorities; by 1973, major advanced currencies had largely moved to floating exchange rates. The dollar was not instantly displaced by a global basket. Its international position endured because of deep financial markets, liquid public debt, banking networks, trade contracts and coordination effects.

Special Drawing Rights, introduced by the IMF in 1969, are an international reserve asset and unit of account of the Fund, not ordinary consumer currency. An international accounting unit can exist without replacing domestic deposits and cash. Any proposal for a world currency must identify the issuer, redemption obligations, loss bearers, crisis lender and political authority responsible for its governance.

## XV. Modern fiat systems and the creation of deposits by banks

After the collapse of the dollar-gold parity system, fiat money became dominant worldwide. **Fiat** does not mean central banks can decree an unchanged purchasing power. It means the monetary unit is not generally a promise to deliver a fixed quantity of metal. Its value depends on acceptance, productive capacity, macroeconomic management, taxation, law and expectations.

In modern banking, a commercial bank ordinarily creates a deposit when it extends a loan. Its balance sheet simultaneously records an asset — the loan receivable — and a liability — the customer's deposit. When a customer transfers funds to another institution, the originating bank must arrange settlement reserves. Initial deposit creation therefore does not give banks unlimited lending capacity. Regulatory capital, expected profitability, default risk, credit demand, funding prices, settlement liquidity and supervision all impose meaningful constraints.

A simplified balance-sheet example clarifies the process. A bank issues 100 units of credit; its loan assets increase by 100 and its deposit liabilities increase by 100. If the borrower transfers those 100 units to a customer at another bank, the originator must supply an acceptable interbank settlement asset. When the loan is repaid, the loan asset and corresponding monetary liability can decline, subject to other simultaneous transactions. Bank money is elastic but not costless.

A mechanical reserve-multiplier model does not capture this whole process. The Bank of England explains that commercial banks do not merely relend deposits that savers first placed with them, and that most broad money consists of deposits. This does not make real saving and the availability of physical resources irrelevant to investment. The banking system can create means of payment but cannot produce skilled labour, machinery or energy without mobilizing productive factors.

Bank loan granted → deposit created as bank liability → spending and transfer → need for interbank settlement → central-bank reserves and funding conditions → capital, solvency and liquidity constraints → further lending, repayment or default

## XVI. Inflation, deflation, interest rates and confidence

The identity M × V = P × Y relates money, circulation velocity, prices and real output under consistent definitions. As an accounting identity, it does not identify a unique causal direction. An increase in M may accompany real growth, a fall in V, stronger demand for liquid balances or a change in P. Analysis must establish who created the money, where it entered the economy, how real supply responded and whether wages and prices adjusted differently.

Persistent inflation can arise from combinations of nominal demand exceeding productive capacity, energy and food shocks that propagate, unanchored expectations, depreciation, indexation and fiscal constraints. A temporary supply shock need not generate persistent inflation. The duration depends on policy, wage bargaining, competitive conditions, indexation and subsequent shocks. Deflation, meanwhile, can raise the real burden of nominal debts and undermine spending and investment.

Nominal interest is not real interest. Approximately, expected real interest equals nominal interest minus expected inflation; more precisely, (1 + i)/(1 + expected inflation) − 1. A very high nominal rate in a hyperinflationary economy can still imply a low or negative real return. Credit costs also reflect spreads, taxes, collateral, maturity and default risk rather than the policy rate alone.

Monetary credibility is not an autonomous psychological resource. It depends on a government's ability to fund spending without continual inflationary finance, the central bank's conduct, the banking system's resilience, legal continuity, enforceable contracts and the supply of goods. Expectations are social. Households accustomed to decades of daily repricing may anticipate further increases; that learning can persist long after formal monetary reform.

## XVII. Money as distribution, inequality and political power

Inflation does not redistribute income uniformly. Workers facing delayed wage adjustments can lose before their contracts reset; fixed-rate borrowers may benefit when unanticipated inflation reduces real debt; holders of nominal debt securities can lose. Early recipients of new credit may bid for assets before price adjustment spreads through the economy. Higher policy rates may reduce future inflation but also make finance more expensive, hurt debt-intensive industries and benefit some holders of financial claims.

Deflation and monetary tightening distribute costs too. A farmer owing 100 units still owes 100 after the crop price falls. Indebted firms curtail production and employment, deepening recession. Governments choose which groups absorb losses in banking crises: shareholders, subordinated creditors, large depositors, taxpayers or public-service users. The design of settlement and bank resolution contains political decisions about risk and fairness.

A related conflict concerns **seigniorage**: the economic benefit from issuing money, net of costs and conditioned on demand to hold it. In metallic systems it may arise from a difference between face value and minting cost; in fiat arrangements it derives from the capacity of the public issuer to create monetary liabilities and hold assets against them. It cannot expand without bound: as citizens abandon the currency, inflationary revenue can collapse. How this benefit is distributed among the Treasury, central bank, commercial banks and payment firms matters as much as issuance volume.

Control of an accounting unit can also create international hierarchies. Countries with foreign-currency debt suffer when their currency depreciates, because the liability expands in domestic units. Exporters may gain exchange-rate revenues while importers of energy and medicine face higher costs. The same depreciation can improve trade incentives and contract balance sheets simultaneously. Reserve-currency issuers enjoy financing advantages, but those advantages require continued institutional confidence and foreign asset demand.

## XVIII. Brazil: colonial circulation, the Real and instant payments

Brazil's monetary history began with a mixture of circulating pieces, including Portuguese and Spanish coins valued by their metallic content. The réis standard coexisted with shifting issuance, fiscal arrangements and an economy shaped by slavery, agricultural exports, mining and external commerce. Gold extraction within a territory did not ensure widely distributed wealth or accessible credit: transfers abroad, taxation, land concentration and forced labour limited benefits for much of the population.

Under the Empire and First Republic, banks, public debt, exchange-rate policies and issuance reflected conflicts over development, stabilization and state finance. The *Encilhamento* and subsequent policies exposed tensions between credit expansion, speculation, real productive capacity and trust. During the twentieth century, industrialization, external shocks, indexation and fiscal deficits shaped price behaviour. Deficit monetization mattered in several episodes, but chronic inflation cannot be assigned one unchanging cause across all governments.

In the 1980s and early 1990s, several plans failed to break inflation durably. Indexation carried past inflation into wages, contracts and future prices; it protected some agents while perpetuating inertia. In March 1994, the Real Unit of Value (URV) established a temporary unit of account that was not itself a physical payment medium. Prices could be quoted in URV and paid in cruzeiros reais. Establishing a common reference was meant to coordinate expectations and relative prices before introducing a new currency.

On 1 July 1994, one URV became one Brazilian real, equivalent to 2,750 cruzeiros reais. Brazil's central bank reports twelve-month inflation of 4,922% in June 1994, followed by rapid disinflation. The outcome cannot be explained merely by removing zeroes. It combined a transitional unit of account, fiscal measures, exchange-rate policy, coordination, currency distribution, banking reforms and subsequent improvements to the monetary regime. Brazil provides a powerful case of changing **the unit in which people think about prices** before changing the physical means of payment.

The launch of Pix on 16 November 2020 changed the speed and accessibility of transfers in reais. It did not create another Brazilian currency: balances remain in reais and settle through a regulated infrastructure. The innovation was primarily institutional and operational — interoperability, availability, speed and reduced friction — rather than replacement of the unit of account with a new token. Brazilian discussions of tokenized assets and Drex must distinguish pilot projects, regulatory decisions and actual implementation instead of presuming that experimental designs already function as everyday national money.

## XIX. The dollar century: deep markets, global crises and dependence

The dollar's international position rests not solely on US economic size but also on its deep Treasury market, banking networks, trade invoicing, global credit, derivatives, foreign exchange and crisis financing. Reserve holdings create structural demand for dollar assets. At the same time, US monetary decisions affect funding conditions in countries whose citizens do not vote on them.

The eurodollar market — dollar-denominated liabilities and deposits outside the United States — established that global dollar credit is not identical to physical cash issued within US territory. International banks, funds and derivatives generate intricate funding chains. During crises, the ability to roll over dollar liabilities can vanish even where underlying assets retain apparent value. Central-bank swap lines and extraordinary liquidity operations address these vulnerabilities.

During the 1970s, oil shocks, domestic policy decisions and exchange-rate changes challenged price stability. Aggressive US tightening beginning under Paul Volcker in 1979 contributed to disinflation but raised debt pressures on emerging borrowers, including in Latin America, as interest rates and the dollar climbed. The debt crises of the 1980s demonstrate how a currency used internationally transmits a shock from a financial centre to liabilities in peripheral economies.

The Asian crisis of 1997, Russia's crisis of 1998 and the global financial crisis of 2008 involved different mixes of foreign-currency exposure, maturity mismatch, leverage, asset prices and capital flight. Public debt and money issuance alone do not explain them. The decisive questions are **who borrowed in which currency, over what maturity, against what collateral, and with what refinancing needs**. A foreign-exchange crisis can arise from private balance sheets; a banking crisis can require state monetary capacity to prevent the payments system from stopping.

## XX. 2008 and 2020: central banks, financial assets and balance-sheet expansion

The 2008 crisis revealed a financial system in which credit instruments served as collateral, repurchase-agreement assets and the foundations of off-balance-sheet funding. Falling confidence in structured assets triggered collateral calls, forced sales and disappearing market funding. Central banks had to address the movement of liquidity among financial institutions, not only the level of short-term interest rates. Money as a payment medium became inseparable from money-like safe collateral.

Asset purchase programs expanded bank reserves and compressed some financial risk premia. This does not mean that each unit of additional reserves automatically becomes credit or household spending. Reserves generally represent the claims of eligible banks against the central bank; deposits owned by firms and households are liabilities of commercial banks. Transmission depends on the composition of balance sheets and the actions of borrowers and lenders.

During the 2020 pandemic, supply shutdowns, collapsing initial activity, fiscal interventions and extraordinary monetary responses occurred together. Subsequent inflation across different countries reflected interacting forces including demand recovery, disrupted logistics, energy, labour and policies, with country-specific weights. It is inadequate to reduce the episode to “money printing”; it would be equally inadequate to deny the role of monetary conditions and nominal demand.

Asset-price increases can reward existing wealth holders and enlarge wealth inequality, particularly when housing supply is constrained and borrowing favours people who already own collateral. But not every rise in equity or housing prices can be attributed to central-bank purchases. Housing supply, demography, productivity, taxes, real rates and expected income must enter the same causal assessment.

## XXI. The euro, China and the limits of a post-dollar order

The euro's introduction as an accounting currency in 1999 and physical cash in 2002 constituted an unusual monetary union among states with different fiscal policies and labour markets. Eliminating foreign-exchange costs and integrating payments did not erase productivity differences, public debt or banking fragilities. The euro crisis beginning in 2010 showed how a monetary union without sufficiently developed banking support, supervision and fiscal coordination can intensify differences between members.

Internationalization of China's renminbi depends on the scale of China's economy but also on openness, financial-market depth, legal predictability, convertibility, cross-border capital mobility and foreign confidence. Demand to invoice commercial transactions in a currency does not automatically produce demand to hold that currency in large official reserves. This separation of invoicing, funding and reserves prevents trade rankings from predicting monetary leadership mechanically.

In the second quarter of 2026, the IMF recorded a US-dollar share of 56.70% in reported official foreign-exchange reserves, compared with 20.60% for the euro and 2.11% for the renminbi. These figures measure **foreign-exchange reserve composition**, not all monetary gold, all international payments or the entire global financial system. Dollar dominance persisted despite modest diversification. A serious challenger would have to offer not just a geopolitical symbol but abundant, liquid and trusted safe assets, funding capacity and markets that remain accessible during crises.

Debates about alternatives among BRICS members, regional blocs and trade partners often mix four distinct proposals: settling bilateral trade in local currencies, defining a common accounting unit, building a clearing infrastructure and creating a genuinely new sovereign or supranational currency. Each requires different institutions. A payments platform may reduce operational dependence on an existing network without eliminating currency conversion, exchange-rate exposure or reserve needs.

## XXII. Digital rupture: Bitcoin, stablecoins and private money's return

Satoshi Nakamoto's 2008 paper proposed an electronic way to transfer value without reliance on a central trusted ledger operator. Bitcoin demonstrated that distributed consensus can maintain a public transaction history with known issuance rules. It did not show that volatility disappears, that operational security is costless or that every legal obligation can settle in a cryptoasset. An asset with a fixed or predictable quantity can still move sharply in price relative to dollars, reais or goods.

Programmable networks and smart contracts made it possible to represent and transfer tokens, collateral and digital rights. Legal ownership and technical control remain different questions. Possessing a cryptographic key does not guarantee an enforceable claim against an insolvent issuer; tokenizing real estate does not replace land registration, jurisdiction or contractual enforcement. Programmability reduces some transaction costs while introducing software errors, validator concentration, key loss, fraud and dependence on external data.

Stablecoins promise a stable relationship with a reference currency, overwhelmingly the dollar. Their quality depends on reserves' composition, liquidity, segregation and auditability, legal redemption rights, insolvency treatment and custody infrastructure. A token can transfer around the clock yet fail when holders demand cash simultaneously. In the United States, the GENIUS Act became law on 18 July 2025; passage of legislation should not be confused with full enforcement and achieved policy outcomes.

The Bank for International Settlements assesses next-generation arrangements against the **singleness of money**, **elasticity** and **integrity**. Its 2025 and 2026 work argues that tokenization of bank deposits, government bonds and central-bank reserves can preserve a public anchor while improving settlement. This is an institutional assessment, not proof that every tokenized architecture will succeed. Private networks, banks and states are competing over ledger control, interoperability costs, data and seigniorage distribution.

## XXIII. Central bank digital currencies: freedom, control and access

A central bank digital currency, or CBDC, is not synonymous with instant payments, mobile banking or cryptocurrency. Designs may target financial institutions, the public or both. They may use accounts or tokens, differing privacy arrangements, holding limits and offline functions. The decisive features are the liability's legal identity, redemption or transfer rules, settlement finality and oversight.

The digital euro remained in technical preparation after the European Central Bank's October 2025 decision. Issuance would require a subsequent European legislative process and decision. It cannot be described as already issued for public circulation. Experiments elsewhere can demonstrate technical possibilities, but effective adoption requires public use, merchant acceptance, reliability and integration with banks.

Potential benefits include broader access to public money, resilient infrastructure and greater payment competition. Risks include surveillance, digital exclusion, operational concentration and abrupt shifts from private deposits into central-bank liabilities during banking panics. Holding limits, distribution via private intermediaries and legal privacy protections can reduce some risks while limiting usefulness. Cryptography alone cannot resolve the tension; legal rights, enforceable limits and institutional accountability are essential.

The deeper social issue is who can pay and be paid without arbitrary denial. Physical cash provides a measure of privacy and independence from networks but faces theft and logistics costs. Digital payments can support inclusion where they are affordable and accessible; they can also exclude those without documents, devices, connectivity or platform acceptance. Resilience demands a plurality of payment methods, operational redundancy and proportionate supervision.

## XXIV. The historical register: currencies are born politically and die economically

A global database published on 6 November 2026 by Iñaki Aldasoro, Jon Frost, Rudraksh Kansal and Catherine R. Schenk as BIS Working Paper 1392 catalogued **568 official currencies** between 1850 and 2026, including colonial, territorial and wartime currencies. The count circulating at a given time rose from roughly **67 in 1850** to a peak of **192 in 1994**, after the post-Soviet transformations, then fell to **163 in 2026**, in part because the euro replaced national currencies.

These three points are selected observations, **not a reconstructed annual time series**. A line connecting them does not describe developments in every intermediate year. The dataset concerns official currency units as defined by its methods, not every monetary object, cryptocurrency, bank or private payment instrument. Around 70% of the recorded currencies were discontinued; median lifespan was 33 years; independence accounted for 28% of issuance reasons. Name or unit changes accounted for 35% of discontinuations; combined with inflation, they represented 55%.

The database alters the long-run perspective. Currency issuance frequently establishes or expresses a new jurisdiction: independence, imperial breakup, territorial reorganization or political legitimacy. Currencies disappear through redenomination, instability or adoption of another monetary standard. Longevity is not a direct index of freedom or prosperity. Authoritarian systems may maintain durable currencies; democracies may enter currency unions; and states may reform units without a productive breakdown. Lifespan measures survival of an official unit, not institutional quality.

Ultimately, a currency is a record of sovereignty carrying a society's memory. A new name can seek to interrupt inherited expectations, but credibility requires sufficient change in the supporting economic and institutional system. Its fate depends on the ability to preserve contracts, intelligible prices, safe settlement and fiscal and banking constraints consistent with real growth.

## XXV. The enduring variables: a matrix of monetary causality

Across periods, eleven variables can be followed without forcing very different settings into one supposedly universal model. **Productive capacity** determines the supply of real goods and services that purchasing power can mobilize. **Fiscal capacity** affects whether public issuers can honour obligations without persistent inflationary taxation. **Liability structure** reveals maturity, currency denomination, indexation and collateral. **Legal quality** shapes enforcement, losses and ownership. **Payment infrastructure** changes the time, cost and risk of final settlement.

**Concentration of issuance** affects who captures seigniorage. **Liquidity elasticity** shows whether a system can meet a sudden demand for safe payment means. **External openness** determines access to foreign currency and foreign funding. **Political legitimacy** affects tax acceptance and the sacrifices involved in stabilization. **Wealth distribution** determines who can hedge risks. **Ledger technology** changes the cost of verifying and transferring rights. None of these variables determines every other one, and effects change over the economic cycle.

The most general crisis mechanism occurs when nominal promises increase faster than perceived capacity to honour them, and holders simultaneously seek conversion into something else: goods, foreign currency, metal, deposits at another bank, safe securities or physical cash. Runs can affect metallic currencies, commercial banks, sovereign liabilities and stablecoins. The differences lie in who supplies final settlement, which assets are marketable and who ultimately bears losses.

## XXVI. Five competing monetary futures

**Continuation of the two-tier system.** Private banks continue to create deposits and extend credit under public rules while central banks anchor the unit of account and ultimate settlement. Instant payments and tokenization reduce costs without overturning the monetary hierarchy. Evidence for this outcome would strengthen if interoperability and competition improved without undermining confidence; concentration of access and data among dominant intermediaries would weaken it.

**Fragmentation among geopolitical blocs.** Sanctions, rivalry, data control and regional agreements encourage local-currency trade and alternative networks. The result could be more resilience but higher exchange costs and collateral needs. The proposition requires evidence of sustained shifts in contracts, funding and reserves, not merely diplomatic announcements about new platforms.

**Expansion of dollar stablecoins.** People in countries with unstable local money gain access to digital instruments linked to the dollar. Such instruments may improve payment choices and individual hedging while reducing domestic deposit demand and monetary-policy autonomy. Critical evidence will be redemption stability during stress, holders' legal protection and effects on banking credit and fiscal revenues.

**Competitive public digital money.** CBDCs and interoperable public settlement infrastructure expand access and standardize payments while bringing possible concentration and surveillance risks. Adoption will depend on privacy law, offline resilience, data limits, voluntary acceptance and integration cost. Technical feasibility is not a substitute for democratic legitimacy.

**Regulated monetary plurality.** Physical cash, deposits, deposit tokens, stablecoins and decentralized assets coexist for different purposes. Healthy plurality would require intelligible convertibility, insolvency rules, transparency of backing and exposure controls. If every payment forces recipients to investigate the private issuer's solvency, some of the efficiency historically delivered by a common currency disappears.

## XXVII. The unresolved monetary problem

The first transformation was the representation and measurement of obligations; the second, the issuance of objects accepted beyond personal ties; the third, the organization of transferable credit and settlement among intermediaries; the fourth, the construction of public anchors supporting an elastic financial system; the fifth, the digitization of records and competition among payment architectures. None entirely eliminated its predecessor. Metal remains a reserve asset; cash still protects some individual autonomy; accounting and debt remain at the centre of digital transactions.

Money's future will not be settled solely by protocol speed or the mathematical scarcity of a token. The decisive question is which institutions can turn private promises into accepted payments, absorb shocks without arbitrarily assigning losses and prevent the privilege of issuance from undermining people's ability to buy, save and invest. That involves choices about banking competition, public budgets, privacy, financial citizenship and international dependence.

A currency can endure for centuries without retaining the same value, form or political framework. It can also disappear within weeks while remaining physically intact. What persists is not the object, but the need to establish a common measure for human obligations — and to ensure that a promise made today remains understandable and acceptable tomorrow.
