# Global Macro — The inflation test after resilient US hiring: markets enter a data gap, not a new trading day

**8 November 2026 · Sunday, Brasília time · Information cutoff: 05:00 BRT.** This is a weekend edition. Regular US, Brazilian and major European cash-equity markets did not open a new Sunday session. Market observations below are Friday 6 November closes or the latest institutional releases available before the cutoff. The October US employment report was published on 6 November; the October Consumer Price Index is scheduled for Tuesday 10 November and has not yet been observed. [1][2]

The information gain is not a new Sunday price. It is the sequence of evidence now facing monetary policy. US payrolls grew by 165,000, while unemployment remained at 4.1%. That is inconsistent with an immediate collapse in hiring, but it does not settle the inflation question. A second release is still ahead, and the latest productivity data complicate the wage signal: output per hour rose strongly in the third quarter while unit labour costs fell on a quarterly annualised basis. The distinction matters because a resilient labour market can coexist with less pressure on firms' labour cost per unit of output. [1][4]

The three preceding editions concentrated on industrial activity, the repricing of technology shares and the payroll surprise. This edition connects the labour and productivity releases to the next CPI test, the Treasury curve, credit spreads, emerging-market portfolio flows, China's official manufacturing survey, energy and food prices. The result is not a forecast of a Federal Reserve decision. It is a map of the variables that can change that decision and the channels through which the same US data may affect economies with different monetary and fiscal constraints.

## Labour: payrolls, wages and productivity tell different stories

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported a 165,000 increase in nonfarm payroll employment in October. The unemployment rate was 4.1%, little changed, and the number of unemployed people was 7.1 million. Employment gains were concentrated in professional and business services, health care, and transportation and warehousing. The report therefore describes continued hiring in selected service and logistics activities, not an acceleration shared by every industry. Mining, construction, retail trade, information, financial activities and government showed little change over the month. [1]

The establishment and household surveys measure different things. Payrolls count jobs reported by employers; the household survey estimates the labour-force status of people. Their estimates need not move together in a single month. The August and September payroll estimates were revised upward by a combined 84,000, a historical correction that changes the earlier path but must not be added to October's 165,000. Average hourly earnings increased 0.4% in October and 3.1% over twelve months. The average workweek remained 34.4 hours. These figures show nominal wage growth, not the change in real purchasing power, and they do not identify the source of any future price increase. [1]

The preliminary third-quarter productivity release provides a second test. Nonfarm business labour productivity increased 3.6% at a seasonally adjusted annual rate from the previous quarter, as output rose 3.7% and hours worked increased 0.1%. Productivity was 2.1% above the same quarter a year earlier. Unit labour costs fell 0.7% at an annual rate during the quarter because productivity growth exceeded the increase in hourly compensation; over four quarters, however, unit labour costs were still 2.4% higher. A quarterly decline is not proof that labour-cost inflation has disappeared. It is evidence that the latest quarter's output-per-hour gain temporarily absorbed the rise in compensation. [4]

The sectoral split is important. Manufacturing productivity increased only 0.3% in the quarter, while manufacturing unit labour costs rose 2.7%; over four quarters, manufacturing unit labour costs were 4.0% higher. Durable-goods productivity increased 2.2%, but nondurable-goods productivity fell 1.7%. A single aggregate productivity figure therefore cannot be applied uniformly to factories, services or the firms most exposed to imported inputs. The report also incorporates revised national-account and compensation data, so the preliminary third-quarter estimate should be treated as a current vintage, not a permanent historical series. [4]

## Monetary policy: three central banks, different constraints

The Federal Reserve held its target range at 3.75%–4.00% on 28 October. The vote was 10–2: two members preferred an increase of 25 basis points. The statement described activity as expanding at a strong pace, domestic spending as resilient and inflation as still elevated. The dissent is evidence of disagreement within the Committee, not a decision to raise rates at the next meeting. The Committee said that further information would guide the appropriate stance. [6]

The European Central Bank also kept its rates unchanged on 29 October. The deposit facility remained at 2.50%, the main refinancing rate at 2.65% and the marginal lending rate at 2.90%. The ECB described higher energy prices as an upside risk to inflation and a downside risk to growth, while noting that financing conditions had tightened since the previous meeting. Its meeting-by-meeting approach is not equivalent to the Federal Reserve's internal debate: the two institutions face different energy exposure, wage dynamics, fiscal structures and credit transmission. [7]

Brazil's Copom maintained the Selic target at 13.75% on 4 November. Its statement cited a more adverse external environment, tighter policy in advanced economies, higher volatility in asset and commodity prices, moderating domestic activity and inflation measures above the upper limit of the target tolerance interval. Market expectations collected by the Focus survey had moved to 5.2% for 2026 and 4.8% for 2027, according to the statement. These are expectations, not realised inflation or the Central Bank's own forecast. The high nominal policy rate also cannot be compared directly with the Fed target without considering inflation expectations, currency risk and the domestic credit structure. [8]

Fiscal policy enters through the supply of sovereign debt, debt-service costs and the ability to cushion energy or food shocks. The information set available for this Sunday does not justify attributing the Friday Treasury move to a single fiscal announcement. Nor does it establish a new fiscal package in the United States, euro area or Brazil. The relevant distinction is between a government's budget constraint and a specific policy action: the former shapes the background price of duration, while the latter requires a dated decision and an identifiable financing source.

## Markets: equities, yields, credit and currencies

The US equity rebound on Friday did not erase the week's repricing. The Nasdaq Composite rose about 1.1% on 6 November but fell 3.4% over the week, its fourth consecutive weekly decline. The S&P 500 gained 0.32% on Friday and lost 2.7% for the week. The contrast between the daily rebound and the weekly decline is not contradictory; the two figures measure different intervals. The move followed a sharp technology and semiconductor selloff, but an index decline does not by itself establish that data-centre utilisation, chip orders or AI revenues have deteriorated. Those claims require company filings, order data and realised operating results. [14]

The US Treasury par curve ended 6 November at 4.51% for two years, 4.86% for ten years and 5.26% for thirty years. The ten-year minus two-year spread was therefore 35 basis points. This arithmetic slope is not a direct measure of the term premium and does not provide a recession probability. Short yields reflect expectations for policy and near-term inflation; long yields also incorporate duration supply, inflation risk and uncertainty over future real rates. A movement in the curve cannot be assigned to one component without a decomposition. [3]

Credit provides a useful countercheck to the equity narrative. The ICE BofA US high-yield option-adjusted spread was 3.48% on 5 November, after 3.32% on 4 November. The observation is a US below-investment-grade bond spread over a Treasury curve, not a global credit index. The increase deserves monitoring, but the level alone does not establish a funding crisis or a broad default cycle. It is also important not to mix the 5 November spread observation with a later update that was not available at the Sunday cutoff. [5]

Foreign-exchange references show how the dollar channel can reach different markets, but reference rates are not executable transaction prices. On 6 November the ECB's euro reference rate was US$1.1165 per euro; the same reference table recorded R$6.1449 per euro, ¥176.77 per euro and CNY7.4485 per euro. These cross rates do not measure capital flows or the cost of hedging a specific borrower. They do show why a single dollar headline is insufficient: currency effects differ by trade exposure, local interest rates, reserve policy and the maturity of foreign-currency liabilities. [13]

The Institute of International Finance estimated that non-resident portfolio investors withdrew US$33.8 billion from emerging markets in October, compared with US$19.3 billion of outflows in September and US$27.4 billion of inflows in October 2025. This is a monthly estimate, not a measure of Sunday trading or a complete balance-of-payments account. It nevertheless shows that the financing environment for emerging markets was already less supportive before the next US CPI release. The data do not establish that every country experienced outflows or that the same investors sold local bonds and equities in equal proportions. [12]

## China and industrial activity: weak breadth beside resilient sectors

China's official October manufacturing PMI fell to 49.3, below the 50 threshold that separates a broader expansion from a contraction in surveyed conditions. The production sub-index was 49.8 and new orders were 49.4; both were below 50. The employment index was 48.4. At the same time, the main raw-material purchase-price index was 56.8 and the producer-price index was 51.7. The combination points to weak demand breadth alongside continued input-cost pressure in the surveyed manufacturing sector. It is not a direct measure of industrial output, corporate profits or national-account growth. [10]

The non-manufacturing business-activity index was 49.7 and the composite output index was also 49.7. New orders in non-manufacturing stood at 44.4. The survey therefore does not support a simple claim that services fully offset manufacturing weakness. Nor does it show that all Chinese industries are contracting: large manufacturers remained above the threshold at 50.2, while medium and small firms were below it. Firm size and sector composition matter for supplier bargaining power, credit access and employment. [10]

This matters for global markets because the demand for commodities, freight and imported capital goods is not determined by one headline PMI. A decline in new orders can weaken procurement before it appears in production data; inventory adjustment can delay the effect on imports. Conversely, a rise in input prices can reflect supply costs rather than stronger final demand. The survey is a leading indicator, not a physical trade-flow dataset. A conclusion about iron ore, copper, semiconductors or energy imports would require the relevant customs and production series.

## Energy, food prices and external financing

Brent futures settled at US$98.32 per barrel on 6 November, according to Reuters' market report. That quotation is a futures settlement, not the delivered price paid by an importer or proof of a physical cargo interruption. The distinction matters for fiscal and inflation analysis: exporters receive prices linked to contract terms and quality differentials, while importers face freight, insurance, refining margins, exchange rates and domestic taxes. A change in the benchmark can therefore pass through unevenly. [14]

The FAO Food Price Index averaged 133.9 points in October, down 1.2% from September but 5.9% above its level a year earlier. The cereal price index fell 4.5% month on month, while the sugar index rose 6.6% and reached its highest level since December 2024. A global commodity index is not a measure of household affordability, nutritional status or retail prices in a particular country. Exchange rates, processing, transport, market concentration and social transfers determine how international prices reach households. [11]

For commodity exporters, a softer cereal or crude benchmark can reduce import costs for some inputs while lowering export receipts elsewhere. For net food and energy importers, lower benchmark prices may help the current account, but the benefit can be offset by currency depreciation, freight and domestic distribution margins. The IIF's October outflow estimate adds a financial channel: when portfolio capital leaves emerging markets, local-currency funding and foreign-currency refinancing can become more expensive even if a country's commodity import bill improves. This is a mechanism to test, not a universal outcome. [11][12]

## Regional transmission and scenarios

```flow
Resilient US payrolls + firm nominal earnings → labour-income and demand assessment
Productivity growth → output per hour → unit labour cost pressure
October CPI (not yet released) → inflation breadth assessment → expected Fed path
Treasury yields + credit spreads + currency hedging → refinancing costs and equity discount rates
Emerging-market portfolio flows + energy and food prices → external funding and household purchasing power
```

The arrows describe channels, not estimated causal coefficients. Productivity can absorb wage growth only if it is sustained and broad enough to affect firms' unit costs. A single quarterly estimate cannot establish that condition. Likewise, a rise in Treasury yields may affect dollar borrowers, but the realised cost depends on hedges, debt maturity, collateral and access to domestic funding.

```map
title: Regional exposure to the next US inflation test
United States | Labour, inflation and policy expectations | Payrolls and Q3 productivity are observed; October CPI is still pending
Euro area | Energy, sovereign yields and common monetary policy | ECB held rates; national activity and fiscal positions differ
China | Manufacturing orders and input costs | Official PMI is below 50; it is not a measure of physical output
Brazil and Latin America | Selic, currency and portfolio funding | Domestic inflation expectations interact with external flows
Commodity importers and exporters | Oil, food and freight | Benchmark prices do not equal delivered costs or household prices
```

The base case is not a forecast of recession or a rate increase. It is a period in which the next US inflation observation has unusually high information value because the labour market remains resilient while productivity and unit labour costs send a more mixed signal. An upside scenario for risk assets would combine a broad moderation in CPI, continued productivity gains, stable credit spreads, less negative emerging-market flows and lower delivered energy costs. That combination would reduce the chance that the Fed needs to respond to wage growth with a more restrictive path.

A downside scenario would require more than a single high CPI print. It would involve broad price acceleration across services and goods, a renewed rise in unit labour costs, a persistent widening of credit spreads, continued portfolio outflows and a rise in delivered energy or food costs. If these conditions occurred together, higher discount rates could coincide with weaker final demand. That would be more damaging than a repricing confined to technology valuations.

The interpretation would be weakened if October CPI were broadly soft, subsequent labour data showed no acceleration in compensation, productivity remained strong across sectors, high-yield spreads stabilised and emerging-market portfolio flows returned to positive territory. It would also be weakened if China's new orders and non-manufacturing activity recovered without a renewed rise in input prices. These are observable tests. No scenario probability is assigned because the available evidence does not support a calibrated forecast.

## Evidence boundary

There was no new Sunday cash-market session. The latest Treasury curve, equity prices and most credit observations refer to Friday or the last available business day. The IIF flow figure is a monthly estimate; the ECB currency table is a reference rate, not a transaction quote; the FAO index tracks internationally traded food commodities rather than household nutrition; and China's PMI is a diffusion survey rather than a physical output measure. No complete, comparable panel of small-market credit spreads, daily foreign-exchange flows, physical Gulf cargo interruptions or strategic inventories was verified by the cutoff. The report does not fill those gaps with proxy numbers.

The central conclusion is narrower than a claim about the next Federal Reserve decision. October hiring was resilient, but the inflation implications depend on productivity, unit labour costs, price breadth, credit conditions and external financing. The CPI release scheduled for 10 November is the next test, not an outcome that can be stated in advance.
