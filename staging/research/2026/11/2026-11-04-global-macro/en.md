# Global Macro — Higher bond yields and divergent interest-rate decisions expose the limits of oil relief

**4 November 2026 · Brasília early edition · Information cutoff: 00:39 BRT (03:39 UTC).** The complete market and macroeconomic observations below refer to Tuesday, 3 November, or explicitly dated earlier releases. The US congressional election outcome was not established as final at this cutoff. Brazil's 4 November Copom decision, new US services and employment releases, and the Eurostat October inflation flash are forthcoming rather than observed results. [1][2][7][9]

The opening of 4 November presents three simultaneous signals that cannot be compressed into a single risk-on or risk-off narrative. US September job openings reached **7.5 million**, an indication that labour demand has not collapsed, even though hiring stood at **5.4 million** and layoffs at **1.6 million**. The US ten-year Treasury constant-maturity yield finished 3 November at **4.95%**, four basis points above the 2 November observation, while the Australian central bank **raised its cash rate by 25 basis points to 4.85%**, explicitly citing stubborn inflation and an investment-led demand component. [1][2][3]

Oil futures moved in the opposite direction: Brent settled at **$103.60 per barrel**, down about 1%, following the unusually large fall of 2 November. Yet Brazil's Ibovespa lost **1.42%**, reaching **165,528.92 points**, as Petrobras and domestic financial conditions weighed on the index. The US S&P 500 and Nasdaq finished higher while the Dow declined. Cheaper futures energy, a US technology rebound and a stronger labour-demand reading did not produce synchronized financial easing across regions or sectors. [4][5][6]

The conflict is now between the prospective disinflation from lower energy prices and the already embedded cost of financing capital, government debt, households and infrastructure. The governing questions are not whether crude declines on a particular day, but whether physical supply normalizes; whether nominal yields fall sustainably; and whether central banks can reduce financing costs without reigniting domestic inflation.

## 2. American labour demand: vacancies are not payrolls

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported September **job openings at 7.5 million**, little changed in its statistical characterization, with a **4.5% vacancies rate**. The August number was revised to **7.2 million**. September hiring totaled **5.4 million**, and overall separations **5.3 million**. Quits rose to about **3.4 million**; layoffs and discharges remained approximately **1.6 million**. These are monthly flow and end-of-month stock-like vacancy measures, not a single internally additive employment balance. [1]

The distinction explains why vacancies can look resilient without strong net employment growth. An unfilled job advert reflects employer demand at a point in time, but some vacancies are filled slowly, suspended or repeated. Gross hires and gross separations can both be large while the change in net employment is small. Quits indicate workers' willingness and ability to change employer, but a higher number does not by itself prove improving real wages. Moreover, surveys are revised as establishments report additional data and seasonal factors change.

```chart

```

For monetary policy, resilient vacancies reduce the force of a sudden employment-collapse argument. They do not show the rate of wage inflation, hours worked, or payroll gains in October. Those require separate datasets. A labour market can simultaneously preserve a meaningful number of openings and impose strain on sectors exposed to long-term borrowing costs, commercial real estate or capital-intensive projects. The latest JOLTS therefore argues against mechanical recession certainty and against premature declarations that labour tightness has ended.

Households experience the transmission through a different set of prices from those observed in equity markets: credit-card costs, mortgage rates, energy, rent and the availability of jobs. High Treasury yields can coexist with strong share prices when earnings expectations favour particular companies; they can still restrict homebuyers and smaller firms. The socially relevant outcome depends on whether nominal pay rises faster than essential expenses and finance charges. [1][2][6]

## 3. Australia: tightening in a world expecting cheaper fuel

On 3 November the Reserve Bank of Australia voted **six to three** to raise its cash rate target from **4.60% to 4.85%**. The board described inflation as broad-based, raised its projected inflation path and indicated that elevated fuel costs and domestic capacity constraints had spread into prices. Its statement also identified strong AI-related and data-centre investment as a contributor to technology-goods price pressures and overall business investment. [3]

This decision complicates the proposition that lower international crude futures must quickly produce lower policy rates. The RBA is responding to inflation already present in domestic price setting. If firms raised prices after higher transport and energy expenses, one lower crude settlement does not reverse the contracts, wage discussions or expectations underlying those increases. Australian housing, investment and household consumption already face pressure from restrictive rates. Raising the policy rate can reduce new borrowing and spending, but it can also deepen the activity slowdown before inflation fully returns to target.

The three dissenting votes favoured leaving the rate at 4.60%, not reducing it. That is substantive evidence of disagreement over the marginal need for tightening, rather than a split between tightening and easing. The board said the risks remain uncertain; subsequent decisions are conditional on incoming inflation, wages and activity. The fact that data-centre investment strengthens selected components of demand while housing weakens shows why a technology capital-expenditure cycle cannot be read as a general rise in welfare or productivity. [3]

## 4. Crude oil: falling futures do not settle the supply shock

Reuters documented a second consecutive decline in crude futures on 3 November: **Brent $103.60** and **WTI $96.09** at settlement. Diplomatic contacts involving Iran, the United States and Qatar weighed against additional risks from attacks in the region; negotiations on the war involving Russia and Ukraine also influenced expectations. Reporting that the parties remain far from an agreement prevents a price move from being reclassified as a concluded peace settlement. [5]

Oil has three separate states relevant to inflation: the price of a futures contract, the quantity of crude and refined products physically reaching markets, and the inventory available to bridge a disruption. These can move at different speeds. A fall in the geopolitical risk premium may reduce futures before shipping insurance premiums, refinery utilisation or product availability improve. Diesel and jet fuel prices can remain constrained even when crude contracts fall, particularly where refining or transport capacity is impaired.

```flow
Diplomatic expectations change → Oil futures risk premium falls → Potential future import-cost relief
Shipping access and marine insurance remain constrained → Physical deliveries lag → Refining and freight costs remain elevated
Persistently expensive energy and services → Inflation expectations and policy caution → Long-term financing remains restrictive
```

Increased OPEC+ production requirements or unchanged targets cannot be interpreted as measured deliveries either. The seven-producer announcement of 1 November maintained December required production at September levels; actual production and seaborne flows must be assessed separately. For Brazil, where export revenues and corporate earnings include petroleum while consumers and industry purchase fuels, cheaper crude has distributive effects. The same world price can ease import costs yet reduce oil-sector earnings, and exchange-rate depreciation can cancel part of any domestic fuel-price relief. [5][11]

## 5. Equities and AI capital: earnings resilience versus financing duration

The 3 November US market close was not a uniform selloff. The S&P 500 gained roughly **0.36%**, the Nasdaq approximately **0.83%**, and the Dow lost roughly **0.40%**. Technology and AI-linked shares provided support to broad indices, but composition matters: a market-capitalization-weighted index can rise even while a larger number of its constituents decline. A few dominant companies therefore cannot stand in for the borrowing conditions of the median listed firm or of unlisted businesses. [4][6]

Data-centre investment connects semiconductor purchases, electricity generation, grid connections, construction and long-dated contracts. The economic value of such spending depends on capacity actually delivered, utilization, prices paid by customers, energy costs and financing terms. A new building or chip order is investment activity; it is not evidence that discounted future revenue will cover every capital and operating cost. Rising demand for computing infrastructure may also compete with other users for land, electrical equipment, skilled labour or grid capacity.

A ten-year Treasury yield near five percent raises the benchmark against which investors compare risky cash flows. Firms relying on refinancing or projected profit several years ahead may experience a different effect from companies with large cash balances and current recurring revenue. This explains why a rebound in selected technology shares does not nullify the duration constraint revealed by sovereign yields. It also explains why equity-price increases are not equivalent to fresh cash financing a company's operations.

## 6. Brazil: a weaker market before a real policy decision

Brazil's first November B3 session on 3 November closed with the Ibovespa down **1.42% at 165,528.92 points**, according to the exchange's own investor publication. The commercial dollar finished near **R$5.48**, up approximately **0.79%** on the day. Oil-linked equities, particularly Petrobras, came under pressure as international oil contracts fell. The report also attributed part of the local repricing to a more adverse expectations balance in the Focus survey and uncertainty about fiscal policy. [4][8]

The distributional impact of these movements is asymmetric. A weaker real increases the domestic currency price of imports and foreign-currency liabilities, other things equal. It can improve receipts in reais for commodity exporters, but companies importing equipment or holding dollar debt face offsetting expenses. Oil producers react to the international price as well as to the exchange rate. Brazilian long-term borrowing costs also depend on fiscal expectations, expected Selic rates, risk premia and Treasury debt maturity; none is measured directly by a one-day equity-index change.

The Selic entered the meeting at **13.75% per year**. The 4 November Copom outcome was not known at the information cutoff. B3's coverage reported options prices implying a larger probability of maintaining that rate than of a 25-basis-point cut, but a market-implied distribution is neither a central-bank vote nor a guarantee. The updated Focus survey reflects private-sector forecasts, not the Central Bank's own projection or commitment. [4][8][9]

A hold would preserve the immediate overnight benchmark but would not automatically stabilize the yield curve or the real if fiscal uncertainty remained. A cut could help short-term borrowing conditions while being interpreted as risky if inflation expectations or exchange-rate pass-through continued to worsen. Either action needs to be read with the committee's communication, the distribution of inflation forecasts and subsequent market prices. Assigning all movements to the election or to one policy decision would ignore the simultaneous petroleum and international rates shocks.

## 7. Europe, the US election and competing policy horizons

The latest finalized Eurostat inflation release available before the cutoff was September's **3.8% annual rate** for the euro area. The office scheduled the first estimate for October for 4 November, after the early-edition cutoff. The absence of that number matters: a prospective figure should not be silently substituted for an observed reading, and the headline should be separated from services, energy and goods components when released. [7]

The 3 November US midterm elections create additional uncertainty about legislation, public spending and regulation. As of the stated early cutoff, an ultimate national control outcome is not treated as established. If the composition of Congress changes, the plausible effect on bond supply and AI regulation depends on subsequent laws, budgets and enforcement. A partisan result is not itself an executed fiscal package, and markets may respond to several competing expectations rather than one policy channel.

```map
title: Regional exposure to the energy and financing repricing
United States | Long Treasury duration and labour demand | September JOLTS vacancies resilient; 3 November ten-year yield 4.95%
Australia | Inflation versus slowing housing | RBA raised its policy rate to 4.85% despite weaker activity exposure
Brazil | Oil-linked equities, currency and fiscal risk | B3 fell ahead of an as-yet-unannounced Copom decision
Euro area | Energy import prices versus underlying inflation | September headline inflation 3.8%; October flash still pending at cutoff
Middle East | Physical transport and energy-supply uncertainty | Crude settlement does not verify restored maritime delivery
```

The channels are not interchangeable. An oil-importing household benefits from lower fuel prices only after local currency, taxes and distribution margins transmit the change. A government refinancing its debt may not benefit if investors demand more compensation for inflation and duration. A technology company can report growing sales while still requiring expensive new capital. Cross-market analysis must preserve the units, timing and institutions in each link.

## 8. Conditional scenarios and evidence that would change the assessment

**Energy normalization with easing financing.** Verified improvement in Hormuz transit, lower insurance costs and rebuilding refined-product supplies would reinforce cheaper futures. If inflation expectations and ten-year yields then decline, the relief could reach importers, consumer purchasing power and investment. This outcome would weaken the claim that the financing constraint remains dominant. A durable fall in the Treasury ten-year rate and in Brazilian long-term domestic yields would be stronger evidence than a second daily oil decline.

**Inflation persistence despite cheaper futures.** If the next euro-area estimate remains elevated, US service-price measures stay firm and Australia's domestic price pressure persists, central banks could maintain restrictive settings. Fuel costs previously embedded in contracts, dollar depreciation abroad and deficits financed at high yields would slow disinflation. A rate hike in one country does not forecast identical action elsewhere; policy mandates and initial conditions differ.

**Credit and equity divergence.** AI-related shares may advance while issuance spreads, refinancing costs and capital intensity deteriorate for borrowers outside the highest-quality cohort. A fall in vacancies together with weaker hiring, increasing layoffs and widening corporate credit spreads would indicate a shift from valuation volatility to broader real-economy weakness. Conversely, stable hiring, declining spreads and greater cash generation from data-centre assets would undercut an indiscriminate AI-finance stress narrative.

```flow
Employment and service-price releases → Expected policy path → Two-year Treasury and local short-rate pricing
Sovereign borrowing and inflation risk → Ten- and thirty-year yields → Corporate, housing and public refinancing costs
Oil futures and physical deliveries → Imported inflation and margins → Real income and central-bank room for manoeuvre
Country-specific fiscal credibility and currency → Local term premia → Equity valuation and investment
```

For 4 November the observable checks are the Eurostat flash, the US ADP and services releases, Brazil's labour and foreign-exchange releases, and the Copom communiqué. The United Kingdom's Bank of England decision and Friday's US employment report follow later in the week. Each should be assessed only after the official release; neither meeting outcomes nor vote counts should be projected backwards into this edition. [7][9][10]

## 9. Assessment

Three observations anchor the present regime: US vacancies remain substantial; sovereign financing remains expensive despite cheaper crude; and at least one major central bank has just tightened because domestic inflation is not yet under control. Brazil's renewed equity decline and weaker currency illustrate how the same petroleum correction produces different effects across an exporter, consumers and sovereign funding conditions.

The near-term balance of risk rests on the speed of transmission. Energy futures can change immediately when diplomacy shifts probabilities. Real freight, retail prices, wages, debt service and public financing reprice on other schedules. A measured turn in those slower variables would justify reassessment; a single cross-asset session cannot.
