# War, Industrial Power and the Limits of the Development Thesis

*Research Report — 23 November 2026 | Political Economy & Markets*

## Central assessment

War has repeatedly accelerated technological development, industrial coordination and state capacity in the United States and Europe. It has also destroyed capital, displaced populations, redirected investment away from civilian uses and left fiscal and political liabilities that can persist for generations. Treating war as the principal engine of Western development confuses the effects of mobilisation with the effects of combat. The former can expand productive capacity; the latter can erase it.

The United States and West Germany illustrate two different routes from wartime mobilisation to postwar economic power. The United States entered the Second World War with a large continental economy, substantial industrial capacity and geographic insulation from the main theatres of destruction. Federal procurement and research funding accelerated existing capabilities and created durable innovation clusters. West Germany, by contrast, emerged with a devastated economy, disrupted trade and a depleted capital stock. Its recovery relied on surviving industrial knowledge and organisations, reconstruction, currency and price reform, access to external markets, and the expansion of world demand. The Korean War contributed to that demand; it did not create the underlying industrial base.

The German corporate record also requires a distinction between productive continuity and moral or legal exoneration. Some firms retained engineers, technical knowledge, supplier networks and managerial capacity. Some had expanded during the Nazi rearmament programme through coercive labour and direct participation in the war economy. Others lost plants and machinery, were dismantled, or were broken up by the Allies. The claim that German companies simply emerged stronger from the war is historically inaccurate.

The more general proposition is narrower: **military mobilisation can accelerate development when it creates transferable knowledge, capital equipment, supplier capabilities and institutions that survive demobilisation and can be redirected toward civilian production.** Those conditions are neither automatic nor exclusive to war. They can be produced through civilian infrastructure, public research, industrial policy and procurement programmes without requiring a general war.

## 1. The mobilisation effect is not the destruction effect

A war economy changes the allocation of resources at extraordinary speed. Governments become large and often dominant buyers; they guarantee demand, finance research, coordinate suppliers, standardise components and accept technical risks that private firms may not finance alone. Procurement contracts can support factories, laboratories, engineering teams and specialist suppliers before a commercial market exists. A military requirement can therefore create a platform on which civilian products and industries later develop.

This mechanism is visible in the United States during the Second World War. The Office of Scientific Research and Development funded applied research on a scale that reshaped the geography and direction of American innovation. Gross and Sampat’s study in the *American Economic Review* traces OSRD-funded inventions and finds persistent effects on technology clusters, high-technology entrepreneurship and employment. The effects remained visible for decades and were concentrated around the technologies supported by the wartime programme. [1]

The important variable was not simply the amount spent on weapons. It was the architecture of the spending: research contracts, university participation, engineering capacity, industrial suppliers and a federal government able to coordinate projects across institutions. The war compressed decisions and guaranteed a buyer. Scientific and industrial capabilities created under those arrangements could subsequently be used in civilian markets.

Yet wartime output is not a reliable measure of productivity. Alexander Field’s work on American manufacturing argues that manufacturing productivity fell during 1941–45, recovered only partly by 1948 and grew more slowly in the postwar golden age than in the interwar period. The conversion to military production required new machine tools, specialised plants and unfamiliar production processes; some facilities became obsolete after demobilisation. [2] A factory producing more aircraft or munitions is not necessarily using labour and capital more efficiently. Output can rise because the state is absorbing higher costs and because the objective is delivery, not commercial productivity.

This distinction matters for interpreting the American experience. Mobilisation helped end the demand collapse of the Great Depression by restoring employment and industrial utilisation. It also built scientific and engineering capacity. But the postwar prosperity of the United States depended on more than the continuation of war production: pent-up household demand, housing construction, private investment, technological diffusion, trade and the country's unusually intact productive base all mattered. The United States did not have to rebuild its industrial heartland after 1945.

## 2. Why the United States could convert mobilisation into postwar power

The United States possessed a combination that was unusual among the major belligerents. Its continental territory, energy and raw-material base, financial system and industrial centres remained largely intact. The war expanded production without exposing the core of the economy to sustained aerial bombardment or occupation. Factories could be converted, workers demobilised and equipment repurposed within a functioning national market.

Federal research funding also changed the relationship between government, universities and industry. The OSRD model linked public objectives to university laboratories and private manufacturers. It did not eliminate commercial incentives; it created demand and reduced the early-stage risks associated with technically uncertain projects. The resulting networks were geographically concentrated and persistent. [1]

The institutional legacy extended beyond individual inventions. Federal procurement established standards, technical specifications and long-term relationships with suppliers. Universities gained experience in large applied-research programmes. Firms learned to manage complex systems and to coordinate specialised subcontractors. These organisational capabilities were useful in aerospace, electronics, computing, pharmaceuticals and other sectors, although the timing and scale of spillovers differed by industry.

The Cold War preserved part of this system after 1945. Defence procurement and federally supported research remained substantial, creating a continuous market for advanced technologies. That continuity should not be confused with continuous generalised war. The United States fought major wars in Korea, Vietnam, Iraq and Afghanistan, while also maintaining a large peacetime defence establishment. Much of the technological and industrial effect came from sustained procurement and research, not from combat itself.

This arrangement carried costs. Military demand can lock capital and skilled labour into narrow specifications, protect suppliers from ordinary competition and create constituencies that defend programmes after their strategic rationale weakens. It can also crowd out civilian public investment when fiscal resources and engineering labour are scarce. The existence of technological spillovers does not establish that a defence dollar is more productive than a dollar spent on electricity grids, transport, health research or education.

## 3. Germany after 1945: industrial continuity beneath the ruins

The German case appears paradoxical because the physical destruction was immense while the subsequent industrial recovery was exceptionally rapid. The paradox becomes smaller once the distinction between destroyed capital and surviving productive knowledge is made.

The German economy in 1945 suffered from destroyed infrastructure, damaged factories, disrupted transport, shortages, displacement, territorial losses and the collapse of monetary exchange. Industrial output in 1948 was still reported at roughly one-third of its 1936 level, despite capital formation during the intervening years. The disruption was not only physical: wartime controls, monetary expansion, black markets and uncertainty over the economic order impaired production and exchange. [3]

But the economy was not rebuilt from an empty foundation. Many businesses remained intact or could be repaired. Engineers, skilled workers, technicians, managers and industrial traditions survived. Germany retained deep capabilities in chemicals, machinery, electrical engineering, machine tools, vehicles and precision manufacturing. The wartime economy had expanded some of these capabilities, even as bombing, dismantling and territorial division destroyed or removed other parts.

The June 1948 currency reform was a decisive institutional change. The Deutsche Mark replaced a currency that had lost its usefulness in ordinary exchange. Price controls were relaxed, monetary policy tightened and incentives for investment changed. Real industrial production increased by about 40% in the second half of 1948, while output grew at an average annual rate of roughly 11% from 1949 to 1959, according to the 1991 *Economic Report of the President*. The same report stresses that reform would not have produced these results without intact firms, skilled workers and managers, a functioning legal framework and external assistance. It also notes that unemployment rose sharply during the adjustment. [3]

The Marshall Plan helped address infrastructure bottlenecks and revive trade, but it should not be treated as a single-cause explanation. Economic historians disagree about the size of its direct contribution relative to currency reform, domestic institutional change and the reactivation of existing capacity. Christoph Buchheim argues that the relationship was reciprocal: currency and economic reform helped create the conditions in which the Marshall Plan could support multilateral trade and payments. [4] The IMF's historical account similarly emphasises reconstruction, export recovery and the return of Germany's position as a supplier of capital goods and durable manufactures. [5]

External demand then amplified the recovery. The Korean War generated a global investment and commodity boom that benefited West German exporters. By 1950, exports had exceeded imports; by 1960, West Germany's shares of world exports and imports were above those of the German Reich before the war. [5] This was not a simple reward for wartime destruction. It reflected the reactivation of industrial capacity under a new monetary and institutional framework, combined with demand from a world economy that was expanding.

The continuity thesis also has limits. The Federal Republic was not the whole of prewar Germany. Territorial losses, the division of the country and the removal of industrial equipment in the Soviet occupation zone changed the productive geography. In the western zones, Allied policy initially sought to dismantle or restrict parts of the armaments industry. The postwar economy therefore combined surviving industrial capabilities with institutional reconstruction and a different geopolitical position.

## 4. The corporate record: survival, complicity and dismantling

The experience of individual German companies shows why the phrase “German firms emerged stronger” is too broad.

BMW became a major supplier to the Nazi war economy. Aircraft engines and military production became central to its business. The company used prisoners of war, foreign civilian workers and concentration-camp prisoners under coercive conditions. BMW's own historical account states that approximately 29,000 forced labourers worked for the company by the end of 1944, more than half its workforce, and acknowledges that mass production depended on this labour. [6] This was not an incidental labour-policy failure. It was part of the production system that met rearmament orders.

At the same time, BMW did not simply carry its wartime industrial assets into the postwar period. Allied forces requisitioned and occupied its plants. In October 1945, the US military government ordered the Munich plant and the Allach facility to be dismantled. Equipment from Munich was shipped abroad as reparations, and BMW lost control over its assets for years. The company returned to motorcycle production before it could rebuild its position in automobiles. [7] Its later recovery cannot be described as an uninterrupted wartime advantage.

IG Farben presents a different case. Its Buna synthetic-rubber plant at Monowitz was integrated with the Auschwitz camp system, where prisoners were forced to work under lethal conditions. The United States Holocaust Memorial Museum documents the use of Auschwitz prisoners at the IG Farben plant and the role of the Monowitz subcamp in supplying labour. [8] After the war, IG Farben was broken up by the Allies. Its successor companies later became major industrial groups, but the postwar corporate structure was not simply the old conglomerate continuing unchanged.

Krupp and other heavy-industrial firms also faced occupation, expropriation, dismantling, trials and restrictions. Some industrial assets were removed; some managers were convicted and later released; some corporate capabilities re-emerged under new legal and ownership arrangements. The result was a mixture of continuity and rupture, not a clean transfer of wartime gains into peacetime prosperity. [9]

The distinction is essential. Technical knowledge can survive the fall of a regime. A supplier network can be reactivated. A firm can retain engineers and production routines even after losing equipment. But these continuities do not erase the use of forced labour, the appropriation of occupied economies or the political conditions under which wartime profits were earned. Nor do they imply that the firms were better off in every relevant sense after 1945.

## 5. Europe did not choose war as its development model

The postwar European project was built partly around the recognition that industrial capacity could make another war possible. The Schuman Declaration of 9 May 1950 proposed placing French and West German coal and steel production under a common authority. Coal and steel were not selected as neutral sectors: they were central inputs to heavy industry and armaments. The declaration argued that pooling them would make war between France and Germany not merely unthinkable but materially impossible. [10]

The European Coal and Steel Community was therefore an attempt to change the incentives and institutional control of strategic production. It sought to bind former rivals through shared rules, market access and oversight of the industries that had sustained military power. Integration did not eliminate conflict from Europe, and the continent remained divided by the Cold War. But Western Europe avoided another general war between its major states after 1945. The absence of such a war was not evidence that war was necessary for growth; it was part of the institutional environment in which reconstruction and integration could proceed.

The contrast is important. Europe rebuilt through a combination of domestic reform, external support, trade, investment and institutional cooperation. The process was supported by the American security umbrella, which reduced the need for independent military competition among Western European states. The defence burden was not abolished; it was partly reorganised through NATO and the wider transatlantic system.

## 6. The current defence cycle: when spending produces capability

The present European debate returns to the same economic question under different conditions. Higher defence budgets can support industrial output, employment, research and technological spillovers. But the effect depends on where the money is spent and which capabilities remain in the domestic economy.

The European Central Bank's 2026 analysis finds that longer-term productivity gains depend on structural change, economies of scale, learning by doing and spillovers from defence research into civilian production. Domestic equipment production has a stronger potential effect than imports, while personnel spending has a smaller productivity impact. The ECB reports that public defence R&D amounted to 0.03% of EU GDP in 2022, compared with 0.3% in the United States. It also cites an estimate that an increase in defence spending equivalent to 1% of GDP could raise long-run total factor productivity by 0.3%, primarily through public R&D spillovers. This is a model-based estimate, not a guaranteed return. [11]

An IMF study of EU defence spending reaches a compatible conclusion from a different empirical approach. Using national spending data from 1989 to 2023 and procurement contracts from 2009 to 2023, it finds that output effects vary substantially across countries and periods. Multipliers tend to be larger when import intensity is low, fiscal space is available and public investment is efficient. Equipment procurement has a stronger relative impact than other categories of spending. The authors caution that the current, unusually synchronised European build-up may produce smaller multipliers than historical episodes. [12]

These findings point to a practical test. A defence programme is more likely to create durable economic capacity when it develops domestic engineering, production tooling, testing facilities, skilled labour, component suppliers, software and research institutions. The value of a contract should therefore be measured not only by the equipment delivered, but by the production knowledge and supplier capabilities that remain after the contract ends.

Imports can still be rational when domestic production would be prohibitively expensive, slow or technologically inferior. But a procurement strategy dominated by imports transfers much of the industrial learning and employment elsewhere. Fragmented national orders can also prevent economies of scale and lock European buyers into incompatible systems. Common standards, joint procurement and predictable demand can improve the economics of domestic production, provided they do not protect inefficient incumbents indefinitely.

The same logic applies to civilian policy. Electricity networks, advanced materials, machine tools, transport infrastructure, semiconductor equipment and basic research can create dual-use capabilities without the destruction and emergency conditions of war. Public procurement can support early markets; universities can conduct applied research; development banks can finance long-horizon capital; and competition policy can prevent the resulting suppliers from becoming permanent rent-seekers.

## 7. A development mechanism, not a law of history

The recurring association between war and industrial advance has several sources. War raises the political priority of technologies that might otherwise be underfunded. It concentrates purchasing power, compresses development timelines and tolerates costs that civilian markets would reject. It can also force coordination among institutions that normally operate separately. These mechanisms are real.

But war also destroys physical and human capital, creates fiscal burdens, interrupts trade, displaces labour, damages institutions and can direct scientific talent toward narrow military objectives. The net result depends on the balance between mobilisation gains and destruction losses, and on the ability to convert military capabilities into civilian productivity.

The United States benefited from a particularly favourable conversion environment: it mobilised on a continental scale while avoiding the destruction of its industrial core. West Germany recovered because much of its industrial knowledge and organisational capacity survived, because its monetary and institutional arrangements changed, because reconstruction and external assistance restored the conditions for production, and because expanding world demand absorbed its exports. Neither case demonstrates that general war is a sustainable development strategy.

The claim that the West cannot remain at peace for long also overstates the evidence. The United States has maintained a large defence establishment and fought repeated overseas wars, but these are distinct phenomena. Western Europe, meanwhile, constructed institutions specifically designed to reduce the likelihood of another war among its major states. Proxy conflicts, colonial wars and interventions continued; a general European war did not.

The economic lesson is not that war creates prosperity. It is that states can mobilise capital, research, labour and industry when they treat a technological or strategic objective as urgent. The harder question is whether they can achieve the same coordination under normal political conditions, with transparent procurement, civilian spillovers and democratic accountability. A state that needs a war to build a power grid, a machine-tool industry or a research system has a problem of institutional design, not a law of economic development.
