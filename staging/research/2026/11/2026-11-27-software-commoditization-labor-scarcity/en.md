# Software after scarcity: the commoditization of code and technological labor

## The historical formation of the scarcity premium

The exceptional economic position occupied by software engineers during the past four decades arose from a historical imbalance. Computers became indispensable to enterprises faster than institutions could train the people required to operate and program them. In the mainframe era, access to machines, technical documentation and working systems depended on employers, research laboratories and universities. Even where a programming language was publicly documented, the equipment and time needed to master it were expensive. The shortage was therefore institutional as much as intellectual. The commercial separation of software from hardware, accelerated by IBM's 1969 unbundling, created an industry in which substantial initial investments could support the sale of many copies at low reproduction cost.

That business model combined two kinds of scarcity: the capacity to create useful software and the capacity to reach its customers. Early vendors built proprietary systems, distribution channels and specialist support organizations. Employees with practical knowledge of those systems could command wages above those available in many other occupations. A portion of this premium reflected genuine productivity; another portion reflected the shortage of credible substitutes. As computers entered banking, manufacturing, telecommunications and public administration, the number of organizational problems awaiting automation grew more rapidly than the available supply of technical labor.

The high compensation associated with information technology was neither an immutable property of intellectual work nor a guarantee that the same occupations would remain scarce indefinitely. It was the price of a productive input under particular historical conditions. Those conditions began to change well before generative artificial intelligence. The industry spent decades systematically reducing the difficulty of its own work, and its exceptional profitability financed the formation of competitors and new workers.

## The internet as a global labor-supply shock

In 2026 the International Telecommunication Union estimates that 6.3 billion people, or 76% of the world population, use the internet, compared with a revised 74% in 2025 [1]. Connectivity is not equivalent to programming competence; electricity, language, education, reliable equipment and time remain unequally distributed. Nevertheless, the availability of documentation, public repositories, university courses, discussion forums and collaborative environments has radically reduced the cost of acquiring technical knowledge.

The change has two distinct economic effects. The first is educational: a student outside a traditional technology center can study the same public documentation as a worker in Silicon Valley. The second is commercial: that student can eventually sell services to a firm in a different country without migrating. Outsourcing, distributed teams and remote work turn geographically separated labor markets into partially overlapping ones. Digitally delivered services are now measured internationally by the World Trade Organization [2]. National wage differences survive because of institutional trust, time zones, language, employment law and specialized capabilities, but employers can select from a larger pool.

The industry's salary premium helped finance its own erosion. High pay encouraged universities to expand computer science programs, private training companies to develop intensive courses, governments to promote digital skills and individuals to invest in occupational conversion. The new entrants acquired real opportunities for social mobility. Collectively, they also enlarged the supply of substitutable competencies. This is a familiar response to scarcity in economics; it does not require the assumption that the entire technology workforce is already in surplus.

During the pandemic, extraordinary digital demand and low financing costs temporarily obscured the consequences of this expansion. Hiring retrenchment after 2022 also reflected rising rates, revised growth expectations and the unwinding of exceptional staffing decisions. It would be historically inaccurate to attribute the whole contraction to AI. The new technology acts on a labor market already shaped by several decades of supply expansion and cyclical adjustment.

## Reusable capital and the industrialization of code

Open-source software introduced a second supply shock. A firm no longer has to develop its own operating system, database engine, compiler, networking library or encryption implementation merely to create a commercial application. The knowledge embodied in those components can be reused across millions of installations. Hoffmann, Nagle and Zhou estimate a demand-side replacement value of US$8.8 trillion for widely used open-source software [3]. That is a counterfactual replacement-cost estimate, not annual sales, market capitalization or a transferable asset valuation. Its significance is the scale of productive capability available without an equivalent licensing bill.

Cloud services subsequently reduced the capital required to acquire infrastructure, and standardized interfaces made integration a commercial discipline. The successive layers of abstraction changed the unit of work: instead of implementing every algorithm, developers increasingly assemble, configure, integrate and maintain components. This cumulative process is comparable to industrial standardization, although software does not have the same physical constraints as interchangeable mechanical parts.

The resulting cost reductions have repeatedly created new demand. Mobile applications, digital payments and online retail would have been substantially smaller markets if every project had required its own low-level infrastructure. The past expansion of engineering employment is thus compatible with a declining labor requirement per unit of functionality. The decisive question is whether new demand will continue expanding fast enough to absorb the labor saved by the next stage of automation.

| Period | Production mechanism | Constraint reduced |
|---|---|---|
| 1950–1980 | Mainframes and high-level languages | Direct machine operation |
| 1980–2000 | Personal computers and commercial software | Access to computing |
| 1990–2010 | Internet and open source | Knowledge and reusable components |
| 2005–2022 | Cloud, APIs and mature frameworks | Infrastructure and implementation |
| 2022 onward | Generative models and agents | Execution of selected cognitive tasks |

## Artificial intelligence and the distinction between code and delivery

Generative models affect the creation cost of software rather than merely its reproduction cost. Stanford's 2025 AI Index reports that inference at a specified MMLU performance level fell from US$20 per million tokens in November 2022 to US$0.07 in October 2024 [4]. Benchmark equivalence does not imply identical reliability, security or software engineering performance. Nor do token prices measure the total cost of operating an application. The comparison nonetheless establishes a marked decline in the price of access to a particular cognitive capability.

A 2026 NBER working paper distinguishes increases in code-writing activity from increases in actual software delivery [5]. The distinction is commercially essential. A repository can accumulate code without a corresponding increase in usable services. Requirements, architecture, integration, acceptance tests, security reviews, deployment and maintenance form a chain in which bottlenecks can migrate as one stage accelerates. A production manager should value reliable outcomes rather than generated lines of code. The expense of diagnosing rare defects and taking responsibility for operational failure may exceed the original implementation cost.

Commoditization requires substitutability, not simply a falling price. A routine registration interface or generic reporting component may be easy to reproduce. A payment system handling fraud, settlement, audit obligations and sensitive historical records is more difficult to replace. Software with an identical screen may embody a different level of reliability, institutional knowledge and legal exposure. The scope of commoditization therefore depends on the whole lifecycle of the product.

## Employment, wages and the disappearance of the apprenticeship rung

American occupational projections for 2025–2035 differentiate computer programmers, whose employment is expected to fall by approximately 7%, from software developers, quality assurance analysts and testers, whose combined employment is projected to rise by 10% [6, 7]. These are forecasts for one national labor market and different occupational definitions, not observed global job losses. They support a narrower interpretation: the market for writing instructions can evolve differently from the market for designing and operating systems.

The Stanford Digital Economy Lab's August 2026 revision of its employment research identifies a widening 19% relative employment gap for workers aged 22–25 in highly AI-exposed occupations. The authors explicitly characterize the findings as descriptive early indicators rather than causal estimates and do not report widespread economy-wide displacement [8]. The International Labour Organization's 2025 exposure assessment likewise emphasizes that occupational transformation is more plausible than wholesale substitution for many jobs [9].

The most consequential development may be an interruption in professional formation. Junior workers formerly learned by implementing bounded features, investigating minor defects and gradually assuming responsibility for systems. Automation removes some of the work that financed this apprenticeship. An employer can rationally reduce entry-level hiring while depending on experienced staff whose formation was paid for by earlier employers. If this strategy becomes universal, the supply of experienced engineers could become a future bottleneck. AI tutors and simulations may replace some of the learning process, but they do not automatically reproduce exposure to real consequences, production incidents and organizational negotiations.

Wage compression is also not the same as mass unemployment. It may appear as more demanding entry criteria, fewer salaried positions, a shift toward temporary contracting, stagnation in inflation-adjusted compensation or the consolidation of several responsibilities into a single job. Workers whose skills complement automation may retain premiums while workers performing standardized activities face growing competition. The aggregate headcount conceals distributional differences across seniority, region and occupational function.

## Software markets after the fall in entry costs

A software-as-a-service supplier historically charged for recurring access partly because customers lacked an economically viable substitute. Cheaper internal development improves the buyer's outside option. This does not mean every customer should build its own system: maintaining software and securing data require continuing expenditure. It does mean that the economics of the make-or-buy decision are changing. Per-seat pricing faces a further difficulty if customers automate work and reduce the number of employees using the system while increasing the number of automated transactions.

The vendor's defense increasingly lies in accumulated data, integration, reputation, distribution, compliance and measurable operational results. An enterprise resource planning system with decades of transaction histories and complex dependencies cannot be replaced merely by generating an equivalent visual interface. In contrast, standardized products with low switching costs and little exclusive data may experience rapid competitive entry, shorter product lifecycles and pricing pressure. Subscription businesses built atop model providers may also encounter variable inference expenses, unlike classical software with near-zero marginal copying cost.

The acquisition of customers constitutes a distinct market. A million additional applications cannot create a million additional hours of human attention. Buyers have finite budgets and limited capacity for institutional change. When production becomes cheaper, the scarce inputs may become discovery, trust and integration. Distribution platforms can benefit from competition among their suppliers, particularly when those suppliers lack direct access to customers.

| Product class | Substitutability tendency | Remaining constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Generic administrative applications | High | Distribution and support |
| Horizontal SaaS | Variable to high | Integrations and customer retention |
| Complex enterprise systems | Lower | Process knowledge and migration |
| Critical infrastructure | Lower | Reliability, liability and verification |

## Productivity, demand and the redistribution of capital income

Employment is the ratio of production to labor productivity in a simplified economy with comparable units of output. If labor productivity triples and output doubles, the required labor falls by one third. If output grows fourfold, employment increases by one third despite the same productivity shock. Neither case predicts an actual employment trajectory; software output is heterogeneous and quality is difficult to measure. The arithmetic clarifies why growth in code generation cannot establish growth in the demand for workers.

Price reductions can stimulate new markets in small firms, public institutions and industries that have previously found digitization too expensive. They can also produce saturation when users lack purchasing power or organizational capacity. The outcome depends on the elasticity of demand and on whether new activities arise in which human judgment remains complementary to machines.

The owners of complementary assets can capture a larger share of income. AI-enabled applications depend on electricity, advanced chips, memory, networks, data centers and capital. The UK Competition and Markets Authority's 2025 cloud services investigation documents competition concerns in infrastructure markets [10]. Cheap application production may coexist with concentrated infrastructure ownership. Competitive open models, improved hardware efficiency and local deployment may limit that concentration; the direction cannot be inferred from falling token prices alone.

The geographical implications are uneven. The United States combines capital markets and cloud providers; China combines industrial scale with domestic digital ecosystems; Taiwan and South Korea participate in crucial semiconductor supply chains. India, Eastern Europe and Latin America have developed export-oriented technical services, some of which rely on labor-cost differentials. Automated execution could weaken that advantage, while affordable development tools could allow local firms to enter product markets previously beyond their reach. Access to production tools does not guarantee ownership of the productive assets or revenue streams.

## Physical systems, public institutions and the future of expertise

A cheap digital representation of a factory does not supply machinery, energy or industrial tolerances. Software abundance may increase the relative value of capabilities that join computation to physical processes: energy systems, manufacturing, logistics, robotics, regulated medical devices and engineering. These fields remain exposed to automation, but validation and responsibility impose constraints distinct from writing generic applications. The relative scarcity of people able to integrate multiple disciplines may outlast the scarcity of conventional implementation skills.

Education must adjust to a world in which a plausible portfolio can be produced with extensive machine assistance. The value of a credential increasingly depends on the underlying ability to investigate unfamiliar failures, reason about architecture and understand the physical or institutional setting. Requiring additional credentials without improving these capabilities would merely raise the cost of competition for scarce positions. Firms also have incentives to sustain structured apprenticeships when private short-term savings would otherwise undermine the future supply of expertise.

At the societal level, productivity increases provide the means to raise living standards but do not determine the distribution of income. Cheap digital services cannot fully compensate for lost income where housing, healthcare, transportation and food remain costly. Competition policy, taxation, education and access to capital influence whether the savings become lower prices, higher wages, shorter working hours or concentrated profits. The issue is not whether society should suppress technological efficiency; it is whether the institutions governing ownership and labor can adapt to its consequences.

## Conditional trajectories for 2030–2040

Three outcomes are plausible and can coexist across sectors. In an expansion scenario, lower software prices stimulate enough new demand to preserve or increase technological employment. In a selective commoditization scenario, routine implementation and entry-level employment weaken while system integration, specialized engineering and infrastructure preserve premiums. In a deeper automation scenario, autonomous systems perform a growing share of the complete delivery lifecycle and income shifts more decisively toward owners of complementary assets.

The second trajectory is consistent with the mixed evidence available in November 2026, but is not a statistical forecast. A broad increase in entry-level hiring, sustained real wage growth for standardized tasks and rising paid employment relative to verified production would weaken the labor-commoditization hypothesis. Conversely, persistent contraction of entry pathways alongside growing delivered functionality and declining real compensation would strengthen it. Evidence must distinguish macroeconomic cycles from permanent substitution.

The historical lesson is that the economic value of a competence depends partly on the alternatives available to its buyers. Software may become more indispensable precisely as the ability to produce conventional software ceases to confer exceptional negotiating power. The decisive question for the next generation is who owns the systems, distribution networks and physical resources that turn abundant computational capability into durable economic income.
