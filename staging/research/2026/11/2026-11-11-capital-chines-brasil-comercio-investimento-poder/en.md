# Chinese Capital in Brazil: Trade, Industry and Power, 1974–2035

In 2025, Chinese companies implemented or began 52 investment projects in Brazil, valued at US$6.1 billion by the Brazil-China Business Council (CEBC). Brazilian merchandise exports to China reached US$100 billion that year, while imports from China amounted to US$70.9 billion. By October 2026, two-way merchandise trade had already reached US$159.25 billion over the first ten months of the year. The relationship now extends well beyond soybeans and iron ore. Chinese groups operate electricity networks, acquire mining assets, establish automotive plants, supply logistics equipment and compete in Brazil's digital consumer market. [CEBC, 2026](https://www.cebc.org.br/2026/05/07/o-brasil-foi-o-pais-que-mais-recebeu-investimentos-chineses-no-mundo-em-2025/); [MDIC, October 2026](https://balanca.mdic.gov.br/balanca/publicacoes_dados_consolidados/nota.html).

The same economic relationship generates effects that need not move in the same direction. Commodity sales expand foreign-exchange earnings and regional activity but make Brazil sensitive to Chinese demand. The acquisition of an electricity concession changes the beneficiaries of dividends without necessarily adding productive capacity; a new automotive factory creates physical capacity but may rely predominantly on imported components. Chinese machinery can lower investment costs for some Brazilian producers while exposing competing domestic suppliers to cheaper imports. Outcomes depend on the distribution of income and decision-making among companies, workers, governments, consumers and territories.

## 1. Before investment: the political construction of bilateral relations

Brazil and the People's Republic of China established diplomatic relations in 1974, during Brazil's military regime. A trade agreement followed in 1978, but the institutional relationship did not immediately create the enormous supply chains of the twenty-first century. Geographical distance, different trade structures and China's limited integration into world markets still constrained business. [Brazilian Senate, bilateral chronology](https://legis.senado.gov.br/sdleg-getter/documento?dm=7733829).

Space cooperation anticipated a different kind of bilateral integration. Under an agreement signed on July 6, 1988, Brazil's INPE and Chinese partners began developing the CBERS Earth-observation satellite program. Its first satellite was launched in 1999. Here the countries shared expenditure and engineering tasks rather than merely buying and selling finished goods. According to INPE, Brazil's financial contribution rose from 30% for the first satellites to 50% for later missions. Joint technical development should be distinguished both from foreign direct investment and from simple technology imports. [INPE, CBERS history](https://www.gov.br/inpe/pt-br/programas/cbers/sobre-o-cbers-1/historia); [INPE, investment shares](https://www.gov.br/inpe/pt-br/acesso-a-informacao/perguntas-frequentes/cbers/como-foram-divididos-os-trabalhos).

The strategic partnership established in 1993, upgraded to a global strategic partnership in 2012, broadened channels for negotiation. The high-level bilateral commission COSBAN, established in 2004, institutionalized government coordination. China became Brazil's largest trading partner in 2009 as Chinese manufacturing and demand for food and energy expanded. These milestones do not imply identical foreign-policy preferences. Brazil simultaneously engages with Mercosur, the United States, the European Union, the G20 and BRICS. [Brazilian Planning Ministry, November 2024](https://www.gov.br/planejamento/pt-br/assuntos/noticias/2024/novembro/ministerio-do-planejamento-e-orcamento-e-comissao-nacional-de-desenvolvimento-e-reforma-da-china-assinam-memorando-de-entendimento).

## 2. Chinese industrialization and Brazil's productive structure

China's accession to the World Trade Organization in 2001, rapid urbanization, construction and industrial growth increased demand for iron ore, steelmaking inputs, crude oil, animal protein and feed ingredients. Brazil possessed competitive deposits, expanding agricultural capacity, established commercial farming, mining expertise and transport networks able to grow in scale. Chinese demand reinforced investment in farms, mines, storage and export corridors.

International prices mattered as much as physical shipments. A higher iron-ore price increases dollar export earnings even without a rise in tonnage. A subsequent fall can narrow the surplus despite stable production. This terms-of-trade mechanism is not identical to productivity growth, and China alone does not determine global prices. Other consumers, competing producers, exchange rates, stocks and energy costs also affect them.

Chinese manufacturing also expanded global supplies of intermediate and final goods. Lower-priced machinery may improve Brazilian industrial productivity, but stronger competition can reduce sales by producers of textiles, electronics, consumer goods, industrial machinery and metals. A structural asymmetry emerged: Brazil exports a narrow basket of resources and imports a considerably more varied range of industrial goods, components and equipment.

## 4. Three commodities account for nearly three-quarters of exports

Soybeans, crude oil and iron ore together accounted for approximately **74%** of Brazilian exports to China in 2025, according to BNDES research based on trade statistics. Such concentration matters more for economic autonomy than the size of the bilateral surplus alone. China can substitute at least some Brazilian supply with alternatives, though at a cost; Brazil would not easily replace a buyer of this scale. [BNDES, *Impacts of the tariff shock on Brazil's trade balance*, 2026](https://web.bndes.gov.br/bib/jspui/bitstream/1408/29387/1/PRFol_216976_Impactos%20do%20tarifa%C3%A7o%20na%20balan%C3%A7a%20comercial%20brasileira.pdf).

For soybeans, China's willingness to import raw grain and process it domestically shapes the allocation of crushing, soymeal and oil production. Brazilian grain shipments pay farmers, traders, storage operators and transport firms, but part of the downstream value is generated in China. This does not mean Brazil has no productive competence: agricultural science, machinery, farm productivity, cooperative organization and logistics constitute valuable capabilities. The concern arises when primary output expands without commensurate domestic processing and technical depth.

In mining, Brazilian deposits and railway-port systems supply Chinese steel producers. Iron content, quality and freight costs affect competition with other exporters, while downstream steelmaking may occur abroad. Brazilian metallurgical production faces its own energy and logistics costs, imported competition and fluctuations in Chinese construction demand. A Chinese economy driven more by household consumption and services than property development would create a different mineral-demand profile.

Brazil's offshore pre-salt production has also expanded export capacity, with China serving as an important destination. Crude extraction supports employment, engineering, royalties and government revenue, but exporting crude is not equivalent to domestic refining or petrochemical output. Whether more oil should be processed in Brazil depends on actual refinery capacity, oil grades, fuel requirements, processing margins, energy and financing conditions.

## 5. Five forms of capital that require different economic accounts

“Chinese investment” combines transactions with materially different consequences.

| Operation | Immediate change | What does not automatically follow |
|---|---|---|
| Acquisition of an existing company or stake | Ownership, control rights or dividend claims | A new factory, extra employment or gross fixed capital formation |
| New industrial plant | Construction and potential additional productive capacity | Local components, R&D, technology transfer or higher productivity |
| Expansion of an existing facility | More capacity or efficiency at an operating site | A complete domestic supply chain |
| Infrastructure concession or corporate stake | Operating rights and regulated or contracted revenue | Transfer of Brazilian regulatory sovereignty |
| Loan, credit line or monetary arrangement | Funding or contingent financing | Equity FDI, a productive asset or immediate disbursement |

The CEBC tracks acquisitions, new projects, expansions and joint ventures using project-specific confirmation and estimation procedures. The Central Bank of Brazil records direct investment in balance-of-payments statistics under residence and financial-instrument rules, including equity and intercompany debt. Chinese ultimate owners may invest via entities in Hong Kong or other intermediary jurisdictions, so immediate financial origin and ultimate control do not always coincide. CEBC project totals, BCB liabilities, corporate announcements and government memoranda cannot be added together. [CEBC, 2025 report and methodology](https://www.cebc.org.br/investimentos-chineses-no-brasil/).

The CEBC annual series is sensitive to major transactions rather than exhibiting uniform growth. It records US$13 billion in 2010, US$8.8 billion in 2017, US$1.9 billion in 2020, US$1.3 billion in 2022 and US$6.1 billion in 2025. A multi-billion-dollar acquisition can increase the annual figure without increasing Brazil's productive capital stock by an equivalent amount.

## 6. The 2025 shift toward power, mining and vehicles

CEBC's 52 projects in 2025 were concentrated by value in electricity (US$1.79 billion; 29.5%), mining (US$1.76 billion; 29%), automotive production (US$965 million; 15.8%) and petroleum (US$804 million; 13.3%). Electricity accounted for 27 projects, over half of the total project count. Mining involved fewer deals of relatively greater value. These are distinct patterns: dispersed electricity investment and large mineral-asset transactions. [CEBC, May 2026 publication](https://www.cebc.org.br/2026/05/07/o-brasil-foi-o-pais-que-mais-recebeu-investimentos-chineses-no-mundo-em-2025/).

CEBC also identified **seven announced projects with potential value of US$2.76 billion that did not materialize in 2025**. Counting them would yield a hypothetical US$8.82 billion rather than the confirmed classification. Even implemented projects may involve spending allocated across several years: project cost, financial disbursement, physical completion and the date of a corporate acquisition are different concepts.

The council classified 31 projects as related to sustainability and green energy, about 60% of the 2025 count. This encompasses hydropower, wind, solar and electrified mobility; it is not proof of net lifecycle emissions reductions. Power-system substitution, equipment manufacturing, mining, land use and recycling all affect the result.

## 7. Electricity: foreign ownership under Brazilian rules

The presence of State Grid, CPFL and China Three Gorges illustrates the appeal of long-lived power assets. Regulated or contracted revenue may support large-scale financing and engineering. For Brazil, investment can improve power supply, expand transmission and support generation.

Corporate ownership does not transfer the responsibilities of ANEEL, the Brazilian power-system institutions or the state. Operational risks concern contractual quality, regulatory enforcement, maintenance, vendor concentration and replacement of essential equipment. A profitable foreign-owned operator may remit dividends abroad while also paying Brazilian workers, taxes and contractors and maintaining domestic infrastructure. Consumer tariffs, outages, network losses, investment execution and resilience must be evaluated alongside ownership.

As wind and solar capacity expands, the binding problem can shift from building generation to transmission, storage, connection queues, flexible dispatch and curtailed output. Funding a solar installation, battery factory or high-voltage line addresses different physical constraints. They should not be aggregated as interchangeable green capacity.

## 8. Mining: ownership of deposits is not control of the whole industrial chain

Chinese interest in nickel, copper, lithium, graphite and other minerals reflects their use in batteries, grids and manufacturing. Chinese companies have substantial industrial capacity in several refining and intermediate-processing stages. Acquiring a Brazilian mining firm can provide commercial access to future output; it does not create the geological deposit. Exploration rights, permitted mines, mineral concentrates and refined metals are distinct economic objects.

Brazil's retained value depends on processing performed domestically, jobs, technical services, tax payments, mining royalties, environmental remediation, transport and sales contracts. Exporting a concentrate may generate considerable local income while yielding less domestic transformation than a competitive processing industry. Refining plants require energy, water, scale, skills, environmental management and markets, however; forcing an uneconomic local stage can undermine the activity it intends to deepen.

Land and environmental disputes are equally concrete. Indigenous peoples, traditional communities, rural landowners, municipalities and workers may bear different impacts. Licensing, consultation, waste dams, transport, water availability, restoration and benefit-sharing require site-specific evidence. Neither Chinese nor any other foreign ownership exempts a project from Brazilian laws.

## 9. Oil and gas: common commercial interests, domestic regulatory constraints

China seeks secure energy supply; Brazil has an internationally integrated offshore petroleum industry with Petrobras and private operators. Chinese entry into concessions can mobilize finance and engineering, while exploration remains subject to auctions, contracts, permits and domestic requirements. In 2025, CEBC recorded CNPC's participation alongside Chevron in the award of nine exploration blocks near the Amazon River mouth. **An awarded exploration block is not a producing oil field**, and possible future discoveries are not current export revenues. [CEBC, 2025 edition](https://www.cebc.org.br/investimentos-chineses-no-brasil/).

Oil-price movements can improve export receipts and fiscal revenue while increasing domestic fuel or transport costs. The net distribution depends on taxes, fuel pricing, exchange rates, refining, royalties and consumption. Long-term energy transition adds differentiated risks to fields with different production costs and development timelines without immediately eliminating global crude trade.

## 10. Electric vehicles: from imports to assembly to an industrial ecosystem

BYD, GWM and other producers bring skills in batteries, electric powertrains, power electronics, software and large-scale vehicle production. BYD's plant in Camaçari, Bahia, and GWM's manufacturing presence in Iracemápolis, São Paulo, expanded domestic industrial locations. The Bahia state government reported nearly 20,000 vehicles produced in Camaçari in 2025 and outlined plans for more local suppliers, stamping, welding and painting in 2026. These intentions are not proof that the planned localization has already happened. [Bahia government, January 2026](https://www.ba.gov.br/sde/noticias/2026-01/32970/byd-retoma-producao-em-camacari-e-chega-quase-20-mil-veiculos-produzidos).

Three production models have distinct effects: importing fully built vehicles; assembling imported kits; and manufacturing with a domestic network of components, industrial engineering and services. All may create some local jobs, but the third offers more substantial opportunities for supplier development. Even then, battery cells, chips, patents and specialized machinery may remain foreign-sourced. Local learning is determined by what workers and firms actually design, manufacture and maintain.

Established Brazilian and foreign-owned automotive plants face pressure on prices and product cycles. Consumers may benefit from more affordable, efficient vehicles while traditional suppliers and workers face retraining and adjustment costs. Tax incentives, charging networks, public revenues and industrial spillovers should be evaluated together. Public support for assembly with large imported content may yield weaker long-term returns than demonstrable supplier and engineering development.

## 11. Workers, subcontractors and the social boundaries of investment

Construction at Camaçari also exposed serious labor violations. Brazil's Ministry of Labor and Employment reported that inspectors found 471 Chinese workers brought irregularly into the country, including 163 rescued in conditions analogous to slavery in December 2024. Labor inspectors attributed direct responsibility to the automaker and issued over 60 notices of violation subject to legal procedures. These are administrative inspection findings, not a statement of a final criminal conviction. [Labor Ministry, updated October 26, 2026](https://www.gov.br/trabalho-e-emprego/pt-br/noticias-e-conteudo/2025/junho/byd-e-autuada-por-submeter-trabalhadores-chineses-a-condicoes-analogas-a-escravidao-na-bahia).

The underlying governance problem extends beyond a single plant. Subcontracting can obscure responsibility for hours, housing, immigration documents, pay and effective supervision. Worker safety, collective rights, lawful recruitment and training affect the legitimacy and sustainability of investment. A worker's or employer's nationality does not limit the protections required under Brazilian law.

Gross employment announced by a company does not measure the net labor-market effect. Analysis must account for potentially displaced jobs, wages, formal registration, retention and the difference between temporary construction and permanent manufacturing. States providing tax concessions need to compare forgone revenue with actual wages, downstream suppliers, local skills and future tax receipts.

## 12. Rail, ports and the physical costs of trade

Soybean exports from Brazil's Center-West and mineral shipments from inland deposits require storage, road freight, rail, terminals, deepwater facilities, insurance and shipping capacity. Lower logistics costs can raise producer income and improve domestic competitiveness. They can also raise land values and commercial pressures at agricultural frontiers, affecting local communities and ecosystems.

Brazil and China signed railway cooperation and study arrangements in 2025. In June 2026, Brazil's ANTT presented in China a domestic rail-project portfolio with **potential to mobilize over R$600 billion**. That number represents possible investment in Brazilian projects from various public and private sources, **not committed Chinese capital**. Continental railway proposals and Pacific connections require feasibility work, concession structures, financing, environmental permits and decisions by countries along any route. [ANTT, June 12, 2026](https://www.gov.br/antt/pt-br/assuntos/ultimas-noticias/antt-apresenta-portfolio-ferroviario-na-china-e-aproxima-investidores-de-projetos-estrategicos-no-brasil).

Freight flows in both directions matter. A corridor optimized for bulk exports may have low utilization on return trips. The social return depends on access for smaller producers, integration with regional industry, maintenance and land-use effects. Purchasing Chinese locomotives and wagons does not make the railway Chinese-owned. Manufacturer, lender, concessionaire, rail operator and shipper can be separate actors.

## 13. Green investment and reliance on imported equipment

Brazil's electricity mix is relatively renewable-intensive, while China has industrial scale in solar panels, batteries, inverters and associated equipment. Bilateral projects combine distinct advantages: renewable resources and generation conditions in Brazil, large-scale manufacturing and financing capabilities in China. Climate outcomes depend on whether clean generation displaces actual emissions and whether transmission and storage permit its use.

Imported solar equipment can lower prices and accelerate deployment without domestic manufacturing. Heavy dependence on a small number of equipment or service providers, however, creates exposure to logistics delays, currency movements and trade policies. Local production incentives need market scale, affordable power, qualified labor and component access. The viable economic objective is not to prohibit every import but to develop durable domestic competencies in stages with scope for learning and competition.

Environmental effects extend beyond carbon emissions. Transmission routes and reservoirs affect communities, battery-mineral extraction creates waste streams, and commodity agriculture changes land use. Lifecycle assessments, land-use rules and licensing must be applied rather than treating electrification-related investment as automatically sustainable.

## 14. Telecommunications, platforms and information

Chinese participation in telecom equipment, consumer electronics, online commerce, mobility services and delivery platforms extends the relationship into digital infrastructure. Networks and apps involve scale, user data, interoperability, cybersecurity, platform competition and worker organization. Lower prices can benefit consumers but should be assessed against market concentration, small-business bargaining power and platform workers' earnings and protections.

Brazilian telecommunications, data-protection, competition, taxation and labor rules govern these activities. Supplier nationality is not a substitute for technical testing, security requirements, transparent audits or diversified procurement. A measurable dependency exists when critical equipment, updates or interfaces cannot be replaced at acceptable cost and within a reasonable period—not merely because a component was manufactured in China.

The CBERS space program demonstrates the possible gains from shared engineering and data capabilities. Private digital platforms may operate under a different model in which software, central data systems and intellectual property remain overseas. The opportunities for domestic learning therefore vary substantially across forms of cooperation.

## 15. Finance, the renminbi and the price of capital

International trade is supported by prices, banks, currencies, collateral and payment infrastructure. Settlement in reais or renminbi can reduce specific conversion costs, but it does not change the composition of Brazilian exports or make a foreign-owned plant domestically owned. A soybean contract can be priced in dollars, settled through other currencies and finance farmers whose costs are in reais. Pricing, invoicing, settlement and financing should be analyzed separately.

In 2025, Brazil's central bank supported a new bilateral currency swap arrangement with the People's Bank of China as a liquidity and foreign-exchange market tool. **A central-bank swap line is neither productive investment nor an indication that its full nominal amount has been drawn.** It is contingent financial support subject to specific risks and conditions. [Banco Central do Brasil, CMN Vote No. 14/2025](https://normativos.bcb.gov.br/Votos/CMN/202514/Voto_do_CMN_14_2025.pdf).

Brazil's domestic cost of capital also affects Chinese investment. Real interest rates, exchange rates, expected returns and taxes change the price of acquisitions and the viability of new plants. Real depreciation can make Brazilian assets cheaper for foreign buyers but simultaneously raise the costs of imported equipment and foreign-currency debt service. A weak currency does not mechanically produce industrialization through FDI.

Financing must be separated into shareholder equity, external debt, Brazilian bank lending, public guarantees, supplier credit and possible subsidies. For infrastructure, contractual remuneration, tariff revisions and construction risk may matter as much as the lender's country of origin.

## 16. Brazil's relations with China, the United States, Europe and Mercosur

Chinese expansion occurs amid growing technological and commercial competition between major powers. Export controls, tariffs, rules of origin and subsidies affect where multinational companies choose to produce. A Chinese-owned plant in Brazil may serve Brazilian and neighboring markets, but its origin does not automatically provide preferential entry into the United States, Europe or any other destination. Each jurisdiction applies its own rules.

The United States, European Union, Japan, South Korea and India remain relevant Brazilian customers, suppliers, financiers and technology partners. The economic choice is not necessarily binary. Brazil's diplomatic room for maneuver expands when it possesses alternative commercial relationships, domestic capabilities and institutions capable of assessing contracts.

Mercosur adds a regional supply-chain dimension. Brazilian manufacturing can use parts and industrial services from Argentina, Paraguay and Uruguay; it may also displace regional production if complete imports replace regional components. External tariffs, origin rules and trade agreements shape incentives but do not ensure industrial upgrading. Actual outcomes depend on factory sourcing, universities, transport and supplier competitiveness.

Brazil and China signed planning and government-cooperation documents during Xi Jinping's 2024 visit. Further energy, ethanol and sustainable-technology arrangements followed in 2025. **A memorandum is evidence of a diplomatic commitment or cooperation program, not an implemented investment.** [Brazilian Planning Ministry, 2024](https://www.gov.br/planejamento/pt-br/assuntos/noticias/2024/novembro/ministerio-do-planejamento-e-orcamento-e-comissao-nacional-de-desenvolvimento-e-reforma-da-china-assinam-memorando-de-entendimento); [Ministry of Mines and Energy, bilateral instruments](https://www.gov.br/mme/pt-br/atos-internacionais/atos-internacionais-2025/china).

## 17. Regional and social distribution of Chinese capital

The CEBC identified Chinese investments associated with 20 Brazilian states in 2025, six more than in 2024. São Paulo recorded 17 projects, Minas Gerais and Pará ten each, and Amapá nine. The Southeast accounted for 32.5% of recorded regional occurrences and the North 26.7%. Multi-state projects can appear in several geographic counts, which are not a sum of independent projects. [CEBC, 2025 report](https://www.cebc.org.br/investimentos-chineses-no-brasil/).

São Paulo combines major consumer markets, regulated utilities, company headquarters and manufacturing. Minas Gerais links mining, energy and industrial capacity. Bahia hosts new automotive operations. Pará and Amapá face specific extractive prospects and environmental constraints. In the Center-West, power and logistics projects can support agriculture. An identical dollar amount can produce different local effects depending on worker skills, imported inputs, existing infrastructure and tax concessions.

Distributional analysis should follow the revenues and costs borne by local governments and residents. Mining can produce considerable royalty income with relatively limited direct employment. Manufacturing may create more positions while demanding expensive incentives. Digital platforms can increase consumer choice and provide income opportunities, but can also create intense competition and insecure labor conditions. An aggregate investment number conceals these differences.

## 18. Transmission from investment to income and dependency

```flow
Chinese demand for food and minerals → Brazilian exports → foreign-exchange earnings and producer income → tax revenue, infrastructure and domestic investment
Export-product concentration → exposure to Chinese demand and commodity prices → volatile foreign earnings → exchange-rate and investment effects
New Chinese factory → equipment purchases and hiring → domestic output → conditional local sourcing and learning → wages, tax revenue and productivity
Acquisition of Brazilian assets → change in controlling shareholders → possible restructuring and further investment → dividends allocated under ownership and profitability
```

These mechanisms interact. Export receipts can finance machinery and intermediate inputs that improve Brazil's production capacity. Alternatively, they may reinforce specialization in primary commodities if industrial investment remains uncompetitive. Foreign investors can supply technology, but diffusion depends on domestic suppliers, engineering capacity, contracts and competitive pressure. Building a new plant can increase local activity even when its financing is disbursed over multiple years.

GDP increases when additional goods and services are produced domestically, not when a factory is merely announced. A transfer of shares does not automatically increase national output; actual construction and local equipment production can. Merchandise exports affect foreign demand, while remitted dividends appear in different external accounts. The separation prevents both unwarranted celebration and the claim that all foreign-owned profits represent an unambiguous loss to Brazil.

## 19. Bargaining power, regulation and institutional capacity

The two countries have asymmetrical interdependencies. Brazil is a competitive supplier of strategically useful goods but sells a concentrated basket to an unusually large customer. China depends on worldwide resources and markets and has alternatives, though some are costly or slow to activate. Bargaining power varies by product quality, freight, prices, inventories and the ability to redirect trade.

Brazilian concession rules, environmental permits, antitrust law, labor rights and other regulations provide public authority over local operations. CADE reviews relevant competition concerns; ANEEL and ANP exercise sectoral mandates; environmental and labor authorities have their own powers. Enforcement is not necessarily perfect, but foreign investment does not displace these legal institutions.

Corporate transparency is central. Public authorities and researchers need to identify ultimate owners, related-party contracts, public guarantees, tax concessions, concession duration, tied procurement and intellectual-property rights. Economic-security assessments should document real vulnerabilities such as dependence on irreplaceable equipment, insufficient substitute suppliers, disruption risk or weak oversight. An assumption based only on the nationality of capital is not evidence of such vulnerability.

## 20. Three conditional trajectories for 2027–2035

**The first trajectory extends commodity exports and local assembly.** China remains a major buyer of soybeans, iron ore and oil. Investments grow in mining, electricity and assembly plants, while technically complex components are imported. Brazil may continue posting bilateral surpluses in some years and support specialized regions, yet remains sensitive to commodity prices and externally controlled technology. Observable evidence would include growing raw-material shipments, low local component shares and limited growth in Brazilian engineering services.

**The second trajectory transforms foreign investment into more diversified productive capabilities.** Manufacturers develop domestic suppliers, universities and engineering teams. Mineral projects add competitive intermediate processing, and energy and rail connections serve domestic producers as well as export corridors. Wages and tax revenue could rise with greater productivity and industrial exports. Tests include local input shares, engineers employed, Brazilian suppliers exporting components, locally performed R&D and sustained plant utilization. Announced localization percentages are insufficient.

**The third trajectory is slowdown and fragmentation.** A persistent Chinese industrial or property-sector downturn, financial restrictions, geopolitical tensions or severe price shocks could lower investment and alter trading routes. Brazil could see reduced commodity earnings, stalled projects and idle capacity. This can occur without a diplomatic rupture: profitability, demand and credit have their own dynamics.

These are not quantitative forecasts or mutually exclusive national outcomes. Mining could grow while automobiles slow; electricity may attract capital as exports weaken. Each sector requires its own evidence rather than a single bilateral success or failure index.

## 21. Measuring productive development instead of announcements

New investment announcements should be followed by actual disbursements, physical completion, production, permanent employment and plant utilization. For acquisitions, subsequent capital expenditure, governance, earnings distribution and ownership changes are the relevant measures. Trade statistics should separate price changes from export quantities and distinguish products by processing stage.

In electricity, completed transmission, connection delays, service quality, tariffs and resilience matter more than announced megawatts. In mining, actual production, domestic processing, permits, royalties, accidents, restoration and community benefits are material. Automotive localization requires identifying the origin of battery cells, chips, software, steel, traction systems, tooling and industrial engineering.

Diplomatic relations likewise require operational evidence: signed memoranda converted into contracts, contracts converted into functioning facilities, disputes resolved under legitimate institutions, successful scientific collaboration and diversification of market access. Brazil's autonomy depends on its public institutions and domestic financing and productive capabilities, not solely the volume of foreign capital.

## 22. A new structure of interdependence

Brazil is simultaneously a major supplier of natural resources, a consumer market and an operating base for Chinese firms. China has become not only a buyer of Brazilian commodities but also a corporate participant in infrastructure and industrial activities that influence production costs inside Brazil. These decisions affect electricity tariffs, mineral value chains, mobility, jobs, ports and regional development.

Capital inflows are neither inherently a transfer of sovereignty nor an automatic guarantee of development. Their effects depend on the assets acquired, transaction prices, contracts, ownership rights, competition, enforcement and the capacity to develop local workers and businesses. Maintaining relations with other commercial and technological partners improves the range of available options, but it produces autonomy only when Brazil has the real ability to finance, produce, negotiate and regulate.

The coming decade will be determined less by announced billions of dollars than by the amount of industrial processing, engineering, useful infrastructure, skilled employment, innovation and public revenue that the relationship produces within Brazil—and the social and environmental conditions under which those gains are achieved.
