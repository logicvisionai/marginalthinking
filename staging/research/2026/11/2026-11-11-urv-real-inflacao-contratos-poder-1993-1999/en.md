# Brazil's URV and Real Plan: the monetary engineering that broke inflation — and the price of rebuilding a currency

In June 1994 a Brazilian household needed no economic report to recognize the destruction of money. A wage could be adjusted and still lose purchasing power between payday and the weekly food shopping. A shopkeeper changed prices before receiving new inventory. Manufacturers negotiated input bills, inventory credit, regulated tariffs and payroll without knowing which quotations would survive another month. Inflation silently altered the real weight of public budgets. Banks equipped to invest overnight balances operated in a different economy from workers paid once a month.

The Unidade Real de Valor, or **URV**, was not simply a transitional name between the cruzeiro real and the real. It replaced the language in which prices were *calculated* before replacing the money in which purchases were *paid*. Public authorities separated the unit-of-account function from the means-of-payment function; coordinated wage and contractual conversions; incorporated past inflation without mechanically reproducing it in future contracts; and only then issued new banknotes. This sequence helps explain why July 1994 was not just another surprise price freeze.

Monetary success did not end the stabilization problem. As households learned to budget in reais, Brazil encountered appreciated exchange rates, expensive credit, external vulnerabilities, bank restructuring and new conflicts over employment, income and public finance. The URV episode combines an unusual intellectual innovation, demanding political negotiation, a collective experiment in trust and a distributional settlement extending well beyond 1 July.

## 1. Before the URV: decades of currency erosion

Brazil's inflation began well before the 1980s. Import-substitution industrialization, fiscal funding structures, external shocks and expanding indexation turned price increases into normal operating assumptions. Monetary correction introduced in the 1960s protected some long-term contracts and helped sustain capital-market instruments. In an economy without a credible nominal anchor, however, last period's inflation became an input into the next round of contract updates. Indexation did not independently cause every inflationary shock; it helped make inflation persistent after the original shock passed.

Oil-price shocks, rising international interest rates, the external-debt crisis and early-1980s recession left Brazil facing foreign-exchange constraints, distributional conflict and public-finance problems at once. Democratic transition did not automatically deliver a stable currency. Citizens recovered political rights even while everyday financial planning remained hazardous.

Repeated stabilization plans produced their own history of distrust. The 1986 Cruzado Plan attempted to interrupt inflation with a price and wage freeze and revised wage rules; demand growth, distorted relative prices, shortages and political strains made the freeze unsustainable. The 1987 Bresser and 1989 Verão plans also sought nominal restraint and deindexation without lasting success. Collor's 1990 and 1991 measures added the trauma of immobilized liquid financial assets. After each failure, the population learned to regard the next promise with suspicion.

Inflation also had a moral and political dimension. Every participant expected other people to adjust first: retailers anticipated suppliers; wage earners anticipated supermarket prices; employers anticipated inputs and payroll; unions anticipated corporate margins. High inflation became both the collective result and each participant's defensive response. This was a coordination problem, not proof of universal bad faith. A price increase protecting one business could enter the index that justified the next increase everywhere else.

## 2. Inflation's anatomy: shocks, inertia and financing

Prices do not rise for one reason alone. Exchange-rate changes, global input prices, margins, wages and taxes alter relative prices. Public deficits financed through money creation can support continued nominal expansion. Depreciation expectations induce early repricing. Contractual rules transmit earlier inflation. An analysis of 1993–1994 must distinguish new disturbances from mechanisms that keep inflation moving after those disturbances disappear.

Inertial inflation describes an economy with staggered contracts. Some wages and tariffs were adjusted yesterday, while others would be adjusted tomorrow. Each group seeks to restore a relative position already lost. When these adjustments occur at different dates, their combined effect perpetuates aggregate inflation. A wage contract indexed to the previous inflation rate and a company price rule tied to previous cost increases may reproduce high inflation even when demand pressure falls. Monetary restraint can damage activity and employment before it interrupts the sequence.

Fiscal explanations matter, but accounting concepts matter too. Nominal deficits include nominal interest inflated by rising prices; operational deficits attempt to exclude the inflation component of interest; primary balances exclude interest entirely. A large nominal deficit does not mechanically prove contemporaneous money financing. Conversely, an operational balance near zero does not establish public solvency. Jeffrey Sachs and Álvaro Zini argued in 1995 that Brazil in 1992–1994 exhibited unusually strong inertia and a partly endogenous monetary base; government planners placed greater weight on fiscal discipline as the foundation of credibility. Both perspectives illuminate the distinction between the **immediate mechanism of inflation** and the **long-run durability of a stable unit of account**. [Sachs and Zini, 1995](https://www.scielo.br/j/rep/a/5kRPY7Tfs6KnFxKqGx7kYjR/?format=html&lang=pt).

A conceptual decomposition separates transmission mechanisms without claiming estimated causal weights:

```text
inflation_t ≈ persistence × inflation_(t−1)
            + cost and exchange-rate shocks
            + nominal demand pressure
            + expectation changes
```

This is not an econometric regression fitted to 1994. Coefficients can change, effects interact and causation may run both ways. The point is to prevent two errors: crediting new banknotes alone with the stabilization, and attributing all previous inflation to a single fiscal statistic.

## 3. The economic ideas behind the URV

Luiz Carlos Bresser-Pereira and Yoshiaki Nakano made important contributions to the theory of inertial inflation. Francisco Lopes developed another approach to inflation and stabilization; André Lara Resende and Pérsio Arida proposed indexed-money and coordinated disinflation schemes in 1984. These projects were not identical. Their authors disagreed over relative-price realignment, price controls, fiscal preconditions and how a monetary transition should occur.

The 1994 mechanism was not a direct reproduction of any single 1984 paper. A design with two simultaneously spendable currencies differed from the URV, a legally recognized reference standard that did not circulate as payment. That difference reduced the risk of competing payment systems while firms and households learned a new price scale. Only after contracts and price quotations had been largely converted would the reference unit become money. [Bresser-Pereira, history of inertial inflation](https://www.scielo.br/j/rec/a/4ZqfqsrqsHwFJV9QjJWYsfN/); [Lara Resende, indexed money](https://new.scielo.br/j/rep/a/jkypRL4QtmZY9ZDpq4qhq7r/?lang=pt).

Calling the URV a virtual currency can be misleading. It was not a digital account balance in a payment network, a freely redeemable token or a separate monetary asset that holders could spend. It was a **legal unit of reference**, given a daily value in cruzeiros reais by the monetary authority. The institutional innovation becomes clearer if money's three functions are separated: denominating prices, extinguishing payment obligations and preserving value over time. Brazil first introduced the accounting function, postponed the payment function and made the value-preservation function dependent on subsequent policy and credibility.

## 4. Political power between Collor's removal and the 1994 election

Fernando Collor's impeachment in 1992 brought Itamar Franco to the presidency amid political disorganization and accelerating inflation. The administration's weakness might have prevented a gradual plan requiring Congress, ministries, banks, companies and labor organizations to cooperate. Yet the absence of a credible alternative also made a stabilization coalition possible. Fernando Henrique Cardoso became finance minister in May 1993 and brought together economists including Edmar Bacha, Pérsio Arida, André Lara Resende, Gustavo Franco and Pedro Malan, whose institutional responsibilities changed during the operation.

Itamar was not merely an observer: the political authority to support the plan, negotiate legislation and withstand risks belonged to the presidency and Congress. Fernando Henrique became the plan's most visible political messenger and subsequently benefited electorally from the rapid restoration of confidence. It is plausible that successful stabilization materially influenced the 1994 election; it would still be a causal overstatement to explain the entire vote by disinflation while ignoring party alliances, candidate visibility and regional electoral structures.

The government chose to announce the plan's stages rather than conceal the entire reform until launch. The February 1994 statement of reasons recognized that policy announcements alone could not secure lasting stabilization and called for continuity across administrations. After repeated surprise freezes, a publicly specified rule was an attempt to rebuild trust: households and businesses could calculate conversions and observe whether officials kept their commitments. [Original explanation published 2 March 1994](https://www1.folha.uol.com.br/fsp/1994/3/02/brasil/52.html).

## 5. The first phase: fiscal measures without a magic solution

The June 1993 Immediate Action Program combined spending management, revenue collection, collection of debts owed by subnational governments, public-bank reforms and a continuation of privatization. The Social Emergency Fund, approved through political bargaining in early 1994, gave the federal budget additional flexibility by releasing specified earmarked revenues. The March explanatory statement records a compromise that increased part of the proposed unearmarking from 15% to 20%, while protecting certain state and municipal revenue-sharing funds through compensating measures.

The fund was a genuine institutional instrument, not proof of permanent solvency. Moving revenue from mandatory earmarks to more flexible use can ease execution in the short run; it is not a substitute for expenditure reform, efficient taxation or sustainable debt management. It can also change the struggle over federal and social spending priorities. Early monetary stabilization did not settle those federalism and distribution questions.

An overlooked mechanism is the **Tanzi effect**. If taxes are remitted weeks after they are incurred, inflation erodes their real value. Disinflation, improved collection and shorter settlement delays can raise real revenue without changing statutory tax rates. However, the government could not simply book those future gains before achieving stability. At the same time, the loss of inflation-generated revenue could expose spending previously obscured by rising nominal prices.


## 6. The second phase: two units without two means of payment

Provisional Measure 434 of 27 February 1994 introduced the legal architecture later consolidated in Law 8,880 of 27 May. On 1 March, one URV was worth **CR$647.50**. The Central Bank published a daily conversion; on 2 March, the rate was CR$657.50, and on 30 June it reached **CR$2,750**. On 1 July one URV became one real. The URV was legally part of the monetary system as a unit of value; the cruzeiro real remained the means of payment until the currency change. [Central Bank historical account](https://www.bcb.gov.br/controleinflacao/planoreal); [Law 8,880, arts. 1–5](https://www2.camara.leg.br/legin/fed/lei/1994/lei-8880-27-maio-1994-372593-normaatualizada-pl.html).

| Date | Legal relationship | Function |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 27 Feb 1994 | MP 434 establishes URV | Initial legal instrument |
| 1 Mar 1994 | 1 URV = CR$647.50 | Unit of account; payments in CR$ |
| 2 Mar 1994 | 1 URV = CR$657.50 | Updated daily conversion |
| 27 May 1994 | Law 8,880 | Consolidates contract conversion |
| 30 Jun 1994 | 1 URV = CR$2,750.00 | Final cruzeiro real conversion |
| 1 Jul 1994 | 1 URV = R$1.00 | Real becomes accounting and payment unit |
| 29 Jun 1995 | Law 9,069 | Consolidation of Real Plan law |

Under the old regime, buyers watched a succession of nominal price increases and estimated what goods might cost by payday. Under the interim arrangement, an item could be offered for ten URVs while the checkout payment remained in cruzeiros reais. **A purely arithmetic illustration, not an observed historical price:** 10 URVs would have meant CR$6,475 on 1 March, CR$27,500 on 30 June, and R$10 from 1 July. The larger number of cruzeiros reais alone would not establish a price increase in URV. If the seller instead raised the item's price from 10 to 12 URVs, that would be a genuine **20% increase in URV**.

The two operations are straightforward:

```text
URV price_t = CR$ price_t ÷ daily CR$/URV rate_t
CR$ payment_t = URV price_t × daily CR$/URV rate_t
1 July 1994: R$ price = prior URV price
```

Rules were not identical across all obligations. Law 8,880 kept cruzeiro real amounts mandatory on labels and invoices while permitting parallel URV quotations. Certain contracts entered into from 15 March with settlement after more than 30 days had to be denominated in URV. Savings deposits, bank assets and liabilities, mortgages, agricultural loans, insurance and securities had separate transitional provisions. Claims that all URV adoption was voluntary are thus accurate only for some retail price quotations, not the entire legal framework. [Law 8,880, arts. 7–16](https://www2.camara.leg.br/legin/fed/lei/1994/lei-8880-27-maio-1994-372593-normaatualizada-pl.html).

The daily rate reflected the declining purchasing power of the cruzeiro real, with indices including IGP-M, IPCA-E and IPC-Fipe. The URV's approximate relationship with the US dollar added credibility. This was **not** an irrevocable legal promise to redeem each unit for one dollar, nor did it make the later real a currency-board system. The new real did not operate under a permanently symmetrical dollar bid and offer at one-to-one. A reference anchor and a foreign-currency redemption right are fundamentally different institutions.

## 7. Averaging wages: protection against losses and distributional compromise

The conversion faced a difficult question: which wage belonged in the new money? With monthly inflation around forty percent, the same nominal wage carried very different purchasing power at the beginning and end of a bargaining cycle. Converting the **peak** immediately after an adjustment could increase real costs across the economy; converting the **trough** just before the next adjustment could impose permanent losses on workers. The legal solution aimed to preserve average real remuneration, rather than the last nominal payslip figure.

Article 19 of Law 8,880 required an arithmetic mean of four months for ordinary workers: November and December 1993 and January and February 1994. Each monthly nominal wage was divided by the URV conversion rate on its **actual payment date**. It was not an average of cruzeiro real banknote amounts or a retroactive application of the June conversion rate. For the statutory minimum wage, Article 18 used the relevant month-end rates, with express protection against receiving less in cruzeiros reais than the February amount. Variable pay, annual bonuses and collective bargaining required specific treatment. [Law 8,880, arts. 18–19 and 26–27](https://www2.camara.leg.br/legin/fed/lei/1994/lei-8880-27-maio-1994-372593-normaatualizada-pl.html).

Suppose, only for mathematical illustration, four monthly salaries correctly expressed in URV were 100, 120, 110 and 130. Their mean would be **115 URVs**. These are not historical payroll observations. The relevant operation is:

```text
URV base salary =
  (Nov salary/URV on actual Nov pay date +
   Dec salary/URV on actual Dec pay date +
   Jan salary/URV on actual Jan pay date +
   Feb salary/URV on actual Feb pay date) / 4
```

The rule was designed to neutralize gains and losses arbitrarily generated by choosing a particular freeze date. It could not eliminate all conflict. Workers comparing the new figure to their last payslip might perceive a cut; wage earners with irregular payments, informal arrangements, variable compensation or collective agreements could have different outcomes. The law included base-date adjustment rules and special procedures for certain conversion losses. Distributional evaluation must compare **actual purchasing power**, not simply the numbers printed before and after the reform.

Existing inflation already distributed losses according to defensive capacity. Workers with intermittent adjustments or no liquid interest-bearing assets were particularly exposed. Firms with pricing power and rapid inventory turnover could protect themselves more effectively. The URV did not create equal economic power; it supplied a common rule intended to keep staggered adjustment calendars from determining winners and losers at the moment inflation stopped.

## 8. Relative prices, contracts and the limits of coordination

A stable unit of account does not guarantee that relative prices are right. If rents were unusually high compared with wages, or a regulated utility tariff had been held artificially low, simple redenomination would preserve those distortions until new adjustments occurred. The URV could remove automatic nominal propagation, but not scarcity, conflicts over income shares, concentrated market power or productivity shocks.

The reform therefore did not impose a universal freeze on URV prices. Sellers could adjust quotations, while buyers could compare and challenge them. Deindexation depended on learning: if firms preemptively increased prices in the new reference unit, the URV could develop its own inflation before new banknotes appeared. Bresser-Pereira warned in 1994 about this possibility of strategic repricing in the new unit. [Bresser-Pereira, political economy of the Real Plan](https://www.scielo.br/j/rep/a/G6VvwvLfN6tqvZxy6RdCv5n/?format=html).

Long-term contracts raised more difficult problems. Installment prices could embed expected inflation, financing and risk margins. Simply carrying the whole amount into URV could preserve an excessive inflation premium in the real price. Removing too much could harm creditors or suppliers. Legislation regulated the renegotiation of public contracts, the calculation of average amounts and adjustments to expected inflation in specified circumstances. No single division rule would have been appropriate for wages, taxes, public tariffs, mortgages, inventories and supply contracts.

```flow
Fiscal or external shock → price/cost adjustment → indexed clauses → staggered updates → inflation persistence
Legally organized URV conversion → real-value comparisons → relative-price bargaining → weaker automatic past-inflation transmission
Expected stability → greater willingness to hold money → lower circulation velocity → consistent monetary policy required
Fiscal credibility and banking supervision → more durable monetary regime → lower risk of inflation recurrence
```

The arrows describe hypothesized mechanisms, not estimated effect sizes or inevitable causation. Monetary demand, credibility, inflation expectations and policy responses interact.

## 9. Currency launch day: banknotes, bookkeeping, banks and logistics

A reform flawless on paper but chaotic at checkout counters could have destroyed its credibility on the first day. The Central Bank and the Mint coordinated banknotes, coins, distribution and accounting instructions. The Central Bank reports that by 30 June more than **940 million banknotes** and **688 million coins** of the new currency had already been distributed nationwide. Banks had to accept deposits in the new unit, collect cruzeiros reais and retire obsolete notes. [Central Bank, currency logistics](https://www.bcb.gov.br/controleinflacao/planoreal).

Law 8,880 established 1 July as the beginning of issuance; subsequent rules provided for the old cruzeiro real banknotes to remain temporarily usable for a limited period. Consumers had to learn notes, centavos, correct change and conversion tables. Shortages of banknotes, inadequate till software or contradictory signs could have produced arbitrage, disputes and accusations of fraud.

The transition was also an information-systems overhaul. Checking accounts, ATMs, payroll applications, financial contracts, corporate balance sheets, tax systems and ledgers all had to agree on the new scale while preserving existing rights. It resembled changing the unit of measurement of an entire economy. System engineering was not incidental: a conversion bug could transfer wealth from one contractual party to another.

## 10. The measured break: from 47.43% IPCA to 6.84%

The June–July comparison is the most visible result, but it needs exact definitions. Brazil's **monthly IPCA** was **47.43% in June 1994**, **6.84% in July** and **1.86% in August**. Inflation did not drop to zero immediately. Nor should monthly inflation be confused with a trailing twelve-month rate. The latter had reached **4,922.46% in June**, a completely different measure from that month's 47.43%. ## 11. Monetary psychology: when centavos began to matter again

High inflation changes concrete behavior. Holding cash for weeks seemed irrational; moving wages rapidly into interest-bearing investments, buying goods early, keeping dollar savings or stockpiling necessities could be rational for people with the means. Ignoring small change and rounding prices became normal while the payment unit rapidly lost value. Credibility cannot return by speech alone: it must be reinforced through everyday purchases, paydays and household bills.

The URV allowed a learning period before physical currency exchange. People observed comparatively stable prices in the new unit while cruzeiro real price tags kept changing. Shopkeepers had to distinguish a change in the old-currency conversion from a genuine adjustment in the item's URV price. A positive feedback mechanism was possible: when buyers and sellers trust the new reference, preventive repricing becomes less necessary; when trust falters, any isolated jump may be read as a sign of another failed plan.

Stabilization also changes time horizons. Saving for a goal, comparing household budgets across months and negotiating longer-term finance become more practical when next month's inflation is not assumed to be extreme. It would be a mistake to romanticize this transformation: poor households still faced unemployment, informality, expensive credit and regional inequality. Nonetheless, removing the need to immediately dispose of a wage altered households' ability to plan. [Central Bank, 30 years of the real](https://bcb.gov.br/controleinflacao/30anosreal).


## 12. Distribution, labor and consumption: who gained and who bore the costs?

Stabilization mattered especially to households unable to transfer paychecks immediately into indexed financial instruments. For a family spending a large share of income on food and transport, the delay between receiving cash and purchasing essentials was itself a loss. Lower inflation reduced this penalty for limited financial access and made cash holding less punishing. But the size of the benefit varied with pay adjustments, employment, location, bank access and household spending patterns.

Initial gains should not be equated with an automatic reduction in inequality. A dismissed or unemployed worker can lose income even while inflation collapses. Producers of tradable goods may struggle with cheaper imports while consumers of the same goods benefit. Fixed-rate debtors and creditors can experience very different results when expected inflation falls. Banks lose inflation-dependent revenue but may gain from credit expansion, fees and other activities. Distribution requires balance-sheet analysis of each group, not two undifferentiated camps of winners and losers.

Research comparing the cost of Brazil's basic food basket with statutory wages and household earnings found improved food affordability for low-income groups from 1995 to 1997, largely through higher effective incomes, followed by partial deterioration after 1997. This finding cannot be generalized without qualification to all rural households, municipalities or labor categories, and does not attribute every change solely to the URV. [Nova Economia, food affordability after stabilization](https://revistas.face.ufmg.br/novaeconomia/pt_BR/article/view/2223).

The political importance of stability in everyday life lay in predictability. A fixed installment meant something more definite across time; inflation no longer automatically rewarded those with the best financial defenses and penalized those paid late. Yet credit access, formal employment and asset ownership remained highly unequal. Disinflation removed one structural source of unfairness without erasing others.

## 13. Banks and the disappearance of the inflation business

High inflation generated banking revenue from *float*: transitory funds kept between receipt and payment, or non-interest-bearing balances that could be placed at high nominal rates. Some of these returns vanished quickly with stabilization. Systems designed to manage monetary correction had to find new sources of revenue and adjust credit risk, capital adequacy, liquidity and costs.

The very decline in inflation that encouraged deposits and lending exposed fragile balance sheets. Fast credit expansion without careful borrower assessment could produce defaults. Institutions previously shielded by inflation-related gains became more dependent on asset quality. The Financial System Restructuring and Strengthening Program, PROER, established under National Monetary Council Resolution 2,208 of 6 November 1995, sought to secure liquidity and solvency through Central Bank-authorized reorganizations. The resolution documents the institution's design; it does not establish that every subsequent operation was efficient or distributionally neutral. [Resolution 2,208/1995](https://www.bcb.gov.br/pre/normativos/res/1995/pdf/res_2208_v2_l.pdf).

Payment systems changed too. In an economy without monthly price increases of tens of percentage points, banks could move beyond services dedicated chiefly to protecting balances against immediate erosion. Longer-maturity lending and greater transparency about real interest rates became possible. The risk shifted: mortgages, household borrowing and sovereign liabilities now depended more directly on actual repayment capacity and financing costs, not merely nominal indexation.

## 14. The dollar: an anchor of confidence and a source of vulnerability

During the transition, the dollar served as an international comparison and a psychological support for the new unit. That made sense in an economy that had lost faith in its own currencies. But exchange-rate policy also has real economic effects. An appreciated domestic currency lowers the local price of imports and some inputs, disciplining pricing among import-competing producers. At the same time, it compresses margins for exporters and exposed industries, potentially reducing output and employment in particular sectors.

Instead of fixing a symmetric and permanent R$1-for-US$1 conversion, authorities initially used asymmetric interventions and later managed exchange-rate bands. The bands established in March 1995 were abandoned in January 1999. Sachs and Zini already warned in 1995 that initial appreciation—with the dollar costing fewer than one real during the second half of 1994—required attention to reserves, external balances and interest rates. [Sachs and Zini, 1995](https://www.scielo.br/j/rep/a/5kRPY7Tfs6KnFxKqGx7kYjR/?format=html&lang=pt); [Central Bank, 1999 foreign-exchange policy](https://www.bcb.gov.br/rex/MerCambio/Port/cambio991/1999-1Pol%C3%ADticaCambial.asp?frame=1).

Appreciation creates an intertemporal trade-off. In the short term, cheaper imported goods help disinflation and raise consumption. Over a longer period, current-account deficits need financing from abroad. When inflows dry up because of financial crisis, political uncertainty or worries about debt, the central bank may sell reserves and raise rates to defend the currency, with fiscal and productive consequences. Appreciation is not necessarily the only driver of external deterioration: activity, terms of trade, trade policy and investment flows also matter.

## 15. Interest rates, reserves, debt and the bill for the new regime

Interest rates were central to containing demand, remunerating real-denominated assets and managing currency pressure, especially after international shocks. Yet high rates transfer income to holders of interest-bearing claims, increase business borrowing costs, burden debtors and raise public financing expenses depending on maturity and indexation. A fall in the inflation component of nominal rates does **not** automatically imply lower **real** interest rates.

Monetary policy also faced a paradox: when households regain trust in a currency, they wish to hold more real money balances. Growth in the money stock during remonetization may accommodate rising money demand rather than indicate inflationary indiscipline. Correct diagnosis requires expected inflation, liquidity preference, activity and bank lending to be considered together.

Using contemporary definitions, Rubens Penha Cysne reported an operational deficit averaging close to zero in 1990–1993 but **4.27% of GDP** in 1995–1997. The same historical overview documented a deterioration in the external current account and a change in the ratio of money balances to output. These period averages are not independent estimates of the Real Plan's causal effects and should not be merged mechanically with contemporary fiscal series. [FGV/EPGE, macroeconomic variables 1994–1997](https://epge.fgv.br/users/rubens/brazilian-monetary-and-exchange-rate-policy-meetings/aspectos-macroeconomicos-da-economia-brasileira-no-periodo-1994-1997/).

Short-run purchasing-power gains could coexist with deferred macroeconomic costs. This was the plan's central tension: nominal stability favored investment and planning, while exchange-rate defense could make capital expensive and magnify exposure to global liquidity changes. Praising cheap consumption alone or condemning high rates alone omits half the causal relationship.

## 16. Mexico, Argentina and Israel: comparable disinflation, different institutions

International comparison clarifies the URV's distinct contribution. Israel in 1985 combined fiscal, monetary and nominal measures to halt extremely high inflation. Its experience illustrated the importance of coordination but did not employ precisely the Brazilian sequence of a temporary unit of account followed by currency issue. Argentina's convertibility system from 1991 used a much stricter peso–dollar commitment that dominated its monetary regime and contributed to later weaknesses. Brazil used an exchange reference without giving holders an irrevocable dollar-redemption right at a fixed rate.

Mexico's late-1994 currency crisis demonstrated how rapidly emerging-market capital flows could reverse when concerns about reserves and creditworthiness grew. Brazil responded to regional pressures through interest rates, intervention and managed bands from 1995. Later Asian crises in 1997 and Russia's in 1998 intensified pressure. The lesson was not that every exchange anchor was inherently mistaken; a nominal anchor can buy time to stop inflation while making its own defense costly.
