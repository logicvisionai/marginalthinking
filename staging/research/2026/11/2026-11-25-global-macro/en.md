# Tariff threats return as inflation keeps rates in play

*Global Macro — 25 November 2026 | Information cut-off: 07:45 BRT*

## Executive assessment

Global markets entered Wednesday under pressure from two risks that reinforce one another but should not be confused. The first is the possibility of a new round of US import duties following the investigation into structural excess industrial capacity in 16 trading partners. The second is evidence that price pressures remain difficult to contain in economies with different demand and monetary conditions. Australia's October inflation accelerated, while Japan's services producer-price index also moved higher. In the United States, consumer confidence rose sharply and investors were waiting for October PCE inflation and the second estimate of third-quarter GDP. [1–9]

No new tariff schedule had been formally published by the information cut-off. Reuters reported that the US Trade Representative was preparing to release findings from the existing investigation and that additional import duties were expected. The distinction is important: the investigation is real, but the market was reacting to the possibility and scope of future measures, not to a complete tariff schedule already in force. [1–4]

The price data do not describe one common inflation process. Australia's headline CPI rose to 4.9% year on year and trimmed-mean inflation to 3.8%. Japan's services producer-price index increased 4.2%, but the measure excluding international transportation rose 3.2%. Those series cover different baskets and transaction stages. They point to pressure in household prices and service-input costs, not to a directly comparable inflation rate across both countries. [5,7]

The US consumer-confidence index climbed to 103.3 in November, its highest reading since January 2025. The latest advance estimate put third-quarter real GDP growth at a 3.4% annual rate. These indicators suggest that demand had not collapsed before the new tariff threat emerged. Stronger demand can make it easier for companies to pass on imported costs, while persistent inflation can limit the Federal Reserve's room to respond with lower rates. [8–10]

The central risk is therefore not that tariffs automatically produce a global recession or a permanent inflation shock. It is that trade restrictions add costs and uncertainty at a time when several central banks still face inflation above target. The result could be slower investment, more expensive imported inputs and a longer period of restrictive financing, even if aggregate output continues to grow.

## 1. Washington's investigation has become a market event before it becomes a tariff schedule

The US Trade Representative initiated Section 301 investigations on 11 March 2026 into alleged structural excess capacity and production in manufacturing sectors. The 16 economies named by USTR were China, the European Union, Singapore, Switzerland, Norway, Indonesia, Malaysia, Cambodia, Thailand, South Korea, Vietnam, Taiwan, Bangladesh, Mexico, Japan and India. The European Union is a trading bloc, so the group comprises 16 economies rather than 16 individual countries. [3]

The investigation covers more than one industry. USTR's accompanying fact sheet identifies sectors including steel, aluminium, automobiles, batteries, chemicals, electronics, machinery, robotics, semiconductors, ships, solar modules and transportation equipment. That is an illustrative description of the investigation's industrial scope, not a statement that every product in every sector will face a new duty. [4]

| Economies named in the Section 301 excess-capacity investigation |

The legal process matters because an investigation does not impose a tariff by itself. USTR must assess the policies under review and can propose a response after the procedural steps and consultations. Reuters reported that officials were preparing to publish findings and that new duties on a wide range of goods were expected. Reuters reporting on the negotiations also pointed to differentiated ceilings in existing trade arrangements. Officials were expected to respect a 15% ceiling for goods from the European Union, Japan and South Korea, and a 19% ceiling for Malaysia. For China, the 20% cap on second-term duties was linked to an extension of the trade truce then due to expire on 10 January 2027; without an extension, the reported excess-capacity rate could rise. Mexico and Norway were not expected to face immediate additional levies. [2,11] These are reported parameters and expectations, not final rates adopted in a published USTR determination.

| Partner or arrangement | Reported tariff parameter | Status at the information cut-off |
|---|---|---|
| European Union, Japan, South Korea | 15% ceiling | Existing trade-deal ceiling expected to be respected |
| Malaysia | 19% ceiling | Existing trade-deal ceiling expected to be respected |
| China | 20% cap on second-term duties, conditional on extending the truce | Truce then due to expire on 10 January 2027 |
| Mexico, Norway | No immediate additional levies expected | Source-reported expectation, not a general exemption |

The prospect is nevertheless sufficient to change commercial decisions. Importers may bring forward orders, ask suppliers to absorb part of a potential duty or hold larger inventories. Manufacturers considering a new plant or a supplier change may delay a decision until they know whether the measure applies to the finished product, an intermediate input or the country where the good receives its final substantial transformation. These choices can affect shipping schedules and working-capital needs before a tariff appears in customs data.

The market response was not a uniform liquidation across assets. Reuters' early Wednesday snapshot showed MSCI's global equity gauge down 0.4% and Asian shares outside Japan down 3.2%, while European shares were up 0.5%. In the previous US session, the S&P 500 fell 0.5%, the Nasdaq lost 1.5%, Nvidia declined 5.9% and Anthropic fell 6.5%. The dollar index rose 0.13% to 103.97; the US ten-year Treasury yield was little changed at 4.898%; Brent slipped 1% to US$90.56 a barrel. These figures refer to different market windows within the same Reuters report and should not be treated as simultaneous closing prices. [1]

The relative resilience of European shares and the small change in the US ten-year yield argue against describing the move as a generalized financial crisis. The concentration of losses in Asian technology markets is consistent with their exposure to semiconductor and electronics supply chains, but it also reflects the reversal of a technology-led rally. A tariff headline can accelerate a valuation adjustment without proving that final demand for chips, data-centre equipment or industrial goods has deteriorated.

## 2. Australia's inflation data make rapid easing harder to defend

The Australian Bureau of Statistics reported that headline CPI rose 4.9% in the twelve months to October, up from 4.3% in September. The trimmed mean, which removes unusually large price movements at either end of the distribution, increased from 3.6% to 3.8%. The seasonally adjusted monthly CPI increase was 0.7%; the unadjusted monthly movement was 0.5%. [5]

The rise was not limited to a single volatile item. Housing prices were 6.4% higher than a year earlier, while transport was 10.6% higher. The trimmed-mean result is particularly relevant because it also accelerated. It does not eliminate every measurement issue in a monthly CPI series, but it makes it harder to explain the headline increase solely through temporary fuel or food movements.

The Reserve Bank of Australia had raised its cash-rate target by 25 basis points to 4.85% on 3 November. Its statement described inflation as broadly based and forecast that inflation would remain above 3% for the coming year, with a return to the top of the 2–3% target range not expected until late 2027. The October CPI release adds evidence that the disinflation process is not proceeding smoothly. It does not, on its own, determine the next rate decision; the RBA will also assess wages, activity, employment and the composition of price increases. [6]

Australia is not one of the 16 economies in the specific Section 301 excess-capacity investigation. Its relevance is indirect: a trade conflict involving major Asian suppliers can change input costs, export demand and shipping patterns across the region, while domestic inflation limits the scope for monetary easing. The Australian data therefore reinforce the broader policy problem without being evidence that US tariffs have caused Australian inflation.

## 3. Japan's service prices show why freight and domestic costs need to be separated

The Bank of Japan's October Services Producer Price Index rose 4.2% from a year earlier, compared with 3.8% in September. The index excluding international transportation increased 3.2%, up from 3.0%. On a monthly basis, the all-items index rose 1.1%, while the measure excluding international transportation increased 1.0%. The release is preliminary and measures prices charged by service providers, not household consumer-price inflation. [7]

The difference between the two measures is analytically important. International air and ocean freight, tanker services, international air freight and international mail are included in the headline index but removed from the adjusted reference series. The faster headline increase therefore contains a meaningful external-transport component. The 3.2% increase excluding those services indicates that the pressure is not confined to international freight, but it is materially lower than the headline rate.

```chart

Oct 2026 all services | 4.2
Sep 2026 excluding international transportation | 3.0
Oct 2026 excluding international transportation | 3.2
```

For the Bank of Japan, service prices matter because they can reflect labour costs and firms' ability to pass those costs to customers. The latest release is consistent with a gradual normalization of pricing behaviour, but it does not demonstrate that every service category is accelerating at the same rate. Nor should a producer-price index be used as a substitute for Japan's consumer-price index. Its value is in identifying a source of cost pressure that may feed into final prices with a lag. [7]

The simultaneous acceleration in Australia and Japan is not proof of a synchronized regional inflation cycle. Australia's CPI reflects household expenditure weights and domestic price formation; Japan's SPPI measures prices received by service providers. Their common implication is narrower: the cost of easing monetary policy has risen in economies where inflation pressure has not yet returned to a comfortable range.

## 4. US demand is resilient, but the next data release could change the rate debate

The Conference Board's Consumer Confidence Index rose 11.4 points to 103.3 in November, from 91.9 in October. The Present Situation Index reached 132.8 and the Expectations Index rose to 83.6. The survey covered 1–17 November. Respondents reported improved assessments of business and labour-market conditions and slightly higher intentions to spend on goods and vacations. Confidence remains a survey measure; it does not record actual household outlays. [8]

The latest BEA advance estimate showed that real US GDP grew at a 3.4% annual rate in the third quarter. Investment and consumer spending contributed to growth, while exports declined and imports increased. Real final sales to private domestic purchasers rose 4.9% at an annual rate. The second estimate was scheduled for 25 November, alongside the October Personal Income and Outlays release. [9,10]

| US release scheduled for 25 November | Reference period | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| GDP, second estimate and corporate profits | Q3 2026 | Revises the output and profit picture |
| Personal income and outlays | October 2026 | Measures income and household spending |
| PCE price index and core PCE | October 2026 | Inflation measures followed closely by the Fed |

The BEA scheduled both releases for 8:30 a.m. Eastern Time, equivalent to 10:30 a.m. in Brasília. They had not been released at the 07:45 BRT cut-off. No October PCE result or revised GDP figure is inferred here. The previous GDP advance estimate remains the latest observed national-accounts figure, and market participants were waiting for the new price data before reassessing the December policy outlook. [1,9,10]

A strong confidence reading and solid private domestic demand do not rule out a slowdown. They do, however, complicate the argument for rapid monetary easing while inflation remains above target. If tariffs raise the price of imported consumer goods or intermediate inputs, resilient demand could make it easier for firms to pass through part of the increase. If demand weakens at the same time, companies may instead absorb more of the cost in margins. The actual outcome will depend on product substitutability, inventories, market concentration, exchange rates and the ability of consumers to switch suppliers.

## 5. How a tariff shock travels through prices, investment and financing

The direct effect of a tariff is a tax on covered imports. The importer can absorb it, negotiate a lower price from the supplier, change the source country or pass some of the cost to a downstream buyer. The share paid by each party depends on bargaining power and the availability of alternatives. A tariff announcement does not reveal the final incidence.

The broader effect can arrive through three channels. First, customs duties and compliance costs alter landed prices and the relative profitability of sourcing locations. Second, uncertainty about coverage and future policy can delay investment, especially when projects depend on imported machinery, semiconductors, components or specialised materials. Third, retaliation or trade diversion can change export volumes and prices in markets that were not initially targeted.

```flow
Section 301 findings and possible duties
  → changes in landed costs and supplier incentives
  → inventory decisions, sourcing changes and delayed investment
  → margins, import prices and export demand
  → inflation expectations and central-bank reaction
  → yields, currencies and cross-border financing conditions
```

The distinction between a one-time price-level increase and persistent inflation is essential. If a tariff raises the cost of a product once, the measured price level can rise even if the annual inflation rate later falls as the base period changes. Persistence is more likely if duties are repeatedly expanded, suppliers have pricing power, wages and service prices respond, currencies depreciate or retaliation raises costs across several production stages. A tariff is therefore an inflation risk, but not a mechanical guarantee of a sustained inflation spiral.

Investment decisions can be affected before the first duty is collected. A manufacturer deciding where to locate a plant needs confidence that inputs will qualify for the relevant tariff treatment over the life of the project. Repeated changes in rules can make firms duplicate suppliers or facilities, maintain larger inventories and accept lower short-run efficiency in exchange for lower policy exposure. Such redundancy may improve resilience, but it also requires capital and can raise unit costs.

The retreat in technology shares adds a financing dimension. Semiconductor and electronics supply chains are part of the sectors named in USTR's illustrative list. At the same time, the Reuters market report linked renewed concern about interest rates to the financing structures supporting AI investment. The fall in a technology share price is not evidence that physical investment has stopped: equity prices reflect expected future cash flows and discount rates, while construction, chip orders and electricity demand adjust on different schedules. [1,4]

## 6. Regional exposure and the Brazilian channel

The same trade-policy change would not affect every region in the same way. The United States is the market imposing the possible duties, but domestic households and firms may also pay more for covered imports. China and several Asian exporters face direct uncertainty over market access. European companies face the risk of measures on products sold into the US, while also dealing with the effects of a weaker Asian technology market. Japan combines direct trade exposure with domestic service-price pressure. Australia faces a separate domestic inflation constraint and indirect regional supply-chain effects.

```map
title: Regional exposure to tariff uncertainty and persistent inflation
United States | Import costs and consumer demand | PCE and GDP releases remain ahead at the information cut-off
China and East Asia | Export access and technology supply chains | The 16-economy investigation includes China, Japan, Korea, Taiwan and several ASEAN economies
European Union | Industrial exports and integrated manufacturing | The EU is explicitly named as one of the investigated economies
Japan | Export exposure and service-input costs | Services producer prices accelerated, partly reflecting international transportation
Australia | Domestic inflation and regional trade | CPI accelerated; Australia is not on the 16-economy investigation list
Emerging markets | Trade diversion, currencies and commodity demand | Effects depend on product substitution, dollar financing and the final tariff scope
```

Brazil is not named in the specific 16-economy excess-capacity investigation launched in March. That fact does not provide a general exemption from other US trade measures, and it does not settle the indirect consequences of a dispute involving major manufacturing exporters. If duties redirect Asian exports toward other markets, Brazilian producers could face stronger import competition in selected manufactured goods. If the dispute slows investment and industrial trade, commodity exporters could face weaker demand. Both are transmission scenarios; neither is established by the current tariff reports.

For Brazilian policymakers, the external channel matters through the dollar, commodity prices, imported capital goods and financing costs. A stronger dollar can raise the local-currency cost of imported equipment and foreign-currency obligations. Lower global demand can weigh on export prices, while trade diversion can create opportunities for some suppliers and competitive pressure for others. These effects will differ by product and cannot be inferred from a single global equity index.

## 7. Scenarios, tests and the next information window

The base case is that USTR publishes findings and the administration considers additional duties, but the final scope and implementation are narrower or slower than the most adverse market interpretation. Companies delay some sourcing decisions, risk premia remain sensitive to headlines and central banks wait for more evidence on prices and demand. Global output continues to expand, but trade and investment become less predictable.

An upside scenario would involve limited product coverage, exemptions or negotiated arrangements that prevent a broad rise in effective import costs. US PCE inflation would moderate, the second GDP estimate would leave the private-demand picture broadly intact, and subsequent Australian and Japanese releases would show that October's acceleration was concentrated in identifiable categories. Under these conditions, the market could treat the tariff episode as a temporary uncertainty premium rather than a lasting cost shock.

A downside scenario would combine broad duties with retaliation by affected economies. Firms would face higher landed costs and more difficult market access at the same time that service inflation and household prices remained elevated. If the dollar strengthened and financing costs rose, investment plans could be delayed while imported equipment became more expensive. The risk would be larger if tariff measures extended to capital goods, semiconductors and intermediate inputs rather than a limited set of final products.

The thesis would weaken if USTR's findings produced no material additional duties, if any new measures were confined to narrow products with broad exemptions, and if market pricing normalized without a sustained change in trade volumes or investment plans. It would also weaken if PCE and subsequent service-price data showed broad disinflation, while Australian trimmed-mean inflation and Japan's service-price measures returned toward their earlier paths.

The immediate watch list is the publication of USTR's findings, the October US PCE release and the revised third-quarter GDP estimate. The next rounds of Chinese manufacturing data and central-bank communication will help establish whether the trade-policy shock changes orders, inventories and pricing behaviour or remains largely a financial-market repricing.

## Conclusion

Tariff uncertainty has returned to the centre of the global market just as inflation data in Australia and Japan show that price normalization remains incomplete. The United States still has evidence of resilient consumer sentiment and strong recent output, but the next PCE release will matter for the rate outlook. The early market response has been sharp in Asian equities and technology shares, yet it has not been uniform across stocks, bonds and commodities.

The economic cost will depend on the duties actually imposed, the products covered, the availability of substitute suppliers and the response of trading partners. Until those details are known, the defensible conclusion is that trade-policy risk has increased the range of possible outcomes. It has not yet established a new tariff regime or a global recession. Persistent inflation makes that uncertainty more expensive because central banks have less room to offset a supply-side shock with easier money.
