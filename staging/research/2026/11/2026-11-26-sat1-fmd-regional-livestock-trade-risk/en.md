# SAT1 crosses regional boundaries; livestock risk now depends on surveillance and vaccine matching

*Research Report — 26 November 2026 | Information cutoff: 05:25 BRT, America/Sao_Paulo*

## Executive assessment

Foot-and-mouth disease (FMD) serotype SAT 1 is no longer a concern confined to its historical southern-African range. Official notifications compiled by the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) show first occurrences across parts of the Middle East and Asia in 2025–2026, while recent field support in Mongolia and new notifications in Africa indicate that the regional response remains active. This is a transboundary animal-health problem with direct links to livestock production, animal movement, food supply, trade access and veterinary capacity—not evidence of a human pandemic. [1] [2] [3] [4]

The newest operational signal is not a global case total. On 13 November, FAO reported that Mongolia had requested an assessment after SAT1 outbreaks in Bayan-Ulgii and Khovd provinces; a joint FAO-WOAH mission visited affected areas. WOAH's regional Africa feed lists SAT1 notifications in Botswana on 17 November and Algeria on 10 November. Mauritius reported FMD serotype O on 19 November, which is a separate serotype and must not be counted as SAT1. These notifications demonstrate continued cross-border animal-health pressure, but they do not by themselves establish a single continuous epidemic chain between the locations. [2] [3]

**Observed:** new official notifications and a field assessment; a regional 2026–2030 prevention strategy; known vaccine-match and surveillance constraints. **Inference:** countries that rely on delayed reporting, weak veterinary services or informal livestock movement face higher exposure and greater trade disruption risk. **Scenario, not fact:** if SAT1 reaches additional susceptible herds before matching vaccines and movement controls are available, local production losses and temporary trade restrictions could widen. No global loss estimate or food-price impact is established by the sources reviewed.

## 1. A serotype expansion is different from a single outbreak count

FMD is a highly contagious viral disease affecting cloven-hoofed animals, including cattle, pigs, sheep and goats. WOAH notes that infection can reduce milk and meat production and that detection commonly triggers restrictions on movements of live animals and animal products. Those controls protect animal health and trading partners, but they can also interrupt market access and income for producers. [1]

SAT1 is one of several FMD virus serotypes. A first notification in a country means that the serotype was reported there for the first time through the official system; it does not mean that the country had no FMD previously, that every herd is infected or that the entire country is affected. WOAH's August 2026 summary records first SAT1 occurrences in Iraq, Kuwait, Egypt and Azerbaijan in 2025, and in Israel, Cyprus, Palestine, China and Mongolia in 2026. It also records renewed notifications in Türkiye, Lebanon and Greece after about six decades. The Middle East regional page, updated 24 November, describes SAT1 reaching the region in 2025–2026 and identifies the regional strategy now guiding prevention and control. [1] [4]

## 2. November's notifications show active surveillance and response, not a proven regional wave

FAO's 13 November update states that Mongolia requested support after SAT1 outbreaks in Bayan-Ulgii and Khovd. A joint expert mission from FAO and WOAH conducted a field assessment of response measures and provided technical recommendations. The source describes an official request and an assessment mission; it does not provide a national incidence series, a complete outbreak count or a verified estimate of livestock losses. [2]

WOAH's regional Africa notification page lists Botswana SAT1 on 17 November and Algeria SAT1 on 10 November. It also lists Mauritius FMD serotype O on 19 November and Nigeria African swine fever on 17 November. These are distinct animal-health events. Serotype O in Mauritius is not SAT1, and African swine fever is a different disease. Separating them prevents a regional alert list from being misread as a single pathogen's spread. [3]

The notification pattern can have two simultaneous meanings. It may indicate wider circulation of a virus that has moved beyond its traditional range; it may also reflect stronger surveillance, more timely reporting or improved laboratory identification. A change in the number of notifications cannot be attributed to incidence alone without information on sampling, diagnostic capacity, reporting delays and animal populations at risk.

## 3. Vaccine matching and veterinary capacity are the operational bottlenecks

FMD control depends on identifying the serotype and selecting prevention measures compatible with the circulating virus. [8] FAO's June 2026 regional update warned that commonly used FMD vaccines in South and Southeast Asia were not fit to tackle SAT1 and described support for early warning, risk-based surveillance and rapid laboratory diagnosis in seven countries. That statement is specific to the vaccine products and regions discussed by FAO; it should not be generalized to every vaccine or every country. [5]

WOAH identifies biosecurity, vaccination, movement controls and border surveillance as preventive measures. [1] FAO's March rapid risk assessment, based on information through 23 February, assessed 17 unaffected countries and territories for exposure over the following three months. Aggregating its likelihood–impact matrix gives six very likely, nine likely and two unlikely assessments. These are qualitative categories, not observed infections or probabilities, and the three-month horizon is a historical baseline rather than a current November forecast. The assessment identifies informal animal movement, weak veterinary services and limited SAT1 vaccine availability as risk pathways. [6]

An effective response is therefore a system, not a single vaccine purchase. Laboratories need diagnostic capacity and quality assurance; veterinary services need access to herds and reliable reporting; authorities need movement-control plans that can be applied without leaving informal routes unobserved; and trading partners need timely, credible notifications. If these capabilities are missing, a technically suitable vaccine may arrive too late or coverage may be too uneven to protect the production system.

## 4. The regional strategy creates a coordination framework, not proof of implementation

FAO and regional partners launched the Middle East FMD Prevention and Control Strategy 2026–2030 on 29 September. The strategy covers 14 countries: Bahrain, Egypt, Iraq, Jordan, Kuwait, Lebanon, Libya, Oman, Palestine, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Syria, the United Arab Emirates and Yemen. It establishes a shared framework for prevention, preparedness, surveillance and control. The launch is an institutional milestone; it is not evidence that every country has funded the plan, aligned vaccine procurement, harmonized border procedures or achieved comparable surveillance. [7]


The framework matters because livestock disease control has a regional public-good character. A country can invest in vaccination and surveillance yet remain exposed to infection arriving through a neighbour with weaker coverage or reporting. The same asymmetry affects trade: a delayed or poorly documented notification can cause trading partners to impose wider restrictions than a transparent, risk-based system would require. Regional coordination can reduce that uncertainty, but only if the strategy is translated into funded national operations and interoperable reporting.

## 5. Economic transmission: production, market access and public cost

FMD affects the economy through several distinct channels. Infection can reduce milk yield, weight gain and reproductive performance; control measures can temporarily limit movement of animals and products; export certification and border checks can raise transaction costs; and governments may need to finance surveillance, vaccination, compensation or emergency veterinary support. The size of each channel varies by species, outbreak location, production structure, trade dependence and duration of restrictions. [1] [6]

The effects are not automatically equivalent to a food-price shock. A local outbreak may be absorbed by domestic supply, inventory, imports or substitution between products. A trade restriction may reduce farm-gate prices even while retail prices rise, so producer income and consumer price effects can move in opposite directions. Without affected herd counts, output losses, product-level trade data and the duration of restrictions, no defensible regional cost or price estimate can be derived from the notification list alone.

Smallholder systems may face a different burden from integrated export-oriented production. Small farms can have limited access to veterinary diagnosis, insurance, cold-chain logistics and compensation; large producers may have better biosecurity but greater exposure to export certification and concentrated processing capacity. These are mechanisms to test with country-level evidence, not assumptions about every farm in the region.

## 6. Competing interpretations and scenarios

**Base case — localized control with uneven readiness.** Official notifications continue, but countries with functioning surveillance, serotype-matched prevention and movement controls contain outbreaks locally. Trade interruptions remain product- and jurisdiction-specific. The regional strategy improves coordination gradually, while data quality and execution differ across the 14 participating countries.

**Adverse case — spread outruns response capacity.** SAT1 is detected in additional susceptible herds before diagnostics, vaccination and movement measures can be deployed. Informal animal movement and weak veterinary services undermine containment. More jurisdictions impose restrictions, livestock markets become segmented, and small producers face income and replacement-cost pressure. This is a conditional scenario, not a forecast that the disease will spread to every listed country.

**Improved-control case — surveillance and vaccination become coordinated.** FAO-WOAH field assessments lead to better national response plans, laboratories identify the serotype rapidly, vaccine matching and procurement are coordinated, and reporting becomes more timely. Trading partners can apply proportionate restrictions based on verified affected zones and surveillance evidence rather than broad uncertainty.

The adverse case would weaken if WAHIS notifications stabilize or decline while surveillance coverage remains stable, affected zones are clearly bounded, and countries report adequate vaccine matching and veterinary capacity. The improved-control case would weaken if new notifications continue despite verified high coverage, or if laboratory and field reports reveal gaps in the serotype match or implementation.

## 7. What should be monitored next

The next evidence should be operational and comparable: WAHIS event updates with dates and affected administrative areas; laboratory confirmation and serotype/topotype where reported; vaccination coverage and vaccine-match assessments by country; animal movement restrictions and their duration; milk, meat and live-animal production indicators; trade suspensions by product and destination; and public expenditure on response. The Mongolia mission's follow-up report is especially relevant because it may clarify whether the November assessment produced measurable response changes. [2]

SAT1's geographic expansion is a genuine animal-health and trade risk, but the current evidence does not justify a global livestock-loss estimate, a unified epidemic curve across all notifications, or a claim that food prices have already risen because of the disease. The strongest conclusion is institutional: early warning, laboratory capacity, vaccine matching, animal movement governance and regional reporting determine whether a serotype crossing borders becomes a wider production and trade disruption.
