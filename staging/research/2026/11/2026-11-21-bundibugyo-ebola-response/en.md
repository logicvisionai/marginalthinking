# Bundibugyo Ebola — 21 November 2026

## Response capacity has expanded, but access to communities remains the binding constraint

*Evidence cutoff: 05:00 Brasília time, 21 November 2026. Epidemiological counts are reported by source and date; they are not treated as real-time totals.*

Six months after the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) declared its Bundibugyo virus disease outbreak, the response has more laboratories, treatment beds, community workers and cross-border coordination than it had in May. The operational result remains inadequate. As of 17 November, the World Health Organization (WHO) recorded 11,613 confirmed cases and 5,678 deaths across 68 health zones in eight provinces. Forty-eight zones had reported a case in the preceding 21 days. The crude case-fatality ratio was 48.9%.

The new evidence is not simply that the outbreak remains large. It is that a growing response apparatus is still failing to identify a sufficient share of cases through known contact networks and to reach people before they die outside treatment centres. WHO measured average daily contact follow-up at 81%, below its 95% threshold. Africa CDC's six-month report found that only 11% of confirmed cases in its analysed period had been identified from existing contact lists. These are different measures, but together they point to a persistent last-mile problem: surveillance capacity and nominal treatment capacity do not automatically translate into early access, trust or continuity of care.

## 1. The six-month update: more cases, a wider footprint and uneven local progress

The WHO update published on 20 November compares the situation on 17 November with its previous report, published on 6 November. Confirmed cases increased by 945 and deaths by 480. The number of affected health zones rose from 67 to 68, while the number reporting at least one case in the previous 21 days fell from 51 to 48. Twenty affected zones had no recent case during that period. The aggregate therefore combines sustained high-level transmission with local improvement; neither a single national total nor the decline in active zones is sufficient to describe the whole outbreak. [1][2]

Deaths are included within the confirmed-case total; the bars are not additive. The crude case-fatality ratio is also not a population mortality rate. It divides reported deaths by reported confirmed cases and can change with detection, reporting delays, case classification and the timing of outcomes. It is a severity signal, not a direct estimate of the probability that any individual patient will die.

The geography is also uneven. Ituri and North Kivu remain the most affected provinces. WHO reported that North Kivu had the highest number of cases in the latest seven-day period, while Ituri retained the largest cumulative burden. Sud-Kivu and Sud-Ubangi had not reported cases for more than 42 days. The fact that some health zones have stopped reporting recent cases is meaningful, but it does not establish that transmission has ended across the country. [1]

## 2. A PHEIC is not a declaration of a pandemic emergency

The outbreak remains a public health emergency of international concern (PHEIC). On 18 November, the WHO Director-General accepted the advice of the third International Health Regulations Emergency Committee meeting, held on 17 November, to maintain that status. The distinction is important: the WHO committee's formal record states that the event did not meet the IHR criteria for a pandemic emergency. A PHEIC activates international coordination; it does not mean that sustained transmission is occurring globally. [1][9]

Cases have been reported in five countries since the outbreak was declared on 15 May: the DRC, France, Kenya, Nigeria and Uganda. The outbreaks in France and Uganda have been controlled since August. Kenya has reported nine confirmed cases, including two deaths, all linked to one imported case and a single cluster; it has reported no new confirmed case since 19 October. Nigeria reported one imported case in October, and all identified contacts had completed their follow-up by 13 November. Both countries have entered a 42-day countdown. Switzerland and the United Kingdom reported medical evacuations of health workers from the DRC; the patients recovered and were discharged. These evacuations are not evidence of sustained community transmission in those countries. [1]

WHO's 17 November risk assessment classifies risk as very high in the DRC, high in countries with confirmed cases but no community transmission, including Kenya and Nigeria, and high in some countries with substantial population connectivity or limited response capacity. It assesses risk as low in other states, with no evidence of sustained transmission beyond the DRC. The European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control likewise assessed the risk to people in the EU/EEA living in or travelling to affected areas as low on the information available on 17 November. WHO advises against travel or trade restrictions. This is a regional preparedness problem with a serious domestic emergency, not evidence of a global pandemic. [1][8][9]

## 3. The detection gap is more important than the headline number of beds

WHO reported that the average proportion of contacts seen daily over the preceding 14 days was 81% on 17 November, below the required 95% threshold. The comparable WHO figure on 3 November was 86%. The decline is a direct operational warning: a contact list is useful only when people can be found, reached, followed and supported consistently. [1][2]

Africa CDC's six-month report identifies a different weakness. In the period it analysed, only 1,146 of 10,659 confirmed cases with onset between epidemiological weeks 19 and 45 were identified through existing contact lists, or 11%. The other 89% were not found through those lists. That does not mean that 89% of cases were never investigated. It means that the response often identified people after their transmission link had not been recorded in advance. This is consistent with late detection, incomplete contact networks and community deaths, but it is not interchangeable with WHO's daily follow-up percentage. One measures how cases entered the known-contact system; the other measures the proportion of listed contacts reached each day. [4]

The operational consequence is that laboratory capacity can increase while the system still discovers infections late. More tests and more treatment beds are necessary, but they cannot substitute for reliable notification, trusted local engagement, transport, timely referral and a workforce able to follow contacts. A response can report thousands of trained workers and still miss households if people are displaced, roads are insecure, wages are delayed or local clinics are not trusted.

## 4. Community deaths and the unequal burden on children

Of the 720 deaths reported in the DRC during the three weeks to 17 November, 452 occurred in communities and 268 in treatment centres. Community deaths therefore represented 62.8% of the reported total for that period. In the preceding WHO update, 335 of 512 deaths since 21 October had occurred in communities, or 65.4%. The share improved slightly, but remained high. These deaths occurred outside dedicated treatment centres and point to delays in detection, referral or access to appropriate care; they do not, by themselves, identify the reason for each death. [1][2]

Children carry a disproportionate burden. UNICEF's data as of 4 November identified 2,959 confirmed cases among children under 18 and 1,737 deaths. Children represented 28% of confirmed cases for which age data were available and 35% of deaths. Among children under five with confirmed infection, nearly 72% had died. The WHO Director-General's 17 November remarks described the same pattern as more than one quarter of confirmed cases and one third of deaths, with seven in ten confirmed cases among children under five ending in death. The age-specific figures must be read with their stated data cutoffs and denominators; they are not a new national population mortality estimate. [3][6]

The consequences extend beyond clinical care. UNICEF reports that children have lost parents and caregivers, missed health and protection services and experienced disrupted schooling in communities already affected by conflict and displacement. DRC-wide, 14.9 million people were estimated to need humanitarian assistance in 2026, including eight million children. The outbreak therefore competes with other urgent needs for the same health workers, transport, safe facilities, water, nutrition and protection systems. [6]

## 5. Insecurity interrupts the response chain

WHO identifies rising insecurity as the greatest obstacle to expanding operations. In remarks on 17 November, the WHO Director-General reported that an attack in Cantine-Aloya earlier that month killed 66 civilians, including three health workers; health facilities were looted and burned, and more than 1,000 contacts were lost to follow-up. The joint statement issued by Africa CDC, UNFPA, UNICEF, WFP and WHO on 15 November also reported three facilities looted, more than 80 attacks on health care since the outbreak was declared and three confirmed cases that remained unaccounted for. These are claims attributed to the named institutions; this assessment does not present them as independently verified incident counts. [3][5]

The same WHO remarks reported that a nurse was killed and another health worker badly injured while responding to an alert in Tshopo province. The Director-General also said that delays in paying frontline health workers and responders were creating tension and slowing the response. These details matter because security is not an external variable separate from epidemiology. It determines whether teams can enter a health zone, whether a patient can be referred, whether contacts can be followed and whether local staff remain available. When operations stop, the surveillance picture becomes less complete precisely where uncertainty is highest. [3]

| Operational layer | Reported capacity or result | Remaining constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Diagnostics | More than two dozen laboratories, up from two | Geographic reach, timely sample transport and detection before community deaths |
| Treatment | Close to 2,000 beds in 104 treatment and transit centres | Access, staffing, referral and quality of care |
| Community workforce | More than 17,000 community health workers and 46,000 community actors trained and supplied | Safe access, payment, retention and local trust |
| Contact tracing | 81% average daily follow-up against a 95% threshold | Incomplete lists and missed follow-up |
| Safe burials | Almost 90% of registered deaths managed with safe and dignified burials | Community deaths remain high; the denominators are different |
| Food assistance | Nearly one million hot meals across 59 facilities, reaching more than 180,000 people | Wider food insecurity and disrupted livelihoods |

The capacity figures come from the five-agency statement of 15 November. They show substantial mobilization, not proof that every affected community can access those services. In particular, the 90% safe-burial figure refers to registered deaths managed through that service; it should not be compared directly with the share of deaths occurring outside treatment centres. The two indicators measure different parts of the response. [5]

## 6. Food, livelihoods and essential services are part of outbreak control

The World Food Programme's 3 November field account from Mongbwalu describes a mining town where farming had become difficult, economic activity had contracted and some merchants avoided contact with money from affected households. This is a field observation from one affected locality, not a measured estimate for the whole DRC. It nevertheless illustrates how fear, illness, displacement and restricted access can reduce household income and make routine food access more difficult. WFP reported that more than 26 million people in the DRC faced severe hunger, including about three million in Ebola-affected areas. Food assistance can support patients and health workers, reduce the need for repeated movement in search of food and make it easier for families to seek care. [7]

The joint agency statement says nearly one million hot meals had been delivered through 59 treatment, isolation and transit facilities to more than 180,000 people affected by the outbreak and response. It also reports that treatment for acute malnutrition and maternal health services were being maintained in affected areas. These are important outputs, but they do not establish that food insecurity or essential-service disruption has been resolved. The wider humanitarian system still needs to protect water, sanitation, nutrition, maternal and newborn care, child protection, mental health and education while the emergency response continues. [5][6][7]

No reliable national estimate of GDP loss, trade interruption or aggregate labour-market impact was identified in the primary sources reviewed for this edition. The report therefore does not convert local accounts of closed markets, interrupted farming or displacement into a national economic-loss figure. The systemic finding is narrower: the outbreak is taking place in a setting where food insecurity and conflict already weaken the household and public-service buffers needed to comply with health measures.

## 7. Regional readiness is improving, but it is not the same as outbreak control

A WHO report published on 20 November provides a measurable counterpoint from South Sudan. Its national readiness score rose from 29% in May to 72.5% in October after four self-assessments. Authorities identified 15 high-risk counties, strengthened surveillance and screening, established isolation capacity and conducted a full-scale simulation in October involving 93 participants from government, WHO, UN agencies and implementing partners. The simulation tested coordination, information-sharing and the hand-offs between response functions, including at priority points of entry. [10]

This is evidence of preparedness investment in a neighbouring country, not evidence that the DRC outbreak is controlled or that South Sudan has eliminated all risk. It demonstrates that readiness can be improved before an imported case is detected. The WHO risk assessment also emphasizes the difference between countries with strong response capacity and those with high connectivity but limited surveillance and access. Cross-border readiness is therefore not just screening at airports. It depends on laboratories, local health systems, referral arrangements, information-sharing and the ability to sustain operations at formal and informal crossing points. [1][10]

## 8. Scenarios and indicators to monitor

The base case is continued high-level transmission concentrated in the DRC, with local improvements in some health zones and no sustained community transmission beyond the country. Kenya and Nigeria continue their 42-day countdowns while maintaining heightened surveillance. Under this case, the main test is whether the daily contact-follow-up rate moves back toward or above 95%, the number of active health zones declines and the share of deaths occurring in communities falls.

A deterioration scenario would involve further insecurity, new attacks on health facilities, delayed payments, additional displacement or new health zones reporting cases. Contact follow-up could fall further, community deaths could remain high and cross-border readiness could be tested by another imported case. This is a conditional scenario, not a claim that such an event has occurred.

An improvement scenario would require the response to convert capacity into earlier detection and more reliable access: contact lists identifying a larger share of new cases, daily follow-up reaching the WHO threshold, fewer deaths outside treatment centres, sustained payment of frontline workers and continued access to food and essential health services. The reduction from 51 to 48 active zones is encouraging but insufficient on its own. It must be sustained across multiple reporting periods and accompanied by better detection.

The thesis would be weakened if contact follow-up reaches the 95% threshold, the share of cases found through known contacts rises materially, community deaths decline and affected zones continue to report no new cases over successive periods. It would be strengthened by persistent missed contacts, renewed geographic expansion, security incidents that interrupt operations or evidence that frontline services are not being paid and supplied. No single weekly total should replace those operational indicators.

## 9. What the evidence establishes

The outbreak remains a severe public-health emergency in the DRC and a regional preparedness concern. It is still a PHEIC, but the formal IHR record does not classify it as a pandemic emergency, and WHO reports no sustained transmission beyond the DRC. The response has expanded laboratories, beds, trained workers, food assistance and cross-border readiness. Yet the share of cases found through existing contact lists is low, daily contact follow-up remains below target and community deaths remain a large share of reported deaths.

The binding constraint is therefore not simply the number of facilities or the existence of a response plan. It is the ability to reach communities safely, identify cases early, pay and retain frontline staff, preserve essential services and sustain trust. The next phase should be judged by those operational outcomes rather than by the size of the mobilized response alone.
