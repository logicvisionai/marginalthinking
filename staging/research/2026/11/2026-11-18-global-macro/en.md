# Global Macro — European energy inflation persists as AI capital commitments expand

**18 November 2026 · Information cutoff at 16:20 Brasília time (19:20 UTC).** US and Brazilian equity closes refer to 17 November. Nvidia reported after Tuesday's regular session. The complete October euro-area and UK inflation releases published on Wednesday are included. The FOMC minutes were released at 16:00 Brasília time and are incorporated below; the Senate vote on Brazil's PLP 299/2026 remained pending at the 16:20 cutoff. [3][9][10][13][14][20]

The European Commission projects **1.2% euro-area growth in both 2026 and 2027**, with inflation of **3.2% and 3.1%**, respectively. Eurostat's complete release confirmed **4.0%** annual inflation in October, with energy prices up **21.0%**. UK CPI inflation reached **3.6%**, while Anthropic's prospectus revealed large multi-year compute commitments. Observed activity and prices, forecasts and contractual obligations have different time horizons. Comparing them requires attention to those distinctions before inferring a path for interest rates or investment. [1][2][9][10][11]

On Tuesday, US equities fell across capitalization segments: the **S&P 500 lost 1.03%**, the **Dow 1.64%**, the **Nasdaq 0.74%** and the **Russell 2000 1.50%**. The breadth matters. Semiconductor valuation concerns alone cannot account for weakness in banks and smaller companies. Bond yields reportedly eased in the session, weakening any claim that an increase in the sovereign benchmark was the immediate sole driver. This is a simultaneous market observation, not a causal identification. [3]

## 1. A forecast revision with conflicting policy implications

| Euro-area indicator | Spring 2026 forecast | Autumn 2026 forecast | Status |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| 2026 real GDP growth | 0.9% | 1.2% | Commission projections, percentage change [1][2] |
| 2027 real GDP growth | 1.2% | 1.2% | Commission projections [1][2] |
| 2026 consumer inflation | 3.0% | 3.2% | Commission projections [1][2] |
| 2027 consumer inflation | 2.3% | 3.1% | Commission projections [1][2] |
| 2027 euro-area general-government deficit | 3.5% of GDP | 3.6% of GDP | Commission projections [1][2] |
| 2028 euro-area general-government deficit | — | 3.8% of GDP | Autumn forecast only [1] |

The striking change is not a collapse in output. The Commission raised its 2026 growth estimate by 0.3 percentage point while increasing its 2027 inflation estimate by 0.8 point. The latter is larger than the growth revision and changes the distribution of costs between borrowers, savers and energy-intensive producers. It does not prove that any particular central-bank decision is predetermined. [1][2]

The Commission associates the inflation outlook with energy costs, freight and insurance, refining bottlenecks and food-price transmission. It reports that energy inflation reached almost 21% year on year in October and that unprocessed food inflation was around 4%. These are not equivalent to a measured 21% rise in every household's energy bill: baskets, contract structures and national pricing differ. Eurostat's complete 18 November release confirmed **21.0%** annual inflation in the energy component, compared with **20.8%** in the 4 November flash estimate. [1][9]

## 2. The financing chain reaches beyond the euro

```flow
Energy-route disruption → Freight, insurance and delivered fuel costs → Industrial margins and household purchasing power → Inflation persistence and public compensation costs → Sovereign financing and private credit conditions
```

A government may compensate households for energy prices while its own interest expenditure increases. The Commission expects euro-area deficits to widen to 3.8% of GDP in 2028 and sovereign debt to reach 91.3% of GDP that year. The simultaneous increase in interest costs and support measures can narrow fiscal space without implying imminent sovereign default. Financing risks differ substantially between euro-area member states, and the Commission specifically notes widening sovereign spreads. [1]

The banking channel has a different clock. Higher benchmark rates and tighter lending standards can reduce new credit demand before investment and employment visibly fall. Conversely, strong order books, existing fixed-rate contracts and export demand can cushion the effect. The Commission expects non-residential construction to be supported by public investment, while residential construction remains weak. A claim of universal industrial recession would be inconsistent with that composition. [1]

The Russell 2000's 1.50% decline and the Dow's 1.64% decline show that Tuesday's repricing was not limited to the Nasdaq. However, index returns do not measure capital outflows, new loan pricing or the number of firms facing refinancing. The Treasury yield curve should be verified against the Treasury's dated par-yield series rather than inferred from an intraday quote. The day's observed direction of government yields and risk assets does not identify the credit-spread contribution. [3][5]

A firmer dollar and lower gold prices have been reported alongside expectations of tighter US policy. That is consistent with dollar-denominated funding pressure, but exchange-rate changes have different effects on exporters and importers. The Reuters gold report dated 18 November describes a one-year low, while the actual cross-asset closing comparison must respect time zones and contract conventions. [6][8]

## 4. Oil: easing physical surplus versus persistent disruption

Brent settled at **US$93.14/barrel** on 17 November, up **0.76%** in the session, while WTI settled at **US$86.01**, up **0.34%**, according to Reuters. Reuters reported that Iranian drones struck a US military radar system in Bahrain and that a ship was hit twice in the Strait of Hormuz, with its crew unaccounted for. The US military also carried out airstrikes on Iran. Yemen's Houthis claimed five drones toward two pumping stations on Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline; this remains an attributed claim rather than a verified strike in this report. These events raise route and insurance risk, but do not establish a sustained closure or a quantified interruption of oil flows. [4]

The Commission's forecast is conditional on an energy shock lasting through 2027, with unusually expensive gas, diesel and shipping insurance. A single crude settlement cannot invalidate that broader cost forecast: refined-product margins, delivered gas prices and freight can diverge from crude. The causal test is sustained normalization of actual delivered prices and route availability, not merely a one-day fall in futures. [1][4]

## 5. Geographic and sectoral transmission

```map
title: Regional exposures to the financing and energy shock
Euro area | Energy-intensive industry and sovereign spreads | Export strength offsets some demand weakness; exposure differs by member state
United States | Equity valuation and private refinancing | Lower sovereign yields do not guarantee narrower credit spreads
Gulf and shipping corridors | Route security, oil and insurance | Reuters reported an Iranian drone strike in Bahrain and a ship hit in the Strait; the Houthi pipeline claim remains attributed; no sustained closure established
East Asia | Technology supply chains and dollar funding | Export demand may coexist with expensive imported energy
Brazil and other importers | FX pass-through and local rates | Dollar and energy effects depend on hedges, domestic taxes and monetary policy
```

In Europe, capital-goods suppliers can benefit from global AI infrastructure investment even as energy-intensive basic materials producers face higher input bills. The Commission explicitly notes support to exports of power, cooling and chip-making equipment. In emerging markets, the net effect of higher dollar rates and oil is not uniform: commodity exporters may receive additional revenues, while fuel-importing firms and governments face higher costs. This distinction requires country-specific balances and should not be converted into a ranking without comparable data. [1]


## 6. Eurostat confirms the shock and separates its sources

Eurostat's full release on 18 November confirmed **4.0%** annual inflation in the euro area in October, up from **3.8%** in September and **2.1%** in October 2025. The harmonised index increased **0.5%** during the month. Inflation across the European Union also reached **4.0%**. The final release preserved the headline rate in the flash estimate but revised energy inflation from **20.8% to 21.0%**. Reporting the flash component as the definitive result would therefore introduce a factual error. The 0.2 percentage-point revision to the energy component must also be distinguished from the 0.2-point rise in headline inflation from September to October. [9]

| Component of euro-area annual inflation | Contribution to the October headline rate |
| --- | ---: |
| Energy | +1.89 percentage points |
| Services | +1.55 percentage points |
| Non-energy industrial goods | +0.30 percentage points |
| Food, alcohol and tobacco | +0.29 percentage points |

The contributions sum to **4.03 percentage points** after rounding, while the published headline is **4.0%**. Energy contributes more than services, but service-price inflation remains important for persistence. Motor fuels and electricity may respond to international prices, taxes and contracts at different speeds. Services are more directly exposed to wages, rents, productivity and domestic demand. A week of falling Brent prices may relieve expectations for fuel without quickly reducing prices already contracted in services. The completed inflation release therefore helps test the Commission's forecast assumptions while leaving the trajectory of 2027 open to further evidence. [1][9]

Inflation also differs considerably between member states. Eurostat reported annual rates of **0.6% in Sweden**, **6.1% in Lithuania** and **7.0% in Romania**. These are harmonised national consumer-price measures and do not imply one financing cost for every government. Sovereign bond markets depend on fiscal prospects, growth, liquidity and debt structure. A bank lending to energy-intensive manufacturers in Central Europe can face a very different sector mix from one lending mainly to domestic service companies. This heterogeneity makes national spreads, loan delinquencies and new lending more informative than a regional average when assessing whether a widespread recession is under way. [1][9]

## 7. The United Kingdom and Australia show how central banks face asymmetric shocks

The Office for National Statistics reported on 18 November that UK **CPI rose 3.6%** in the twelve months to October, up from **3.5%** in September. **CPIH**, which includes owner-occupiers' housing costs, reached **3.7%**, up from **3.6%**. Motor-fuel prices rose **33.2%** over twelve months, while transport prices increased **7.5%**. The ONS reported an average diesel price of **199.5 pence per litre**. Motor fuel is a particular category with a large percentage increase, while the CPI covers a much broader consumer basket. The household-budget impact depends on whether people must travel, which fuel they use and whether they have alternative transportation. [10]

The United Kingdom's labour-market release on 17 November estimated unemployment at **4.6%** in July–September, down **0.3 percentage points** on the latest quarter (from **4.9%**). The ONS's provisional October payroll estimate fell by **34,000** month on month and **95,000** year on year. These measures come from different sources and periods. A central bank may see energy inflation accelerate while parts of the labour market soften. The eventual effect on services inflation depends on wage growth and how far businesses can pass higher costs through. One monthly observation does not determine an immediate policy-rate change. [16]

In Australia, minutes published on 17 November explained the vote at the early-month meeting. The Reserve Bank of Australia had raised its cash-rate target to **4.85%**, with **six votes for an increase** and **three votes to hold at 4.60%**. The majority considered the risk of allowing inflation to become more persistent greater than the risk of tightening financial conditions too much. The minutes document a decision already made and explain its voting distribution. They are not a forecast of another identical increase at the next meeting. Comparison with Europe and the UK shows that central banks may face similar price pressures with different rate levels, activity conditions and currency risks. [12]

## 8. Nvidia, Anthropic and the difference between revenue and investment commitments

Following the regular close on 17 November, Nvidia reported quarterly revenue of **$115.8 billion**, including **$107.6 billion** from Data Center. Consolidated GAAP gross margin was **74.8%**. The midpoint of management's next-quarter revenue outlook was **$130 billion**, with a **3%** range on either side. The results confirm realized sales and the supplier's expectation for the next quarter. They cannot yet establish how much of the capacity purchased by customers will generate paid services, when that will happen or how profitable it will be after energy, depreciation and financing costs. [14]

**Anthropic's** updated prospectus, filed with the SEC on 17 November, adds the infrastructure customer's perspective. For the nine months through September, it reported **$33.242 billion in revenue**, compared with **$2.545 billion** a year earlier. Compute and infrastructure expenses were **$18.808 billion**, compared with **$4.381 billion**. The simple ratio of these expenses to period revenue is approximately **56.6%**. It measures one expense category against revenue for the period. It does not equal operating margin because other expenses, income items and accounting rules determine the final result. [11]

The same filing describes approximately **$900.9 billion** in aggregate contractual obligations and commitments, mainly linked to compute and infrastructure, including purchases, leases and arrangements that had not yet commenced. About **$186.2 billion** of the aggregate is subject to exercisable termination rights or contingent conditions before commencement. The filing excludes an estimated **$150 billion to $175 billion** of payments under other compute and infrastructure arrangements that do not specify a fixed dollar commitment. It expects **$70.7 billion** of the aggregate to be paid in the twelve months following September 2026, of which **$4.2 billion** is subject to contingent conditions or termination rights. Approximately **$451.6 billion** is expected to be incurred before the end of 2030. The $900.9 billion figure is therefore neither a single-year cash outlay nor a complete ceiling on possible future spend, and it is not uniformly unconditional. Adding it to expenses already incurred or calling it all 2026 spending would overstate current investment. Reconciliation requires payment schedules, contract terms, available cash and financing sources. [11]

The prospectus proposes an initial offering of **825 million Class A shares**, with an indicative price range of **$70 to $78 per share**. The proposed transaction includes newly issued company shares and shares offered by existing shareholders. Cash available to Anthropic depends on shares sold by the company itself and transaction costs. The selling shareholders receive the proceeds from their own shares. An implied market capitalization also depends on the total shares outstanding after the offering and the rights of different classes. Gross offering value and the company's valuation therefore refer to different quantities. Their distinction matters when assessing capital available to support future compute commitments. [11]

A comparison shows where each business sits in the supply chain. Nvidia earns revenue as sales are recognized and has manufacturing and delivery obligations. Anthropic converts contracted compute into models and services while financing commitments over time. Risk can reach the supplier through delayed future orders. It may reach the buyer earlier through a mismatch between infrastructure payments and receipts from subscriptions or usage. Financing resilience depends on recurring revenue, commitment maturities, debt instruments and fresh equity capital. Fast revenue growth can lower the ratio of expenses to sales while the absolute stock of obligations still has to be funded. [11][14]

The FOMC minutes released at 16:00 Brasília time add a contemporaneous financial-stability assessment. The staff highlighted rapid growth in debt associated with the AI buildout and warned that off-balance-sheet leasing commitments and circular financing arrangements could amplify losses if returns on AI investment disappoint. Almost all participants discussed that scenario; most observed that AI-related debt had grown rapidly through complex and opaque structures. The minutes do not report that these losses have occurred. The staff judged the banking system well capitalized and household balance sheets a buffer, while identifying elevated asset valuations and hedge-fund leverage as salient vulnerabilities. This is a policy discussion and risk assessment, not evidence that Anthropic's obligations have already impaired lenders or that sector investment will fail. [20][21]

## 9. Brazil and transmission to emerging economies

Brazil's Senate rescheduled the vote on the four outstanding floor highlights in **PLP 299/2026** to 18 November after postponing the previous day's vote. The Executive estimated that the package could improve the 2027 primary balance by up to **R$56 billion**. This is a conditional estimate dependent on legislation and implementation. The effect on debt also depends on nominal interest costs, GDP growth and other revenue and spending. Deferring transfers between government levels can improve the federal cash position in one fiscal year without extinguishing the public sector's underlying obligation. [13]

Brazil's 17 November close showed the **Ibovespa at 169,816.64 points**, up **0.31%**, and the commercial dollar near **R$5.78**. These prices predate Wednesday's legislative decision. Assessing the market response to the final text requires trading windows after the vote. Rising equities and a rising dollar in the same session illustrate why one price cannot serve as a direct measure of fiscal confidence. Export sectors may receive more reais per dollar of revenue, while companies dependent on imported components may face higher replacement costs. Domestic interest rates and credit maturities determine how much of that difference reaches investment. [13][15]

Brazil also receives the technology shock through different channels. Energy and mineral exporters may earn more as worldwide infrastructure projects lift demand for inputs. Companies buying accelerators, servers and electrical equipment in dollars face global prices, exchange rates, taxes and financing costs. The value of an AI investment depends on actual productivity, equipment utilization and payback time. A global expansion in chip purchases can create supply opportunities without guaranteeing that every buyer earns a return. [11][14]

Three sets of observable data will make the 2027 outlook easier to interpret. The first covers delivered energy prices, freight and insurance, which shape imported inflation. The second covers services inflation, wages, real sales and unemployment, helping separate demand from costs. The third covers issuance, credit spreads, cash flow and capital commitments, measuring the capacity to finance the technology cycle. Sustained declines in delivered costs alongside stable spreads would weaken the prolonged-tightening hypothesis. If energy prices, fiscal expenses and infrastructure commitments keep growing faster than cash generation, financing constraints become more likely. [1][9][10][11]


## 10. United States: slower nominal sales and higher import prices

The Census Bureau estimated that retail and food services sales reached **$748.2 billion** in October, a nominal increase of **0.2%** from September. Revised September growth was **0.8%**. The series is adjusted for seasonal and calendar effects but measures sales in current prices. The 90% confidence interval for October's monthly change includes zero. The observed slowdown therefore does not establish that the volume of goods sold increased. Revenue can change with prices and product composition. Real consumption and disposable-income measures are needed to judge purchasing power more directly. [17]

| US October indicator | Reported change | Frequency and definition |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Retail and food services sales | +0.2% | Monthly nominal revenue, seasonally adjusted [17] |
| Total import prices | +1.1% | Monthly prices, unadjusted [18] |
| Import prices excluding fuel | +0.8% | Monthly prices, unadjusted [18] |
| Total industrial production | -0.4% | Monthly volume, seasonally adjusted [19] |
| Manufacturing output | +0.3% | Monthly volume, seasonally adjusted [19] |

The Bureau of Labor Statistics recorded a **1.1%** rise in import prices in October, matching September's increase. Excluding fuel, import prices rose **0.8%**. Imported capital-goods prices increased **1.5%**, with contributions from computers, peripherals, semiconductors and machinery. The import-price index is not a direct deflator for retail sales. Comparing the series shows that some input costs rose while nominal sales growth slowed. It does not quantify the amount passed on to consumers. Importers holding inventory purchased before a price increase may see a lag, while companies operating under fixed-price contracts may absorb a larger part of the change in margins. [17][18]

Industrial production fell **0.4%** over the month after a **0.7%** increase in September. Utilities output dropped **4.3%**, while manufacturing gained **0.3%**. Excluding motor vehicles and parts, manufacturing changed **-0.1%**. The divergence calls for care when interpreting the aggregate index. Broader gains in new orders and output beyond the motor-vehicle sector would weaken the industrial-slowdown hypothesis. If real sales remain weak and imported component costs stay high, pressure on margins and working capital is more likely to persist. [19]

## 11. Scenarios and disconfirmation

| Conditional path | Transmission | Evidence that would weaken it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Energy normalization | Lower delivered gas, diesel and freight reduce inflation pressure and ease policy constraints | Sustained disruption, renewed refining bottlenecks and rising food prices |
| Persistent supply shock | Inflation remains elevated while fiscal support and refinancing needs increase | Verified decline in delivered energy costs and stable lending spreads |
| Financial tightening without higher Treasury yields | Equity repricing and wider private spreads restrain investment | Stable issuance volumes, narrow spreads and resilient realized capital expenditure |

The completed October euro-area HICP and the FOMC minutes released at 16:00 Brasília time are incorporated. The next tests include dated US Treasury yields, private borrowing spreads, the outcome of Brazil's fiscal vote, customer utilization of AI capacity, financing terms for AI infrastructure and actual delivered energy costs. Market responses occurring after the cutoff require their own observation windows. [20][21]

The Commission forecast is primary institutional evidence for projections; it is not an independent audit of future outcomes. AP and Reuters provide market and conflict reporting; their prices and attributed claims are distinct from official statistics. Missing comparable cross-border credit spreads, fund flows and delivered-energy prices prevent a quantitative estimate of the global cost-of-capital transmission.
