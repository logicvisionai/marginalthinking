# Global Macro — 22 November 2026

## Productivity gains remain concentrated as markets reassess the AI growth premium

*Information cutoff: 05:00 Brasília time, 22 November 2026. Financial-market observations refer to the 20 November close; official statistics retain their own reference periods and release dates.*

The latest evidence points to a gap between strong pockets of measured productivity and the much broader investment narrative built around artificial intelligence (AI). U.S. nonfarm labour productivity rose at a 3.6% annualized rate in the third quarter, but the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) found that total factor productivity (TFP) increased in only seven of nineteen major industries during 2025. In China, high-tech manufacturing continued to expand rapidly while fixed investment and consumer-goods sales remained weak. The distinction is consequential: capital expenditure, output growth and economy-wide efficiency are related, but they are not interchangeable measures.

The sharp equity decline on 20 November therefore has a more specific interpretation than a generalized rejection of growth. Investors are repricing the timing, financing and distribution of expected AI-related returns while the evidence of productivity diffusion remains uneven. The available data do not establish that AI investment is unproductive, nor do they prove a broad credit event. They do show why the distance between announced capacity, operating assets and measurable efficiency gains matters for valuations, energy demand, industrial margins and public policy.

## 1. A different question from the previous editions

The three latest published Global Macro editions examined different stages of the same repricing cycle. The 17 November issue covered semiconductor valuations alongside Brazilian activity and inflation expectations. The 18 November issue assessed European energy inflation and the financing commitments attached to AI infrastructure. The 21 November issue concentrated on the cross-asset distribution of the 20 November sell-off, including Treasury yields, currencies, commodities and credit transmission.

The new information is not another account of falling share prices. It is the release of the BLS's 2025 industry-level TFP estimates on 20 November, read alongside the preliminary third-quarter productivity report published on 5 November and the National Bureau of Statistics of China's October activity data. These sources make it possible to test whether the investment and valuation cycle is already producing broad efficiency gains, or whether the measurable gains remain concentrated in a limited set of industries.

The distinction between labour productivity and TFP is essential. Labour productivity measures output per hour worked. It can rise because workers use more or better capital, because production is reorganized, or because technology improves. TFP compares output with a broader bundle of capital, labour, energy, materials and purchased services. Neither measure identifies AI as the cause of a change without additional evidence. The BLS industry release is annual and retrospective; the quarterly labour-productivity estimate is more recent but preliminary. They should not be merged into a single series.

## 2. Aggregate labour productivity is strong; total factor productivity is not broad

U.S. nonfarm business labour productivity increased 3.6% in the third quarter of 2026 at a seasonally adjusted annual rate. Output rose 3.7%, while hours worked increased 0.1%. Compared with the third quarter of 2025, productivity was 2.1% higher. Unit labour costs fell 0.7% at an annualized quarterly rate, but remained 2.4% above their level a year earlier. The quarterly improvement is meaningful for the near-term cost outlook, although the year-on-year figures show that compensation and productivity have not yet produced a sustained fall in labour costs. The estimate is preliminary and is scheduled for revision on 8 December. [2]

The annual industry data reveal a much less uniform pattern. TFP rose in seven of nineteen major industries in 2025. Wholesale trade recorded the largest increase among the listed industries, at 3.7%. Information, real estate, professional and technical services each recorded 1.9%; finance and insurance rose 1.3%; agriculture, forestry, fishing and hunting rose 0.7%; and health care and social assistance rose 0.4%. Manufacturing, utilities, construction, retail trade and several service industries recorded declines. [1]

The price data reinforce the unevenness. In twelve of the nineteen industries, output prices increased faster than combined input prices in 2025, a pattern the BLS associates with declining TFP. In other services, except government, output prices rose 4.1% while combined input prices rose 1.5%; measured TFP declined 2.6%. This does not prove that firms in those industries raised prices because of market power. It shows that nominal pricing and measured efficiency moved in different directions and that cost pass-through cannot be inferred from output prices alone. [1]

The quarterly labour-productivity result and the annual TFP distribution are therefore compatible. Capital deepening and changes in the mix of production can lift output per hour even when efficiency gains across all inputs remain narrow. The data are consistent with a productivity improvement in parts of the economy, not with a uniform transformation of the productive base.

The year-on-year comparison is less dramatic: productivity rose 2.1% and unit labour costs rose 2.4%. The quarterly annualized rate should not be presented as the growth rate for the full year, and the two measures use different denominators. [2]

## 3. The AI investment pipeline is global, but announcements are not operating capacity

Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook described the AI infrastructure buildout as a global investment cycle on 20 November. She cited company plans for more than US$2 trillion in specific U.S. data-centre construction projects, while stressing that only a fraction had been realized. She also noted that roughly half of the world's data-centre capacity under construction was estimated to be outside the United States. These are attributed estimates and announced project values, not a verified inventory of completed facilities or committed cash expenditure. [3]

The investment already affects adjacent markets. Cook identified prices for chips and high-tech equipment, construction wages and electricity costs as channels through which the buildout reaches the wider economy. Copper, lithium, transformers, cooling equipment, transmission capacity and power generation can all become constraints when data-centre demand grows faster than local infrastructure. The same investment can raise measured output in construction and equipment suppliers before the services using the installed computing capacity achieve productivity gains.

The sequence is therefore longer than a simple capital-expenditure story:

```flow
Announced data-centre projects → financing and equipment orders → construction and grid connection → operating computing capacity → adoption inside firms → changes in work processes → measured productivity and cash flow
```

Each step has a different evidentiary threshold. A project announcement does not establish financing close; financing does not establish construction completion; completion does not establish high utilization; and utilization does not establish a measurable productivity gain for customers. The BLS's information-industry TFP growth of 1.9% is consistent with some efficiency improvement, but the industry definition is too broad to attribute that result to generative AI. [1][3]

The European Central Bank's November Financial Stability Review adds a financial channel. AI-related firms accounted for between 16% and 26% of euro-area non-bank equity investments, depending on the investor group described in the review. Non-banks have also begun to hold debt issued by AI-related firms, including large companies financing capital expenditure, although those debt holdings remain limited in absolute terms. The ECB warns that high valuations, concentrated portfolios, leverage and liquidity mismatches could amplify a change in sentiment. It does not conclude that banks are already facing material direct losses from AI assets. [4]

A correction in AI-related shares can therefore reduce the market value of concentrated portfolios without immediately stopping construction or cancelling projects. The transmission becomes more serious if lower valuations raise the cost of equity, debt spreads widen, lenders tighten underwriting or project sponsors delay grid connections and equipment orders. Evidence of those changes would be more informative than a single daily index decline.

## 4. China shows strong production alongside weak domestic investment

China's October data present a second version of the same distinction between output and diffusion. Industrial value added rose 4.8% year on year in real terms. Equipment manufacturing increased 10.6%, while high-tech manufacturing rose 17.6%. Production of lithium-ion batteries and industrial robots increased 30.0% and 26.0%, respectively. These are material gains in selected production segments, but they do not establish an equivalent rise in domestic final demand. [5]

Fixed-asset investment excluding rural households fell 8.1% year on year in the first ten months. Manufacturing investment declined 2.9%, infrastructure investment 5.1%, and private investment 11.4%. Retail sales of consumer goods increased only 0.4% in October, while the manufacturing purchasing managers' index stood at 49.3. The service-retail measure grew more quickly than goods retail over the first ten months, but it is a separate series with a different coverage. [5]

The composition matters more than a single growth headline. High-tech output can expand through exports, substitution, inventories or capacity utilization while private investment and household goods consumption remain weak. The National Bureau of Statistics itself noted that the imbalance between strong supply and weak demand remained acute. Its price data show industrial producer prices up 5.7% year on year in October, while purchasing prices for industrial producers rose 7.9%. Fuel and power purchasing prices increased 18.6%. These figures point to cost pressure in upstream inputs alongside weak consumer-goods demand; they do not establish a generalized acceleration in household inflation. [5]

For global markets, this divergence affects industrial commodities, equipment suppliers and trade partners differently. It can support export-oriented manufacturing while limiting the pass-through from output growth to household income and domestic demand. The implications for trade balances and regional supply chains depend on volumes, prices and the composition of imports, not on industrial production alone.

## 5. Monetary and energy conditions remain uneven across regions

Japan's national consumer price index rose 2.3% year on year in October. The index excluding fresh food increased 2.2%, and the measure excluding fresh food and energy also rose 2.2%. The figures were published on 20 November. They show that inflation remains above the Bank of Japan's 2% objective, but they do not by themselves establish the timing of the next policy adjustment. [6]

Euro-area inflation was estimated at 4.0% in October, with the energy component at 20.8% year on year. The European Commission's 20 November flash consumer-confidence indicator improved by 1.0 percentage point to -16.9, but remained well below its long-term average. The combination is not contradictory: a small improvement in confidence can coexist with a high price level and weak household sentiment. The inflation figure is a flash estimate, not the full November price release. [7][8]

Brazil's Copom maintained the Selic rate at 13.75% at its November meeting. The committee said activity had moderated more clearly in cyclical sectors, while headline and underlying inflation had accelerated and inflation expectations had moved further from target. The October IPCA rose 0.14% in the month, according to IBGE, after a 0.82% increase in September. The monthly slowdown does not erase the central bank's concern about expectations or the accumulated inflation process. [12][13]

The oil market is also split between crude supply and refined-product availability. The International Energy Agency estimated that global oil supply rose to 105.4 million barrels per day in October and that observed inventories increased by 52 million barrels. Its November report forecasts a 2.7 million barrel-per-day decline in average global demand in 2026, to 102.3 million barrels per day. Yet the IEA continued to identify tight diesel supply, with prices near US$180 per barrel and freight costs elevated. The supply and demand figures are estimates and forecasts under the report's stated assumptions; they do not remove the risk of localized shortages or renewed disruption to shipping and refining. [14]

## 6. Markets, yields, credit and capital flows

Friday's market session was sharply negative for equities. The MSCI global-share gauge fell 3.30%; the S&P 500 declined 3.70%, the Nasdaq 5.28% and the Dow 1.93%. The STOXX 600 fell 2.0%, emerging-market shares lost 3.10%, the Asia-Pacific index excluding Japan declined 3.27%, and the Nikkei fell 5.06%. Taiwan's market recorded a much larger weekly decline. The Treasury response was different: the U.S. ten-year yield fell 10.43 basis points to 4.948%, while the two-year yield declined 13.21 basis points to 4.647%. [9][10]

The dollar index was almost unchanged at 103.90. The euro traded near US$1.0961 and the dollar near ¥158.6. Gold fell 2.81% to US$3,520.09 per ounce, copper declined 2.9% to US$12,162.50 per tonne, while Brent rose 0.68% to US$92.61 per barrel and WTI gained 1.49% to US$83.67. The pattern does not resemble a uniform flight into every conventional defensive asset. It reflects different demand, positioning and geopolitical channels; the price changes alone do not identify a single cause. [9]

The latest U.S. Treasury International Capital release reported a net inflow of US$261.0 billion in September, including US$267.0 billion in private inflows and US$6.0 billion in official outflows. Foreign residents made net purchases of US$186.7 billion in long-term U.S. securities. These are lagged portfolio and banking observations, not evidence of what investors did after the 20 November sell-off. Treasury also warns that custodial reporting limits precise country attribution and that TIC does not include direct investment flows. [11]

Credit conditions require similar care. The ECB reported that euro-area corporate financing costs had risen, credit volumes remained resilient but had lost momentum, and banks had tightened lending standards further. It also identified concentrated AI exposures and vulnerabilities in non-bank funds. These are documented channels of sensitivity, not proof of a generalized credit freeze. No same-day global high-yield spread or cross-border fund-flow series was verified for the Sunday cutoff. The next decisive evidence would be issuance pricing, credit spreads, bank lending surveys, redemptions and confirmed changes in project financing. [4]

No new major fiscal package or central-bank rate decision was verified for the cutoff. The Federal Reserve's target range remained 3.75%–4.00% after its October decision; Governor Cook's 20 November remarks are an individual policy-maker's assessment, not a new committee decision. Brazil's Selic remained 13.75%. Sovereign yields and debt-service costs continue to constrain fiscal choices, but a market repricing should not be described as a new fiscal shock without a corresponding budget or issuance measure. [12][15]

## 7. Regional transmission and what to monitor

```map
title: Regional transmission of productivity, capital and cost pressures
United States | Strong quarterly labour productivity; TFP gains concentrated by industry | AI project announcements remain well ahead of completed capacity
China | High-tech and equipment output expand | Fixed investment and consumer-goods sales remain weak
Europe | Energy inflation remains high; confidence improves from a depressed level | Corporate financing costs and non-bank concentration warrant monitoring
Japan | October inflation remains above the 2% objective | Energy imports and exchange rates affect household purchasing power
Brazil | Restrictive Selic and cautious inflation expectations | Monthly IPCA moderation does not remove expectation risk
```

The United States combines a strong quarterly labour-productivity estimate with a narrow annual TFP distribution and concentrated financial exposure to AI-related assets. China combines high-tech manufacturing growth with weak fixed investment and goods consumption. Europe faces energy inflation and tighter corporate financing conditions. Japan remains sensitive to imported energy and exchange-rate pass-through. Brazil retains a restrictive policy rate despite a lower monthly IPCA reading. These are distinct regional mechanisms, not a synchronized global cycle.

Three paths remain plausible. In a diffusion scenario, AI capacity moves from construction into high utilization and broad adoption; productivity gains spread beyond ICT, real wages and output improve, and investment is increasingly supported by operating cash flow. In a concentration scenario, infrastructure and equipment suppliers benefit while most industries record limited efficiency gains; valuations remain sensitive to financing costs and power constraints. In an adverse scenario, lower expected returns lead to project delays, credit tightening and weaker demand for chips, metals, construction services and electricity equipment. None of these paths is established by the current data.

The diffusion thesis would strengthen if subsequent BLS releases showed broader TFP gains, if China’s private investment and household demand recovered, and if operating data showed that data-centre capacity was being used productively. It would weaken if productivity gains remained concentrated, if unit labour costs accelerated, if project announcements were repeatedly deferred, or if credit spreads and financing terms deteriorated materially. The market-stress thesis would become more systemic only with evidence of forced deleveraging, sustained redemptions, funding-market dysfunction or a measurable contraction in credit availability.

## 8. Balance of evidence

The data support a narrower conclusion than either an AI productivity boom or an AI investment bust. U.S. labour productivity was strong in the third quarter, but annual TFP growth remained concentrated in seven of nineteen industries. China’s high-tech output expanded while investment and consumer-goods sales were weak. The AI infrastructure pipeline is global, but announced projects substantially exceed completed capacity. Market prices have adjusted sharply, yet the latest Treasury flow data predate the sell-off and the ECB's evidence describes vulnerabilities rather than a generalized credit breakdown.

The principal macroeconomic risk is a mismatch between the speed of capital deployment and the speed at which productivity, cash flow and household income improve. That mismatch can sustain investment for a time while increasing sensitivity to financing costs, electricity constraints and changes in expected returns. A broad productivity dividend remains possible; it is not yet demonstrated by the available industry data.
