# Before and after agriculture: wealth, inheritance and the institutions of prehistoric inequality

A burial furnished with thousands of ornaments can belong to a society without a permanent ruling class. A village of farming households can have unequal harvests without giving the fortunate household a hereditary claim over its neighbors. Conversely, households with superficially similar houses can differ sharply in access to livestock, land, labor, marriage alliances and authority. The origin of inequality is therefore not a date at which human beings first acquired different possessions. It is a history of how unequal advantages became reproducible, politically protected and transmissible across generations. [1][2][3]

The transition to cultivation and animal management changed this problem, but did not solve it in a single direction. Grain storage and field improvement increased the potential value of durable assets. Herds could reproduce and be transferred. Permanent dwellings gathered ancestral memory around places. Yet these developments could coexist with redistribution, shared rights, restrictions on prestige, collective ritual and other checks on accumulation. Bowles and Fochesato's 2024 reassessment finds substantial and enduring inequality far less widespread in early western Eurasian farming societies than a simple agricultural-revolution narrative implies. The deeper transition was from occasional differences to institutions that allowed differences to persist. [1]

The archaeological record also resists the opposite mythology. Some foraging groups marked children or adults with exceptionally elaborate funerary treatment long before agriculture. Some early agricultural communities differentiated access to special goods. Later households in Bronze Age central Europe demonstrably contained multigenerational high-status kin and lower-status unrelated people. No single contrast between mobile equality and settled hierarchy can explain these differences. [4][5][6]

## 1. What was unequal: goods, power, security or life chances?

Wealth is not identical to money. In societies without coins or written asset registries, wealth may include portable ornaments, tools, houses, cleared land, stored food, domesticated animals, knowledge, useful social relationships and access to productive places. These resources do not operate identically. A person's knowledge of hunting grounds may be difficult to confiscate, whereas a herd can be seized, inherited, multiplied and guarded. A ceremonial reputation can command cooperation without granting exclusive rights over a field. [2][3]

Economic inequality concerns differences in control over resources, consumption and claims on other people's labor. Political inequality concerns unequal authority to decide, enforce, punish or adjudicate. Social inequality extends to standing, mobility, marriage choices, ritual recognition and protection from violence. These dimensions interact but need not move together. A household may own more cattle yet remain bound by communal pasture rules; a renowned ritual specialist may have high status but no enforceable claim to taxation; a powerful lineage may redistribute food to sustain authority rather than retain the largest house.

The most demanding question is persistence. A lucky hunting season, a rich harvest or a highly decorated burial records difference at a moment. Durable inequality requires mechanisms through which an advantage survives accidents, deaths, quarrels and generational succession. Inheritance rules, exclusive access to assets, rights to compel labor, coercive capacity and institutions recognizing claims can turn a temporary lead into a predictable social position. The strength of each mechanism has to be demonstrated, not assumed from a conspicuous artifact. [1][2]

## 2. An uneven archive and the problem of measuring the past

Archaeologists rarely observe income, wages, land titles or a complete population. A preserved house floor records construction and abandonment, not a household balance sheet. Graves document a selection of the dead and the decisions of surviving mourners, not the whole living community. Inheritance may leave genetic and spatial patterns, but written legal rules are absent for most prehistoric cases. The evidentiary path runs from measurable remains through a model of what they represent to a hypothesis about social institutions.

Housing is one widely used proxy because floor areas sometimes preserve comparable household-scale investments. Kohler and colleagues calculated Gini coefficients from house-size distributions across 63 sites or groups of approximately contemporary sites. Their medians differ across subsistence regimes. A house-area Gini, however, is neither an income Gini nor an exact measure of landownership; household size, building function, preservation, shared workspaces and chronological overlap all complicate the comparison. The sample also underrepresents or excludes important parts of Africa, South Asia, South America and Oceania. [3]

| Evidence | What can be observed | Potential inequality mechanism | Principal inference hazard |
|---|---|---|---|
| House floor area | Differences in the scale of surviving buildings | Labor investment, domestic productive capacity, display | Building size is not verified monetary wealth |
| Burials and grave goods | Unequal funerary treatment among recovered dead | Prestige, descent, ritual differentiation | The buried sample and mourners' choices are selective |
| Herds, fields and storage | Animal remains, enclosure traces, crop and granary contexts | Durable capital, exclusion, seasonal buffering | Storage alone does not establish private property or tax |
| Ancient DNA and isotopes | Biological kinship, movement and dietary contrasts | Kin-centered succession, restricted marriage, differentiated access | Kinship is not legal inheritance or political office |
| Injuries, fortifications and weapons | Trauma, defenses and material coercive capacity | Ability to protect resources or impose transfers | Violence does not automatically mean a state |
| Repeated construction and monuments | Sustained labor mobilization and ritual investment | Collective coordination or concentrated command | Scale alone does not distinguish voluntary work from tribute |

A quantitative series requires an equally explicit warning about comparability. The Gini coefficient ranges from zero for equal observations to one in its extreme theoretical distribution; archaeological estimates apply to a selected proxy. It would be invalid to splice a prehistory house-size Gini directly onto modern national income data or to treat a funerary volume Gini as the same variable. The research question may be similar while the measurement object differs. [2][3][7]

## 3. The comparative pattern: agriculture changes the opportunity, not the verdict

Across the archaeological contexts sampled by Kohler and colleagues, the median house-size Gini is reported as approximately 0.17 for foragers, 0.27 for horticulturalists and 0.35 for agriculturalists. These are medians of site-level proxies, not world averages of household wealth, and the categories aggregate distinct places and periods. They support an association between subsistence institutions and uneven residential investment; they do not prove that planting crops mechanically caused a linear increase in inequality in every region. [3]

The 2017 study also found greater post-Neolithic disparities in its sampled Eurasian societies than in the sampled societies of North America and Mesoamerica. Its interpretation gives importance to the availability of large domestic animals, more extensive cultivation and eventually mounted military elites. Such a mechanism is plausible because animals provide traction, manure, transport and a transferable asset; mounted force can also increase the geographical scale of appropriation. But multiple institutional configurations coexist with those technologies, and the study's geographical gaps and heterogeneous chronology limit claims of a universal civilizational ladder. [3]

A separate measurement check published in the same 2017 paper draws on ethnographic wealth estimates for living or historically recorded societies. Across five foraging groups the reported mean of group means is 0.25, across four horticultural groups 0.28 and across eight agricultural groups 0.44. Unlike the preceding chart, these are **not house-size Ginis or archaeological medians**: they are averages from heterogeneous wealth proxies in independent ethnographic material. Their principal value is testing the approximate direction and scale of the house-area estimates; the different populations, weighting and asset definitions forbid subtracting the bars of one chart from those of the other as if they were matched observations. [3]

A complementary test comes from the comparative anthropology of 21 historical and contemporary small-scale populations. Borgerhoff Mulder and colleagues found stronger transmission of important forms of wealth and more pronounced inequality among pastoral and small-scale farming groups than among the foraging and horticultural groups studied. Material wealth, bodily skills and relational wealth have different rates of inheritance. This is not direct observation of the Neolithic: the contemporary comparisons identify mechanisms that archaeological research must independently test. [2]

```flow
Storable output and durable productive assets → larger potential differences between households → exclusion or shared-use rules → intergenerational transmission or redistribution → persistence or reversal of unequal advantage
Coordinated ritual and collective obligation → sanctions on accumulation or prestige → negotiated distribution of resources → possible containment of hierarchy
Control of land and animals + enforceable labor claims → accumulated surplus and patronage → capacity to defend exclusive claims → potentially self-reinforcing political inequality
```

## 4. Before fields: distinction among mobile and semimobile populations

Upper Paleolithic societies were not economically identical. Seasonal movement, ecological productivity, storage possibilities, hunting equipment and exchange distances varied. In a highly mobile group, bulky stores and fixed structures can be costly to retain; cooperation and social sharing may reduce the incentives or opportunities to hoard. But tools, adornments, knowledge, relationships and strategic access to places can still be unequal. Mobility limits some forms of asset accumulation rather than eliminating status differences. [1][2]

Sunghir, in present-day Russia, demonstrates the distinction between differential treatment and an attested class system. Elaborate Upper Paleolithic graves include extraordinary ornamentation and unusual ivory objects, including richly furnished child burials. Research into the diversity of treatment among the dead shows that funeral investments were not uniform. Because the deceased children could not have acquired adult prestige through long careers, some researchers consider ascribed or kin-associated standing possible. Yet the grave assemblages alone cannot establish a persistent tax-collecting nobility, a whole-population wealth distribution or the rules governing living households. [6]

Seasonally abundant fish, game or plant resources could support more durable settlements, accumulated stores and competition over places even without domesticated plants. Conversely, highly productive environments might also sustain redistributive institutions. Neither “forager” nor “farmer” is a substitute for analysis of ecological constraints and rights of access. Broad ethnographic averages must not obscure differences in political and economic choices among communities.

## 5. Agriculture, sedentism and a new capital structure

Domestication was neither instantaneous nor geographically uniform. Communities adopted cultivation, animal keeping, storage and settlement in different combinations. Farming requires labor invested before harvest; grain can survive between seasons, but it can rot, be stolen or be exhausted. Cleared fields, irrigation works, terraces, fences, breeding animals, and built granaries create assets whose usefulness can last beyond one production cycle. These assets may increase productivity and security while also making exclusion more valuable. [1][3]

Control of land is not equivalent to ownership under a modern title system. Households can have seasonal access, kinship-based use rights, collective pasture rules or claims mediated by councils and ritual authorities. An improved plot can be inherited without becoming freely saleable. Some obligations may take the form of reciprocal labor rather than rent. Whether a harvest surplus becomes durable inequality depends on who can refuse another's access, who decides after the holder dies and who can enforce the decision.

Food storage modifies the distribution of risk. Households facing independent crop failures can insure one another through exchange. Common reserves can dampen famine disparities. But concentrated storage can make dependents reliant on gatekeepers, especially when repayment obligations accumulate over several seasons. The same technological capacity therefore creates both redistributive and extractive possibilities; material evidence of stored food by itself cannot discriminate between them.

Livestock changes the mechanism again. Reproducing animals, traction and manure may increase the returns to previously accumulated holdings, enabling better-resourced farms to cultivate larger areas. This can magnify differences even without an intentional conspiracy to create elites. Yet disease, drought, raiding, bridewealth, common grazing and redistribution can destroy or transfer a herd. Institutions determine how often accumulation becomes self-reinforcing. [2][3]

## 6. Inheritance turns a difference into a structure

An analytical distinction separates a household that becomes wealthy during one lifetime from a lineage whose descendants predictably inherit productive advantage. For persistence, families must be able to transfer or control assets and retain those claims despite opposition. Material property, specialist knowledge, productive relationships and recognized social standing are inherited differently. Some skills demand years of training; other assets can pass by succession; relations to allies can be renewed or lost. [2]

A community may recognize inheritance of houses but not of surrounding land. It may require redistribution at marriage, death or communal feasts. It may also distinguish descendants' rights by age, gender, adoption or affiliation. Without written records, such mechanisms must be reconstructed cautiously through repeated spatial patterns, grave sequences, genetic kinship, dietary access and long-term differences in domestic investment.

The strongest evidence of structural reproduction combines independent lines. Consistently privileged houses across generations are more informative than one opulent burial; biological lineages associated with durable social differences become more persuasive when isotope and archaeological contexts agree. Yet there is no one-to-one conversion from DNA relationship to legal descent, and an outsider's apparently low-status grave does not alone show slavery.

## 7. Çatalhöyük: complex households without a proven enduring elite

The Anatolian settlement of Çatalhöyük illustrates why agricultural intensification cannot be equated with an inevitable state. Its dense mudbrick houses, storage, ritual installations and burials beneath floors preserve a long record of household activities and identities. The settlement required ongoing coordination, but absence of a documented palace or royal bureaucracy does not prove absence of all political inequality. Nor does the ritual prominence of a house automatically establish rent extraction. [5]

Twiss and colleagues' 2024 multi-proxy investigation compares contemporaneous buildings, abandonment contexts and changes over time. It finds broadly comparable access to staple foods alongside differentiated access to some non-essential goods, periods of greater disparity and no consistent trajectory toward permanent institutionalized economic and social inequality in the measures considered. Differences existed. The disputed point is whether they compounded, persisted and became enforceable class rights. [5]

A powerful group might monopolize a special craft or secure influence over marriage and burial without controlling everyone's subsistence. Other households could exercise countervailing influence, with ritual obligations limiting individual accumulation. These are hypotheses consistent with the observed complexity, not recovered constitutional rules. Çatalhöyük also warns against treating a single dataset, such as house size, as sufficient to measure political authority.

## 8. Labor, gender and the distribution of costs

Permanent cultivation creates repeated demands for sowing, weeding, guarding, harvesting, grinding, carrying water, maintaining houses and managing animals. Large stores and monuments embody accumulated labor whose division may be unequal. Archaeological comparisons need to ask not only who possessed prestigious goods, but whose bodies performed the work and whose diets bore the cost. Skeletal stress, injury, disease and isotope differences can inform that question when the sampled groups and causal alternatives are made explicit.

Gendered labor roles cannot be deduced from present-day stereotypes. Evidence of patrilocal or matrilocal residence in one population does not establish that all women lacked autonomy or that women governed. Marriage rules can distribute people and productive connections across communities; burial selection can conceal children, migrants and dependents. The ability to mobilize other people's labor is a central channel linking wealth to power, but its archaeological signal varies sharply among societies. [2][4]

Social security matters as much as nominal possessions. Collective food obligations might protect a household with few stores. A labor-dependent household could have strong subsistence rights without political independence. The form of domination may involve marriage restrictions, debt, service obligations, control of grazing or protection, even before standardized taxation or money.

## 9. From episodic prestige to enforceable rank

A conspicuous object can be an episodic prize, a gift, a ritual obligation or a marker of lasting privilege. These explanations have different consequences. Prestige freely granted for competence may fade; an inherited right to control fields or command dependents can outlast the original holder. Political hierarchy becomes durable when social claims are monitored, validated and defended over time.

Monumental construction is an especially ambiguous signal. Large projects demand coordination, provisioning and specialist knowledge. They can be organized through seasonal assembly, councils, ritual associations, competitive households, coercive command or combinations of these. Without administrative records, restricted access patterns or durable concentration of productive assets, the leap from monument to monarch is not justified.

A related caution applies to conflict. Weapons, skeletal trauma and defensive architecture can indicate violence or threats. They do not independently identify who authorized a raid, who controlled captured resources or whether a standing army existed. Yet coercive capability can matter causally: an elite able to protect exclusive claims while imposing obligations on others may convert otherwise unstable advantage into more persistent inequality. [1][3]

## 10. Evidence for long duration: Bronze Age households

The Lech River valley in southern Germany offers a more direct test of inherited status. Mittnik and colleagues combined genome-wide data from 104 individuals with isotopic and archaeological information from farmstead-associated cemeteries spanning the Late Neolithic to Middle Bronze Age. Their study identified households in which higher-status related family members and lower-status unrelated individuals coexisted over multiple generations, with patterns consistent with patrilocality and female exogamy. The configuration persisted across a sequence described as approximately 700 years. [4]

This matters because biological relatedness, mobility indicators and mortuary status are partly independent observations. In combination, they support the interpretation of an enduring social distinction associated with family organization. The evidence still does not authorize calling all unrelated individuals slaves, assigning precise wages or reconstructing a regional legal code from one microregional sample. Status and dependency require historically bounded vocabulary.

A single region cannot establish a continent-wide date when hierarchy “began.” The Lech valley shows one durable institutional configuration, while settlements and funerary systems elsewhere may have limited such arrangements or organized rank in different ways. Its comparative value lies in demonstrating an empirical bridge between household descent, differentiation and long-run reproduction, rather than claiming that every farm inevitably generated an aristocracy.

## 11. Ritual, mortuary investment and unequal visibility

Graves are a particularly seductive source because spectacular objects survive and attract excavation. Yet elaborate disposal requires decisions by the living. A costly burial may represent obligations to the deceased, family reputation, ritual danger, mourning conventions or the status of the mourners themselves. The quantity and quality of recovered goods are shaped by soil, reuse, looting, excavation strategy and the unequal likelihood of formal burial. [6]

Research on approximately 5,000 individual burial mounds in prehistoric central Europe, reported by Marzian and colleagues in 2024, uses mound volume to explore relational wealth within an already selected upper social segment. The study finds a changing, wave-like pattern of inequality within that sample. The limitation is central: the mounds do not represent the entire living society, and their volume is not equivalent to an individual's lifetime income. They document costly memorial treatment and networks of recognition among people selected for such burial. [7]

## 12. Disagreement is about institutions, not whether differences existed

One family of explanations emphasizes productive assets and the returns to cultivation. Investments in fields, traction animals and herds make it easier to accumulate transferable advantage. Political organizations then amplify unequal ownership through labor and coercion. Kohler's comparative evidence supports some elements of this path, though it cannot on its own distinguish the causal importance of every institutional step. [3]

Bowles and Fochesato instead place special weight on the long gap between early agriculture and widespread enduring inequality in their western Eurasian review. Their account allows substantial temporary disparities while arguing that egalitarian sanctions and community organization could frustrate their reproduction. Later technological changes and proto-state power increased the possibility of lasting concentration. Agriculture is thus a change in the feasible set of institutions, not the instant creation of a permanent class system. [1]

John Robb's 2025 assessment of prehistoric Europe similarly challenges a story assembled from a selective gallery of exceptional elite graves. His comparative account emphasizes heterogeneous political arrangements and collective practices that could contain hierarchy. It is an argument about evidence selection and historical agency, not proof that every prehistoric European community was uniformly egalitarian. [8]

These positions are not mutually exclusive in every case. Asset accumulation can create pressure toward hierarchy, while communities may regulate that pressure successfully for long periods. A strong research design therefore compares sites with similar productive technologies but different social outcomes, and follows the same locality across several generations instead of explaining social history through one exceptional monument.

## 13. Where geography changes the mechanism

The broad label “Neolithic” conceals diverse ecologies and calendars. Farming on floodplains, dryland fields, terraces and forest clearings requires different combinations of water control, storage, cooperation and animal power. Long-distance exchange may privilege those who maintain routes, but those routes can also distribute access and risk. Domesticable large mammals were unevenly available across regions, an important part of the explanation proposed for Eurasian and American differences in the Kohler sample. [3]

Comparison must avoid treating North America and Mesoamerica as a single political economy, or using Eurasian evidence to infer the Andes, Africa and Oceania. Some regions have sparse comparable house plans; in others communal buildings complicate residential measures. Local evidence of terracing, herding, exchange and shared infrastructure must come before claims about continental inequality.

## 14. What a real causal test would require

A convincing account of inequality cannot simply align an agricultural date with a later aristocratic grave. It needs sequences showing that assets accumulated, ownership or access differentiated, transfers survived succession and means of enforcement maintained the difference. The relevant evidence will rarely arrive in one excavation. It must connect subsistence, architecture, funerary treatment, mobility, environmental changes and sometimes ancient genomes.

Several rival mechanisms can produce the same superficial signal. Larger houses may be built by larger cooperative groups, not wealthier families. Valuable grave goods can signal intense ritual obligations rather than command over labor. More cattle bones may reflect seasonal feasting rather than private herd holdings. Defensive walls can protect common stores rather than an elite treasury. A credible interpretation specifies which additional discovery would distinguish these hypotheses.

A finding of repeated wealth differentials without genealogical continuity would weaken the inheritance hypothesis. Conversely, persistent differences associated with lineages, control of production and unequal access to labor would strengthen it. Evidence of recurrent collective redistribution or routine destruction of prestige assets would support the role of institutional limits on accumulation. Archaeology becomes more explanatory when such possibilities are set before the conclusion, not added as decorative uncertainty afterward.

## 15. The economic and political result

Agriculture expanded the range of assets that could be stored, defended, managed and passed to descendants. It also increased dependence on seasonal labor, land management and coordinated risk reduction. These changes made durable inequality more feasible, but did not dictate whether communities would create enforceable hereditary advantage. Some prehistoric groups differentiated prestige while preserving broad access to staple subsistence. Others eventually linked households, inheritance, coercion and productive resources into durable social ranks. [1][2][4][5]

The evidence favors a history of contingent institutional formation over either an original paradise destroyed by farming or a universal agricultural march toward kingship. Inequality could precede domestication, grow after it, recede under collective action or become entrenched when material wealth and political power protected each other. The central historical threshold was not the first surplus. It was the stabilization of claims that let one household's advantage survive the household and bind the next generation.

In later urban systems such as Uruk, administrative records, specialized institutions and large-scale coordination provide different kinds of evidence for how obligations were registered and resources moved. Their analysis must begin from those documentary and archaeological mechanisms, rather than assuming that the appearance of agriculture centuries earlier already determined the political outcome.
