# Where Wealth Is Actually Safe: States, Law, Currencies and the Global Geography of Property

## States, laws, currencies and the global geography of property

**11 November 2026 | Economics · Politics · Society**

A building may survive centuries and lose its legal protection in a single political decision. A government bond may be repaid in full while its proceeds lose half their purchasing power. A solvent bank can be required to freeze an account because a foreign authority has imposed applicable sanctions. A farm may remain physically intact while losing a water entitlement, an environmental license, an export market or the legal ability to transfer ownership. Economic security has never resided entirely within the object being owned.

Wealth rests on enforceable rights, institutions and material capacity. An apartment depends on a title register, possession, structural integrity, demand and courts. A bank account depends on bank balance sheets, deposit insurance, currency and permitted transfers. A public bond depends on the solvency and legal obligations of a sovereign. A farm depends on the history of its ownership, water, soil, climate, roads and rights of other parties. Equity shares depend on corporate governance, the future earnings of the enterprise, investor protection and securities custody. These are different claims embedded in different legal systems.

A Brazilian resident might own Japanese equities through an Irish fund serviced by a United States broker, settled through a European central securities infrastructure, with Brazilian tax obligations. The underlying income could originate in Asia while custody and enforcement are governed elsewhere. A household owning real estate in Uruguay and farmland in Paraguay may remain exposed to the same global commodity-price shock. Geographical separation is not necessarily economic diversification.

The World Justice Project reported in October 2026 that the rule of law declined in **62% of 143 countries and jurisdictions**, with deteriorations outnumbering improvements for the ninth consecutive year. Denmark, Norway, Finland, Sweden and Luxembourg led the ranking, while Venezuela, Afghanistan, Haiti, Cambodia and Nicaragua occupied the lowest positions. This is an eight-component institutional measure, not a probability of investment loss. In its October 2026 financial stability assessment, the IMF found a system that had remained resilient yet was exposed to sovereign-yield shocks, technology-sector concentration, leverage, nonbank intermediaries and cyber incidents. Institutional security and the absence of financial volatility are different properties. [1][2]

## I. Property is not a thing: it is a bundle of enforceable rights

### 1. The rule of law and the ability to enforce contracts

A secure property claim requires rules that can be understood in advance, documentary evidence that can be checked, courts capable of reaching a decision and effective enforcement. Written law is inadequate when an order can be ignored, a registry altered without remedy, or the cost of litigation exceeds the asset's value. Predictability of the law matters alongside the ability to challenge its application. A state can be highly efficient at implementing adverse decisions without offering robust independent checks on the authority that made them.

Civil justice, contract enforcement, regulatory supervision, constitutional rights and physical safety do not necessarily move together. An investment may be protected against arbitrary confiscation and lose value in an ordinary recession. A property owner can win in court yet lack the cash to sustain litigation. A lender can possess valid collateral and be unable to recover its value quickly. Legal certainty concerns the process by which rights are recognized, altered and enforced; economic certainty concerns whether those rights retain useful value.

### 2. Seven risks that must not be collapsed into one score

**Credit risk** concerns the ability of a debtor to pay. **Market risk** concerns changes in tradable prices. **Monetary risk** concerns inflation and exchange rates. **Institutional risk** concerns changes or arbitrary application of rules. **Transfer risk** concerns whether money and claims can move lawfully across borders. **Operational risk** concerns fraud, failed records, technology and intermediaries. **Physical and territorial risk** concerns war, disasters, occupation, climate and the actual condition of buildings and land. The categories interact but are not interchangeable.

A relatively low interest rate on a highly liquid government bill does not demonstrate that farmland in the same jurisdiction is environmentally secure. Expensive residential property can be legally protected but purchased at an unsustainable price. A deposit within the statutory insurance limit may be protected from a specified bank failure but not from the future decline in purchasing power of the denomination.

| Question | Institution or fact to inspect | Possible failure |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who owes the money? | Bank, sovereign, corporation, fund or no debtor | Default, restructuring, insolvency |
| Who recognizes ownership? | Court, land registry, securities system, depositary | Defective title, fraud, disputed beneficiary |
| Where can legal coercion apply? | Asset territory, payment network, custodian and sanctions jurisdiction | Freeze, lawful seizure or transfer restriction |
| Which currency applies? | Debt denomination, expenses and accounting currency | Inflation, foreign exchange and mismatches |
| What right of use exists? | Zoning, water, mineral rights, licenses and leases | Loss of practical use despite nominal title |
| How can it be sold? | Market depth, buyer eligibility, approvals and costs | Illiquidity and forced discount |
| What happens at succession? | Marital regime, estate rules, tax and control agreements | Contest, delay and family conflict |

The order of the questions depends on the type of property and the owner's residence, nationality, objectives and liabilities. No universal index can substitute for asset-specific diligence.

### 3. The most important institution is not always the central bank

Central banks are critical for inflation, settlement and financial liquidity, but do not determine ownership of a farm, authenticate a land title, settle inheritance disputes or enforce building codes. A farm's economic security may depend more directly on local courts, land administration, access to water and transport, and whether a license can be renewed. Housing may depend on municipal zoning, mortgage law, insurance and the physical resilience of an entire neighbourhood.

The institutional chain runs from rights and records through procedures, contestation and enforcement to the ability to use or transfer the asset. Break one part of the chain and formal ownership may cease to provide the expected economic protection.

## II. The long history of security and expropriation

### 4. Rome, ancient land and the rights of people without power

Property protection predates modern capitalism. Land, grazing areas, irrigation, livestock, food stores and tribute rights were allocated under custom, kinship, religion and the force of political authorities. Communities without modern cadastral titles could still possess enforceable systems of rights. The arrival of a new written register did not prove that earlier claims lacked legitimacy.

Roman jurisprudence differentiated possession, ownership, use and inheritance. Such sophistication did not prevent political confiscation, unequal access to courts, concentration of landed wealth or slavery. What law established as protectable could depend profoundly on social status. The ancient lesson is not that law is irrelevant but that the enforceability of law and the distribution of its benefits must be analyzed separately.

### 5. Magna Carta, England and constraints upon sovereignty

The Magna Carta of 1215 emerged from a conflict between King John and powerful subjects; it was not a universal modern bill of rights. Subsequent English political struggles, including the Petition of Right in 1628, the Habeas Corpus Act in 1679 and the Bill of Rights in 1689, gradually challenged the Crown's ability to tax, imprison and govern without constraint. Credible promises about property and public debt developed through political institutions, not merely through private contracts.

Dutch and English financial innovations linked merchant credit, banking, shareholding and public obligations. As creditors obtained stronger expectations about taxation and repayment, governments could borrow on more predictable terms. These processes were uneven and coexisted with colonial violence, monopolies, slave labour and the extraction of resources outside Europe. The development of secure claims for some investors did not imply security for all communities exposed to imperial rule.

### 6. Independence, colonial land and international capital

Nineteenth-century revolutions and civil codes changed the language of ownership, inheritance and state responsibility. In the Americas, formal independence often preserved concentrated landholding inherited from colonial arrangements. Indigenous claims, communal rights and the consequences of coercive acquisition were regularly treated unequally. A title registered by the successor state could be lawful within its system and still reflect dispossession.

International investors financed ports, railways, mines and sovereign debt in states whose administrative and military capacities differed considerably. The collection of debts sometimes involved diplomatic pressure or direct military intervention. Foreign investment and geopolitical influence became intertwined long before modern sanctions regimes. The key question remains which rights can be enforced, by whom and at whose expense.

### 7. The twentieth century: currency, banking and sovereign failures

War and revolution exposed the difference between legal title, money and economic control. World War I produced requisitions, currency controls and rapidly changing sovereign obligations. The Russian Revolution fundamentally restructured ownership of banks, companies and land. Inflationary episodes destroyed purchasing power even when nominal obligations were discharged as written.

The Great Depression demonstrated the danger of banks financing long-maturity assets with short-term deposits. The creation of United States federal deposit insurance in 1933 and later lender-of-last-resort arrangements addressed some coordination failures, without insuring every financial instrument against loss. Bretton Woods in 1944 established a particular monetary order combining adjustable exchange rates and widely accepted capital controls. The United States suspended dollar-to-gold convertibility for foreign official holders in 1971, permanently altering the global monetary system.

The Latin American debt crisis, Asia's 1997 financial turmoil and Argentina's 2001–2002 collapse showed how currencies, access to deposits, sovereign payment capacity and legal restructuring can diverge. The global crisis of 2008 showed how deeply interconnected private intermediaries could transmit losses through leverage, securitization and declining confidence. Cyprus in 2013 illustrated the difference between insured depositors and exposed creditors. Silicon Valley Bank's failure in 2023 demonstrated that sophisticated regulation does not make a banking institution immune to a rapid outflow of funds.

### 8. The turning point of 2022: sanctions and sovereign reserves

Following Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022, the United States, European Union and partners imposed financial sanctions and restricted assets of the Russian central bank under their respective jurisdictions. As reported by the EU Council and Euroclear, very large Russian sovereign assets remained immobilized. Statements about EU-wide immobilized balances and Euroclear's own affected balance must not be added together as though they were independent assets. The use of extraordinary revenues stemming from frozen assets was legally distinguished from simply transferring ownership of the entire principal. [4][5]

These measures are not evidence that every foreign saver is subject to the same restrictions. They establish that a large sovereign holding may be legally registered and economically inaccessible when it lies inside the operational or legal reach of another state's sanctions system. Ownership, settlement, political alignment and the right to transfer have become more visibly separate dimensions of financial security.

## III. How financial property can actually be protected

### 9. A deposit, a bond, a share and custody are different claims

A bank deposit normally makes the customer a creditor of the institution. Deposit insurance may cover certain classes of claim to a statutory ceiling; it does not cover any product sold through the bank. Public debt makes the investor a creditor of a sovereign, with monetary, interest-rate and fiscal risks. Corporate debt adds issuer credit risk. Equity grants participation in a business with no fixed promise to preserve the market price.

A securities intermediary holds or administers financial instruments under separate rules for customer assets, subcustody, reconciliation and insolvency. Segregation can greatly improve investor rights in a broker failure, but its effectiveness depends on valid records, lending arrangements, shortfalls and court procedures. A regulated custodian is not an insurer of future share prices.

### 10. The actual deposit-guarantee network in 2026

The United States FDIC generally insures eligible deposits up to **$250,000 per depositor, per insured bank and per ownership category**. The Securities Investor Protection Corporation provides a different protection regime for eligible customer assets at failed member brokers, with a total limit of **$500,000**, including a **$250,000** sublimit for cash held for investment; it does not reimburse ordinary market losses. [8][9]

The European Union's national deposit-guarantee systems generally cover eligible deposits to **€100,000**. From 1 December 2025, the United Kingdom Financial Services Compensation Scheme raised the basic limit for eligible bank deposits to **£120,000** per eligible depositor and authorized firm. These figures refer to different legal schemes; banking brands sharing a license may share one limit. [10][11]

In Switzerland, esisuisse protects qualifying customer deposits up to **CHF100,000 per customer and bank**. Canada's CDIC protects **CAD100,000 per eligible insurance category at each member institution**. Singapore's scheme covers qualifying deposits denominated in Singapore dollars up to **SGD100,000**; an American-dollar deposit there is not automatically protected by that scheme. [12][13][14]

Australia's Financial Claims Scheme protects eligible Australian-dollar deposits up to **AUD250,000** per account holder and authorized deposit-taking institution. New Zealand introduced a depositor compensation scheme in July 2025 with a basic **NZD100,000** ceiling per depositor and covered institution. Hong Kong's protection limit is **HKD800,000**, subject to instrument and participating-bank requirements. Japan generally protects eligible ordinary deposits up to **JPY10 million of principal plus qualifying interest**, with separate treatment for some payment accounts. [15][16][17][18]

Brazil's FGC generally covers eligible products up to **R$250,000 per CPF or CNPJ and financial institution or conglomerate**, subject to the applicable **R$1 million aggregate ceiling over a four-year period**. Equity funds, stocks, sovereign bonds and certain securitized products do not acquire FGC protection merely because they are distributed by banks. A large advertised interest rate is not a substitute for verifying the actual debtor, eligible instrument, group structure and guarantee coverage. [19]

### 11. Currencies: the dollar, Swiss franc, euro and other reserves

The United States Treasury market provides exceptional liquidity and global financial integration. A short-maturity Treasury bill has a different sensitivity to rate changes than a long-duration bond; neither guarantees future purchasing power in euros, reais or housing. The issuer is the United States Treasury rather than a private bank, but market pricing, taxation, settlement and sanctions remain separate considerations. [20]

The Swiss franc may diversify exposure to a domestic currency, but its exchange rate and domestic interest rates can work against a foreign holder. The euro is a common currency containing the credit obligations of distinct national sovereign issuers. Germany's bonds and bonds of a more indebted euro-area member are not identical legal claims. Singapore's monetary policy creates an additional currency regime with its own economic exposures.

A currency choice should be related to liabilities. A household financing education and healthcare in Brazil has requirements denominated in reais. Another family planning to purchase a house in Europe may need future euros. A reserve entirely in a foreign currency can suffer large changes in domestic purchasing power when obligations remain local. There is no risk-free currency for every household and horizon.

### 12. Gold, cryptoassets and things without a promise to pay

Physical gold held directly is not a bank's contractual liability, but it still carries price volatility, verification, theft, storage, insurance, transportation and bid-ask spread risks. Vault-held gold creates an additional relationship with the custodian and must be assessed for ownership, allocation, insurance and insolvency treatment. Gold is neither an automatic inflation hedge in every short period nor a source of cash flow.

Cryptoassets may give direct control over certain cryptographic keys, but price exposure, loss of credentials, hacking, smart-contract bugs and exchange insolvency can be severe. Tokens that claim a right to a physical asset require an enforceable connection between the token, legal ownership and the underlying asset; a digital record alone cannot establish a land registry's obligations. Decentralization of transaction validation does not eliminate political or legal jurisdiction over owners, service providers and real-world assets.

### 13. The difference between holding an asset and being able to sell it

Liquidity is a central form of economic protection. A government security may be sold quickly at an unfavorable price; a large rural property may take months or years to sell at all. A sound legal title does not create a buyer, and the sale process may require approvals, environmental due diligence, mortgage discharge or tax clearance. An asset should be tested for its capacity to finance expenses under stress, not only for its expected long-run value.

The cost of an illiquid investment is frequently hidden during prosperous periods. If a household depends on the proceeds of a farm or company to service its loans, a sudden loss of buyers can turn a temporary market decline into insolvency. An appropriately sized liquidity reserve serves a different purpose than a productive fixed asset.

## IV. Houses, land and farms: physical property does not imply autonomy

### 14. The urban home: a tangible asset surrounded by institutions

Real estate has a durable physical component but remains subject to territorial law. Registration, mortgages, easements, zoning, taxation, environmental protection, succession, leases and compulsory purchase all determine practical ownership. The house cannot be transported away from a jurisdiction facing conflict or fiscal pressure. It can retain residential utility when market prices fall, but cannot always be sold or rented when the owner wishes.

A tax cadastre, deed of sale and registered title perform distinct functions. The existence of one does not necessarily establish perfect title or resolve pre-existing claims. A thorough purchase requires review of the chain of conveyances, obligations attached to the land, physical boundaries, mortgages, authorized use and the seller's power to convey.

### 15. Rural land: title, water, soil, licenses, labour and logistics

A farm is an operational enterprise as well as an interest in land. Soil quality, access to water, climate, roads, energy, labour contracts, disease, finance and commodity markets determine value. A formally valid title does not entitle the holder to extract unlimited groundwater, cut protected forest or develop every mineral beneath the surface. Surface and subsurface rights may be separated; community and Indigenous rights can impose important lawful constraints.

Productive rural property may offer exposure to food prices, timber or land scarcity while concentrating risk in one region, crop, climate pattern and legal regime. The price per hectare is incomplete without the cost of documentation, licensing, water, logistics, maintenance and eventual resale. Cheap land can be economically expensive when rights are disputed or inaccessible in practice.

### 16. When foreigners may and may not own land

A strong rule-of-law jurisdiction can lawfully restrict foreign purchases. Switzerland's **Lex Koller** subjects certain nonresident acquisitions to permit conditions. Australia's temporary prohibition on foreign purchases of established dwellings, with exceptions, runs from **1 April 2025 through 30 June 2029** under the current policy. New Zealand's March 2026 reform allows certain qualifying investor-visa holders to buy or build residential properties above **NZD5 million**, without creating a general right to purchase sensitive agricultural land. [21][22][23]

Canada's federal restriction on certain residential purchases by non-Canadians generally extends to **1 January 2027**, with defined exemptions. Singapore applies controls and substantial additional duties to particular residential assets acquired by foreigners. The Reserve Bank of India differentiates nationality, residency, Indian-origin status and property class, with significant restrictions on farmland. [24][25]

In the United States, foreign acquisitions of agricultural land are subject to reporting obligations and potentially state-specific restrictions and national-security review. Brazil's Law 5,709/1971 applies to specified foreign purchases of rural land and certain companies under foreign control. Dubai permits freehold foreign ownership in designated zones, not uniformly across the United Arab Emirates. [26][27][28]

## V. The geography of legal protection: Europe and North America

### 17. Denmark: state strength constrained by institutions

Denmark's high placement in rule-of-law comparisons reflects broad institutional performance across checks on government, corruption control, regulatory implementation and justice. A tax authority capable of collecting revenue efficiently is not necessarily an adversary of private wealth: predictable taxation and enforceable contracts can reduce uncertainty and financing costs. Yet fiscal obligations, local rules for purchasing dwellings, housing prices and exposure to the Danish krone remain important. The institutional index does not establish that Danish residential property will outperform global financial assets.

A foreign buyer must examine residence status, eligibility, housing use, ongoing property charges and the ability to recover capital through sale. A house held for personal use serves a different function from a portfolio security. High social and legal stability can coexist with lower after-tax investment returns.

### 18. Norway: sovereign wealth and the limits of the model

Norway has accumulated substantial public financial wealth through the management of hydrocarbon revenues and the Government Pension Fund Global. This institutional response to exhaustible resource income illustrates how fiscal rules, political consensus and long-horizon governance can transform commodity revenues into a diversified public balance sheet. The state fund, however, is not a retail guarantee of private portfolio returns.

A private Norwegian investment still faces exchange-rate movements in the krone, housing prices, taxes, energy-sector exposure and regulatory requirements. Agriculture and land use are constrained by local law. The country's ability to defend institutions in a major political shock is distinct from the expected return on any individual property or company.

### 19. Finland and Sweden: strong institutions and strategic geography

Finland combines predictable administration, highly developed registries and strong education with a direct geographical exposure to Russia. NATO membership changes the framework of deterrence and mutual defense but cannot move land or infrastructure away from a contested strategic environment. Forestry and land transactions may involve location-specific controls, conservation obligations, and the practical management of timber and access rights.

Sweden combines sophisticated capital markets and extensive corporate governance with a floating currency and significant household mortgage exposure. Residential real estate values can change sharply with financing costs, even when deeds and courts remain reliable. A diversified Swedish equity holding and a heavily mortgaged Stockholm apartment are not equivalent risks simply because they share a legal environment.

### 20. Iceland: a high-quality institutional system that suffered an extreme banking collapse

Iceland is a useful counterexample to the idea that excellent social and institutional indicators eliminate financial catastrophe. During the 2008 crisis, its major banks had expanded far beyond the domestic economy's ability to provide ordinary emergency support. Failure and cross-border disputes revealed the dangers of the relationship between financial-sector scale and sovereign capacity.

The country continues to offer property and economic activity within a developed institutional framework, but a small currency, market concentration, volcanic activity and other natural hazards require independent assessment. The correct lesson is not that Iceland is inherently unsafe: a strong legal system cannot guarantee that every private balance sheet is sustainable.

### 21. Switzerland after Credit Suisse

Switzerland remains a leading centre for private banking, securities custody, financial services and currency diversification. It benefits from a long administrative tradition and well-developed legal processes. The emergency absorption of Credit Suisse by UBS in 2023 nevertheless exposed weaknesses in bank governance, supervision and confidence. The resolution changed the structure and concentration of the Swiss banking sector and showed that institutional reputation does not make subordinated securities or large uninsured deposits riskless.

A Swiss bank account should be assessed for its legal entity, balance-sheet exposure, eligible deposit coverage, securities segregation, fees, beneficial ownership requirements and sanctions obligations. Holding Swiss francs adds exchange-rate exposure. Buying residential property is a separate decision constrained for some nonresidents by Lex Koller. The country's financial reputation must not be used to conceal differences between bank credit, custody, land rights and currency.

### 22. Liechtenstein: foundations, succession and the cost of complexity

Liechtenstein's specialized financial services and foundation laws may serve lawful estate planning and the administration of family capital. Structures differ from ordinary bank deposits: beneficiaries, founder powers, trustees or board members, distributions and enforcement depend on governing documents and applicable law. Legal sophistication can help clarify succession and governance but increases fees, administration and the possibility of conflicts among professional service providers and heirs.

A foundation or trust cannot automatically defeat creditors, mandatory succession rules, tax obligations or sanctions applicable elsewhere. The underlying holdings are still exposed to their issuers and markets. Creating a vehicle without a coherent family-governance purpose may produce more fragility than protection.

### 23. Luxembourg: the fund domicile is not the domicile of every asset

Luxembourg supplies an established framework for collective investments, administration, depositaries and international capital markets. A globally diversified fund domiciled there can invest in companies and bonds across many countries. Its Luxembourg registration does not eliminate the market risk of those assets or the possibility that subcustodians and issuers are subject to foreign law.

The protective architecture lies in prospectuses, asset segregation, authorized service providers, disclosure, supervision and enforceable investor claims. A complex alternative fund, a money-market fund and a stock index fund may have different liquidity, leverage, valuation and redemption provisions while using the same jurisdiction. Price and resale prospects of Luxembourg physical property require a completely different analysis.

### 24. The Netherlands: rule of law and land that must be defended from water

The Netherlands scored 0.82 in the WJP's 2026 composite index, a strong result on the measure's institutional dimensions. Its ports, water-management infrastructure, administrative capacity and deep connection to European commerce support substantial productive activity. Yet climate exposure, the physical position of low-lying land and the long-run costs of flood management are material to the value and insurability of individual properties.

Housing supply constraints, rental laws, taxation and planning decisions affect yields and transaction costs. A legally secure house in the Netherlands may be expensive, highly regulated or physically dependent on public infrastructure. That combination is not a contradiction; it is precisely why institutional quality must be examined alongside local physical and economic exposures.

### 25. Germany: durable contracts during industrial and demographic change

Germany offers developed land registers, contract enforcement, deep public finance and a large industrial economy. These features support confidence in title and business transactions. Its industrial structure, however, is undergoing adjustments related to energy costs, automotive technology, international demand and demographics. Property protections remain relevant even when economic prospects become less favourable.

Federal, state and municipal rules shape property taxation, energy-efficiency requirements, leasing and planning. An investment in German government debt, a manufacturing company and a residential apartment carries three different combinations of credit, rate, currency, industry and physical risk. A relatively transparent purchase process does not ensure a financially attractive acquisition price.

### 26. France and Belgium: strong state capacity also means an ability to tax

France has codified property law, mature administrative institutions and large financing markets. These provide mechanisms to register and contest decisions, but the state can lawfully impose taxes, regulate leases, set building-energy requirements and revise incentives through the legislative process. Inheritance law and taxation may strongly influence how a foreign resident or nonresident should hold a property.

Belgium is home to important European institutions and financial-market infrastructure. Euroclear's role in the custody of Russian assets subject to EU sanctions shows that highly developed financial infrastructure can be required to restrict transfers under lawful measures. Legal efficiency can protect the rights of an entitled investor while simultaneously enforcing constraints against an affected holder. Belgian property markets, regional taxes and language-community institutions add distinctions not captured by the presence of EU institutions in Brussels.

### 27. Austria, Ireland and the United Kingdom: three institutional models

Austria combines developed contract law and Central European economic integration with land and second-home rules that can vary by province. A buyer must establish the exact property classification and local requirements. The reputation of Vienna's housing market says little about the liquidity of remote rural land or the fiscal exposure of a company with concentrated energy imports.

Ireland is significant as a domicile for cross-border investment vehicles and multinational technology operations. Fund regulation and investor custody rights may be strong even where the underlying company operates outside Ireland. Its residential market faces distinct housing-supply pressures, and an investor must not confuse the domicile of a traded fund with the geographic distribution of its economic risks.

The United Kingdom remains important for common-law contracts, courts, arbitration, global finance and property markets. Brexit demonstrated that the legal framework governing trade, professional mobility and regulations can change without a general breakdown of judicial enforcement. In 2026, the FSCS basic deposit limit of £120,000 protects defined eligible claims rather than guaranteeing the value of every investment product. Stamp duties, inheritance rules, tenancy law, currency and the local condition of homes all affect net security.

### 28. Spain and Portugal: common currency does not standardize property economics

Euro-area membership reduces the need to exchange currency between local transactions and monetary liabilities denominated in euros, but does not equalize regional demand, property taxes, legal costs or rental yield. Tourism and coastal property can create cyclical price changes and seasonal cash flows. Water scarcity, wildfire and insurance costs deserve particular attention in exposed landscapes.

In Portugal, acquiring a house, a rural estate or a development parcel raises different questions about building permission, leases, land classification, co-ownership and access. In Spain, autonomous communities can apply different tax and housing rules. Neither should be evaluated exclusively through the promise of residence rights, a promotional yield or the possibility of capital gains from tourism.

### 29. Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania and Poland: institutional protection beside a geopolitical boundary

The Baltic countries have developed digital administration and operate within the European Union and NATO. Their achievements in electronic registration and public services can reduce transaction costs and fraud. Strategic proximity to Russia and Belarus, however, introduces risks to infrastructure, physical assets and supply chains that cannot be eliminated by a strong title registry.

Poland has expanded industrial and logistical capabilities, particularly through European supply chains and its position in Central and Eastern Europe. Political disputes surrounding judicial reform have created uncertainty concerning checks on institutional power, although actual commercial law and court practices are more nuanced than a single political headline. Farmland acquisition may require specific authorizations. A logistics facility near a major transport network and an isolated rural plot should not be assigned identical risk profiles.

### 30. The United States: the centre of global liquidity also concentrates legal power

The United States offers unparalleled breadth in Treasury markets, exchange-traded shares, corporate credit, financial services and private investment. The dollar's international role enhances the ability of assets issued or settled under US jurisdiction to serve global transactions. The rule of law is enforced across a federal system with substantial state-level variation, expensive litigation in certain fields and consequential differences in property, corporate and family law.

The United States ranked 30th of 143 countries in the WJP's 2026 rule-of-law index, despite its prominent global financial position. Deep capital markets and high institutional scores are related but not identical properties. Legal and regulatory disputes, federal political conflicts and the concentration of certain equity indices are part of the economic outlook. [39]

Nonresident investors must also consider estate taxation. The IRS describes Form 706-NA filing rules when a nonresident noncitizen decedent holds US-situs assets with values and other factors passing a **$60,000** filing threshold. A filing trigger is not the same as an automatic tax charge on every account, and exceptions, treaties and asset definitions matter. [29]

The Office of Foreign Assets Control distinguishes blocking from confiscation. Property that is blocked may remain legally owned by its holder while dealings with it are prohibited without authorization. The functional effect can nevertheless be severe, since a holder may be unable to sell, move, receive payments from or otherwise use the asset. Foreign ownership of US agricultural land involves reporting requirements and restrictions that differ between states and categories of buyer. [29][33]

### 31. Canada: robust finance and contested housing access

Canada's banks operate in a developed regulatory framework with an established deposit-insurance system. The CDIC ceiling is determined by eligible category and member institution rather than by the household's number of bank-brand logos. Concentrated banking, substantial household housing exposure and close dependence on the United States economy affect the system's risk.

The temporary federal restrictions on purchases of certain residential property by non-Canadians illustrate that a predictable legal system can deliberately limit entry into an asset class. Province-level taxation and land rules may add conditions. Resource-producing provinces, urban condominium markets and remote agricultural land have different economic and physical exposures. Strong Canadian institutions do not guarantee the resale price of a specific property.

## VI. Latin America: real assets, money and unequal enforcement of rights

### 32. Uruguay: a regional alternative with relatively predictable institutions

Uruguay ranked 22nd globally and first in Latin America and the Caribbean in the WJP 2026 index, with a composite score of approximately 0.72. Its continuity of institutions, civil procedures and historical openness to cross-border economic activity help explain its appeal for lawful regional diversification. [30]

The positive institutional comparison does not transform all Uruguayan property into a safe financial instrument. Agricultural holdings face rainfall, commodity prices, production costs, water and biological risks. Coastal apartments can experience tourism dependence, vacancy and local concentration of supply. A small market may provide fewer buyers for large assets. The choice between owning local real estate and retaining liquid reserves in hard currencies remains a matter of purpose, not simply the country's reputation.

### 33. Chile: relatively predictable institutions, water and earthquakes

Chile has comparatively developed financial markets, substantial mining infrastructure and a history of macroeconomic institutions supporting investment. These factors coexist with political and constitutional debates, dependence on commodity cycles and severe physical hazards. Earthquakes, wildfire and water scarcity can influence properties even when courts recognize their ownership without difficulty.

Rural acquisitions require scrutiny of water rights, local hydrology, land-use permits and access to transport. A legal water entitlement has little economic value if its practical supply is declining; conversely, physical access to water does not necessarily establish the right to extract it. Mining assets involve concessions, environmental duties, surrounding communities and price cycles rather than merely the metal present underground.

### 34. Brazil: financial depth and unequal local execution

Brazil combines sizeable sovereign-debt markets, payments infrastructure, sophisticated domestic financial products, agricultural capability and extensive systems of land registration with legal fragmentation, slow litigation, varying local enforcement, environmental disputes and important security inequalities. WJP's 2026 score of approximately 0.51 masks meaningful differences among civil justice, criminal justice and regulatory factors. [31]

A Brazilian property transaction requires updated registry records, an examination of liens and litigation, verification of the seller's authority, land-use restrictions and the factual state of possession. Rural property adds cadastral obligations, mapping where required, environmental compliance, water entitlements and rights held by communities and neighbouring owners. Tax and environmental cadastres do not independently prove perfect title.

The FGC's guarantee covers particular financial claims under defined limits, not all funds and securities sold by local institutions. Concentrating bank deposits, a company's working capital, a government bond portfolio and a farm in one country can generate common exposure to exchange-rate, fiscal, political and climate shocks. International diversification may reduce some correlations when conducted lawfully, while adding costs, taxes, succession complications and a separate set of risks.

### 35. Paraguay: the agricultural frontier and the hidden cost of unclear title

Paraguay offers extensive agricultural activity, hydroelectric energy and opportunities linked to its regional trade network. Relatively modest land prices can reflect genuine differences in income and infrastructure; they can also reflect poor transport, market access, social conflict or the cost of clarifying ownership. A low price per hectare is not an independent measure of safety.

The land registry, historical transfers, actual occupation, municipal and rural land classifications, environmental liabilities and community rights require special diligence. Particular restrictions may apply near borders or to certain categories of foreign buyer. Crop returns depend on water, climate, export logistics and credit, while large farm resale transactions may be slow. Paper ownership should be verified against operational control.

### 36. Argentina: the separation of property and convertibility

Argentina's repeated bouts of inflation, exchange-rate instability, sovereign restructuring and capital controls make it a particularly instructive case. An apartment can remain in the owner's possession while the national currency depreciates and the availability of foreign currency for transfer or payment changes. The resale value denominated in dollars, the cash flow collected in pesos and the right to remit proceeds are three distinct matters.

Economic reform may improve inflation, financing and asset prices, but a change in current market sentiment does not erase the history of policy reversals. Agricultural estates may possess productive value under international commodity markets yet face domestic taxes, logistics costs and rules affecting export proceeds. Holding something tangible can mitigate one risk while introducing several others.

### 37. Mexico: integration with North America and unequal territorial security

Mexico's industrial integration with the United States gives it important export manufacturing capacity and logistical opportunities. At the same time, organized crime, uneven physical security and different state-level court and land practices can alter the value of property and the cost of operations. A factory in a developed industrial corridor differs from a speculative tourism development in both its cash flow and exposures.

In restricted coastal and border zones, foreign real-estate purchasers may need legally prescribed holding arrangements; an apparently ordinary freehold purchase cannot be assumed. Thorough diligence requires identifying the title, authorized buyer, actual possession, applicable trust or entity rules, environmental risks and ongoing obligations.

### 38. Panama and Costa Rica: dollars, logistics, coastal rights and transparency

Panama's use of the US dollar in much of its monetary system, financial-services sector and canal-related logistics creates a distinctive economic profile. Dollarization changes domestic monetary constraints but does not guarantee fiscal policy, bank credit quality, property titles or political decisions. Corporate and foundation structures require legitimate disclosure, beneficiary identification and an understanding of applicable tax regimes.

Costa Rica has developed services, tourism and an institutional setting that appeals to many foreign residents. However, beachfront and concession property may carry rights very different from ordinary inland titled land. Coastal zones, environmental conservation, erosion, permits and infrastructure can sharply constrain use. Marketing language is not a substitute for checking the exact legal classification and whether a transfer can be registered.

### 39. Colombia and Peru: mining, cities and uneven land rights

Both countries contain important resources, urban economies and potential investment opportunities, but legal and physical risk vary substantially by region and asset. A metropolitan office building, mine concession and remote rural holding face different institutions and communities. Political conflict, security problems, environmental licensing and conflict over land use can materially affect cash flows.

A national law may be well defined while local adjudication, surveying and enforcement remain difficult. The value of a mine depends on rights, permits, financing, logistics and social consent, not only on its geological resource. A rural estate with disputed occupation should not be categorized with a liquid bond merely because both are owned in the same country.

### 40. Venezuela: low prices cannot substitute for enforceable rights

Venezuela's low position in the WJP 2026 ranking reflects major institutional failures relevant to private and commercial rights. Currency instability, changes in property rules, sanctions exposure and weak access to predictable remedies all complicate asset valuation. An apparently inexpensive property may conceal severe limits on transfer, protection, insurance or day-to-day use.

Recovery in the value of assets following a hypothetical political transition is possible in principle, but it is speculative and depends on institutions, stabilization, recognition of past titles and access to capital. A low entry price alone is not a basis for describing an investment as a safe haven.


## VII. Asia and the Pacific: commercial protection, sovereignty and proximity to conflict

### 41. Singapore: efficient commercial administration without an automatic equivalence of political rights

Singapore combines strong scores in corruption control, public order and regulatory enforcement with sophisticated banking and international capital-market infrastructure. In the 2026 WJP index, its composite score was approximately 0.79, with individual factor scores that differed materially. Its standing as a centre for regional investment is supported by high-quality infrastructure and an active regulatory state, rather than by the absence of public authorities from the economy. [32]

The Singapore dollar provides currency exposure distinct from the US dollar or euro. Local deposit insurance covers defined Singapore-dollar deposits, not every foreign-currency balance placed with a bank in Singapore. Residential property is subject to significant buyer duties and ownership conditions for some foreign investors. A country that is convenient for custody or business organization may not be financially attractive for buying a second home.

### 42. Japan: credible contracts, a variable yen and seismic risk

Japan has developed legal systems, financial markets, technological capacity and high administrative competence. These help establish property title and enforce commercial obligations. They coexist with substantial public debt, an ageing and in some regions shrinking population, currency fluctuations and the physical risk of earthquakes and extreme weather.

The same Japanese legal environment can support a liquid government bond, shares in a global manufacturer and a small rural house whose local population is declining. Government bonds carry maturity and currency risks, corporations face profitability and market-price risks, and housing faces location, maintenance, construction and resale constraints. In depopulating municipalities, structures can lose value even while ownership remains securely recorded.

### 43. South Korea: sophisticated industry under enduring military tension

South Korea's electronics, semiconductors, automotive production, education and financial development provide a substantial base of productive activity. Its property and contract frameworks are materially different from those in jurisdictions with weak administration. Nonetheless, North Korea's proximity introduces geopolitical risk that can affect manufacturing operations, insurers, logistics and investor confidence.

Residential finance and large household liabilities can also make property values sensitive to interest rates and employment. A portfolio of internationally traded Korean shares may have a different financial exposure to the peninsula than a manufacturing plant located near a potential military confrontation. Industrial capability is not itself insurance against interruption.

### 44. Taiwan: critical technology in a strategically exposed location

Taiwan combines sophisticated technological production and developed commercial institutions with exceptional exposure to tensions across the Taiwan Strait. Critical semiconductor facilities are economically valuable because of accumulated engineering skill, high capital intensity and global dependence on their output. Their location, however, cannot be treated as free of blockade, logistical or physical-security risk.

Shares in a company listed abroad, a Taiwanese factory, a bank balance and a residential apartment would be affected differently by a cross-strait crisis. A valuation model that incorporates future chip demand but ignores possible disruption of production, payments or shipping is incomplete. The reverse error is to present a conflict as inevitable rather than a conditional and politically mediated scenario.

### 45. Hong Kong: financial effectiveness and changes in institutional autonomy

Hong Kong retains common-law legal traditions, internationally connected banks, securities markets and an established role in channelling capital between China and international investors. Its currency and deposit-insurance scheme have recognizable features; eligible deposits are not equivalent to unprotected market investments.

Political and legal changes since 2020 have altered the setting in which civil liberties, national-security rules and institutional autonomy operate. A commercial dispute may still benefit from developed legal expertise while an investor's political and cross-border assumptions require re-examination. Financial integration with the mainland increases some opportunities and dependencies simultaneously. Legal title, freedom to transfer and the independence of policy decisions must be evaluated separately.

### 46. Mainland China: state capacity is not the same as limits upon state power

China's industrial scale, infrastructure, commercial markets and administrative coordination have enabled exceptional economic transformation. WJP's 2026 composite score of approximately 0.48 reflects institutional characteristics different from those in highly ranked liberal democracies. Government capacity can accelerate industrial construction while imposing capital controls, national-security measures or administrative priorities that affect private claims. [33]

Land tenure also differs structurally. Urban land is state-owned, while rural land is generally subject to collective ownership arrangements. Legally recognized land-use rights can be assigned or transferred under specified conditions, but they must not be represented as the same kind of perpetual private territorial title found in some other jurisdictions. Residential investment requires analysis of use-right duration, developer liabilities, local demand and financing, not merely the existence of a deed or sales contract.

### 47. India: growth, contract traditions and heterogeneity of courts and land

India combines a vast domestic market, an extensive common-law inheritance, technology-service industries and significant regional diversity. National legal rights are implemented through courts and registries with varying capacity, and land disputes can involve possession histories, rural records, local authorities and overlapping claims. Economic growth at the national level cannot replace diligence on a specific site.

The Reserve Bank of India's rules distinguish citizenship, residency, overseas Indian status and asset category. Ordinary residential purchases and agricultural land may not be available to the same foreign buyer under the same conditions. A structure that appears permitted in one market or for one investor category should not be assumed valid everywhere.

### 48. Indonesia, Malaysia and Thailand: the danger of confusing use rights with freehold

Southeast Asian property markets attract foreign residents and businesses, but restrictions vary substantially. In some places, foreign buyers may obtain condominium rights, leaseholds, long-term usage rights or interests through legal entities without obtaining unrestricted ownership of the land beneath them. The difference becomes critical when a right expires, is not renewed, is invalidated or cannot be passed on as intended.

The economic risks include local borrowing costs, tourism dependence, floods, currency movements, construction quality and the management of rental property. Attempts to evade ownership restrictions through nominal local owners or undocumented arrangements may create severe enforceability problems. The legal form must match the actual beneficial interest and applicable law.

### 49. Australia: reliable registration, water scarcity and demanding foreign-investment rules

Australia combines developed land registries, common-law institutions, sophisticated banks and important mineral and agricultural resources. This provides conditions favourable to lawful ownership and commercial activity. Its geography creates material drought, wildfire, flood, distance and logistics risks. The economic value of agricultural land depends heavily on water access and local rights.

As of the 2026 rules, foreign investors were generally prohibited from buying established dwellings from April 2025 to June 2029, subject to limited exceptions. New construction and development may receive different treatment. The purchase of agricultural land may require foreign-investment review and reporting. A foreign saver cannot infer ownership eligibility from the stability of Australian financial institutions. [22]

### 50. New Zealand: institutional security and restrictions on foreign property entry

New Zealand offers generally strong public administration, legal registration and developed agricultural and tourism activity. Overseas investment law nevertheless restricts residential, agricultural and other sensitive land transactions by certain foreigners. Amendments effective in March 2026 created a defined pathway for some qualifying investor-visa holders to acquire or construct homes above NZD5 million, not a universal entitlement to rural land.

Earthquakes, the isolation of transport networks, exposure to agricultural exports and the relatively small number of buyers in some property markets affect investment outcomes. A legally recognized title can coexist with high insurance costs or limited resale demand. The attractiveness of a property for personal residence may diverge sharply from its expected financial return.

## VIII. The Middle East, Africa and jurisdictions under exceptional pressure

### 51. United Arab Emirates: international wealth within territorially specific property systems

Dubai and Abu Dhabi have become centres of international business, aviation, logistics, banking and property development. Foreign ownership rules depend on the emirate, designated freehold areas, licensing zones and specific property categories. A title valid within a designated development is not a general right to own every kind of land in the UAE.

Concentrated government decision-making, cross-border capital flows and regional geopolitical tensions require separate assessment. Property-market liquidity can expand rapidly during inflows and contract when borrowing, tourism or international demand changes. Tax advantages do not by themselves establish net security after fees, vacancy, service charges and succession costs.

### 52. Saudi Arabia: sovereign financial capacity and concentrated decision-making

Saudi Arabia's public resources, energy sector and large-scale investment programmes create financial and industrial capacity. The state can finance projects of exceptional scale while retaining centralized political authority. An announcement of a development programme should not be interpreted as an independently verified return for every landholder, supplier or equity investor.

Foreign ownership and operational rights may depend on licensing, project status and domestic regulations. A concentrated state can implement contracts and infrastructure effectively, yet also shift priorities in ways that affect private expectations. Political and fiscal capacity must be distinguished from independent constraints on executive discretion.

### 53. Israel: developed private law and territorial war risk

Israel has advanced technological sectors, entrepreneurial capabilities, developed markets and private-law institutions. Recent military conflict and regional security threats create exposures that can affect factories, homes, insurance, labour supply and logistics. A strong commercial contract cannot eliminate physical destruction or supply-chain interruption.

Shares traded in foreign markets may allow participation in particular Israeli companies while carrying different operational and custody risks from owning a building or productive facility locally. Political polarization, fiscal financing of security and the direction of regional relationships can alter the economic risk profile without automatically invalidating property rights.

### 54. Türkiye: strategic location, inflation and policy predictability

Türkiye combines a large industrial economy and strategic geography connecting Europe, the Black Sea, the Caucasus and the Middle East. These connections offer trade and investment opportunities. Inflation, exchange-rate fluctuations and monetary-policy reversals have also affected the ability to preserve value in local currency.

Property can protect against some currency shocks while remaining exposed to financing costs, location-specific demand, taxes, seismic risks and the ability to convert proceeds into foreign currency. Political institutions, relations with NATO partners and trade with Russia and other neighbours influence risk, but no single diplomatic alignment determines the value of each Turkish asset.

### 55. Qatar, Kuwait and Bahrain: energy-rich sovereigns with small markets

These Gulf economies combine concentrated public financial resources, energy income or financial-services specialization with small domestic markets and different legal frameworks for foreign landholding, residence and investment. The security of a bank claim must be examined according to the actual institution and its regulatory framework, not simply the wealth of the sovereign.

Real estate dependent on foreign workers, construction programmes or expatriate demand may fluctuate with fiscal and migration decisions. Energy revenue supports public finances when prices and production permit, but concentration and regional conflict remain relevant. Foreigners must verify the property types they can lawfully own and the succession rights attached to them.

### 56. South Africa: constitutional law, agrarian disputes and infrastructure risk

South Africa possesses constitutional protections, established financial markets and a judiciary with substantial institutional authority. At the same time, electricity supply, infrastructure, public safety, fiscal conditions, unemployment and disputes surrounding land reform matter to investors. Legal changes relating to expropriation must be assessed under the constitution, due process and the actual statute, rather than assumed to mean an indiscriminate abolition of all private ownership.

Physical security and water availability vary locally. Agricultural operations need to understand labour relations, community claims, land documentation and infrastructure. A sophisticated stock exchange and serious electricity problems can coexist in the same country; one does not negate the other.

### 57. Nigeria, Kenya and Ghana: economic potential and heterogeneous titles

Nigeria, Kenya and Ghana contain large labour markets, growing business activity and regional centres for finance, trade and technology. Their legal and administrative conditions are not identical. Inflation, currency accessibility, infrastructure, energy, court enforcement and corruption risks can affect capital differently depending on the country and sector.

Some land systems combine customary and statutory rights. Ownership therefore cannot always be understood from one conventional title document. Community claims, compulsory registration, actual occupation and the validity of conveyances require specialist local review. A metropolitan development and a remote agricultural holding can have radically different risk structures despite sharing national borders.

### 58. Mauritius: an investment domicile is not a risk-free ownership claim in Africa

Mauritius is significant for the domiciliation of certain funds and cross-border investment structures serving Africa and Asia. Its institutional services can facilitate administration, legal organization and treaty-compliant investment. A Mauritian vehicle owning companies, buildings or infrastructure abroad remains exposed to the law, politics and economic conditions where those assets operate.

Corporate registration must not be mistaken for a legal exemption from tax transparency, sanctions or beneficial-ownership rules. Investment structures can help organize legitimate risk and succession but cannot neutralize poor commercial prospects, governance failures or physical losses in underlying holdings.

### 59. Russia: physical assets, sanctions and cross-border immobilization

Russia retains substantial natural resources, industrial capacity and domestic markets. However, war, sanctions, retaliatory regulations, restrictions on payments and the geopolitical position of investors create exceptional uncertainty for cross-border holdings. Shares and bonds may remain legal financial claims yet be difficult to trade, settle, receive income from or transfer.

The freeze of major sovereign reserves under foreign jurisdictions illustrates the distinction between economic value and effective mobility. Russian domestic restrictions and foreign sanctions apply through different legal channels. A serious analysis identifies the holder, bank, custodian, transaction, relevant law and exceptions, rather than assuming either that all assets have vanished or that sanctions can simply be ignored.

### 60. Ukraine: reconstruction potential and continuing physical loss

Ukraine has endured destruction of infrastructure, displacement, disruption of agricultural land, industrial losses and changes in territorial control since Russia's full-scale invasion. The potential scale of reconstruction is economically important, but a future opportunity does not make a current farm, home or factory a low-risk store of value.

Property claims can involve destroyed archives, damaged buildings, occupied areas, mines and contamination of farmland, insurance exclusions and long-running restitution disputes. Access to credit, external aid, courts and land records will determine how quickly surviving and damaged assets can return to productive use. Physical security remains inseparable from legal security.

### 61. Countries facing institutional collapse: a price can fall farther than the value of the right

In jurisdictions experiencing sustained breakdown of public administration, widespread violence and severe judicial weakness, the low price of a house, farm or business may reflect a profound loss of enforceability. A contract with an intermediary is of limited value if courts cannot operate, registries are controlled by armed actors or roads cannot be used safely.

Political improvement can eventually produce significant revaluations, but the timing and beneficiaries are uncertain. Absent accessible remedies, lawful transferability and security of possession, apparent bargain prices do not constitute conservative asset preservation. An investor seeking such returns is taking exceptional speculative risk, not purchasing a reliable substitute for cash or sovereign debt.


## IX. Foreign intervention and the limits of sovereign control over assets

### 62. One country need not invade another to prevent a transfer

Financial coercion can operate through sanctions, correspondent banking, financial messaging and settlement systems, exports of technology, insurance, shipping and central securities depositories. A government may have no authority over the land on which a factory stands yet influence whether the factory can obtain equipment, receive payments or sell its products internationally. The mechanism matters more than slogans about economic sovereignty.

An applicable financial sanction may impose legal obligations on banks and custodians that are not located in the owner's country of residence. A foreign investor can be economically affected without the formal confiscation of his asset. The restriction's lawful scope, authority, exemptions, licensing, duration and judicial review must be determined specifically.


| Instrument | Who may be affected | Economic transmission | Interpretive limit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Blocking sanction | Listed person, entity or covered property | Transfer, payment and custody may be prohibited | A block does not establish permanent change in legal ownership |
| Foreign investment restriction | Buyer or foreign-controlled company | Acquisition barred or made conditional | Not necessarily retrospective cancellation of existing title |
| Capital control | Defined residents or transactions | Conversion or remittance limits | Formal ownership may continue under restricted access |
| Court seizure or enforcement | Holder or asset under jurisdiction | Loss of control or ownership through prescribed legal process | Not every lawful execution is arbitrary expropriation |
| Correspondent banking exclusion | Banks, firms or regions treated as high risk | Higher costs, delay or loss of international payment channels | Can arise through private bank assessment without general sanctions |

### 63. The dollar, payment settlement and indirect jurisdiction

The dollar's international role gives US financial institutions, settlement and correspondent networks substantial significance. Transactions routed through persons subject to US jurisdiction may fall under US restrictions even when the holder and underlying business are abroad. European and British financial institutions can be similarly affected by their own sanctions and regulatory duties. Not every payment involving a familiar currency automatically becomes illegal.

Financial institutions may also withdraw from relationships that appear costly or risky for compliance purposes, even without an all-encompassing prohibition. Such **de-risking** can impair legitimate trade finance, charities, family transfers and the operating liquidity of businesses. Legal restrictions and private compliance decisions need to be separated before assigning responsibility for an interruption.

### 64. Sovereign risk differs from the risk of asset blocking

Sovereign-credit risk concerns whether a state fulfils its debt obligations. A bank failure concerns an intermediary's liabilities. A sanctions-related block concerns whether assets or transactions can be used under applicable law. The same portfolio can be exposed to all three risks without their becoming identical.

A blocked asset is generally not necessarily transferred to a new owner. OFAC's guidance distinguishes **blocking from confiscation**: property may remain titled to the holder while prohibited transfers and dealings make its practical economic use difficult. Ownership on a statement and access to the proceeds are two different facts. [6]

## X. Transparency, taxation and succession: wealth across generations

### 65. The disappearance of the secret bank vault

The OECD Common Reporting Standard provides a framework for automatic exchange of financial-account information between participating tax authorities. In 2024, jurisdictions exchanged data concerning more than **171 million financial accounts**, representing around **€13 trillion**, according to OECD reporting. The exchange is structured by participation, residency, account type and relevant agreements; it is not an unrestricted database available to all governments. [36][35]

Transparency can reduce tax evasion, money laundering and illicit transfers. It also introduces documentation, privacy and compliance costs and the potential for erroneous suspicion or delayed transfers. Secure property protection requires both lawful information exchange and mechanisms to correct mistakes, protect confidentiality and challenge undue restrictions. An account abroad is no longer plausibly described as invisible merely because the institution has a reputation for bank secrecy.

### 66. Brazilian residents with property abroad

International assets can provide lawful diversification, but Brazilian residents must understand exchange rules, income-tax reporting, capital-gain recognition, beneficial ownership and the Central Bank of Brazil's information obligations. The annual **CBE** return generally applies when foreign-held assets reach **US$1 million or equivalent as of 31 December**, with quarterly requirements at higher thresholds, including **US$100 million** under specified reference dates and rules. The lack of a CBE filing obligation at lower values does not establish an exemption from tax reporting. [37]

Compliance records matter during sale, remittance, inheritance and bank due diligence. Proving origin of funds, ownership and the correct tax status can reduce operational disruption. Cross-border structures should be maintained for lawful governance and efficiency rather than concealment of a true owner.

### 67. Inheritance: a good portfolio may discover its most important risk too late

The transfer of wealth upon death can be governed by different laws depending on residence, nationality, location of immovable property, form of asset and valid choice-of-law instruments. Forced-heirship rules, spousal entitlements, estate tax and foreign probate proceedings may conflict in ways that require specialist coordination.

A Brazilian family with US-situs assets, a European property and heirs in several countries may need multiple legal procedures. A US broker's convenient custody interface does not automatically solve the rules applying to a nonresident noncitizen estate. Testaments that conflict, unverified heirship documents and inaccessible account credentials can leave economically valuable assets unavailable to beneficiaries for extended periods. [29]

### 68. Corporate liability and the family estate

Founding a company, operating a farm or providing guarantees can expose family wealth to business obligations. The separation of personal and corporate assets depends on valid structures and compliance with law; it is not a license to misappropriate capital, mislead creditors or evade enforceable obligations. Personal guarantees, mortgages, related-party transactions and tax liabilities can create direct channels from business failure to family loss.

Trusts, foundations and holding companies may be useful for lawful succession and administration, but no form supplies immunity against properly established creditor claims, sanctions, marital rights or tax duties. Complexity itself has a cost: governance failures, disputes among beneficiaries and reliance on a single professional administrator can create new concentrations of operational risk.

### 69. Liquidity and emergency reserves

Emergency wealth is useful only if it can reliably cover expenses when needed. Insured eligible bank deposits can protect against specified bank failures up to defined ceilings. Legal institutions, payment access and currency matching determine usefulness. A household should identify its near-term liabilities, the actual bank group and which accounts fall within each guarantee category.

Cash held far above insured limits is a private credit exposure to the bank. Moving all cash into an inaccessible property may eliminate bank credit exposure but destroy emergency liquidity. Distributing funds mechanically among many unfamiliar firms can introduce administration costs and fraud risk.

### 70. Nominal preservation and liquid sovereign instruments

High-credit-quality, short-maturity sovereign instruments can serve as one source of relatively liquid reserves, with differences according to issuer and currency. Their nominal payment profile does not protect against every exchange-rate or inflation shock. Bonds with longer maturities can experience substantial mark-to-market losses before maturity when interest rates rise.

The jurisdiction of the securities intermediary matters alongside the issuer. The investor should determine whether the instruments are held in segregated custody, which entity owes the money, what legal protections apply in insolvency and how proceeds can legally be accessed.

### 71. Productive participation, funds and equities

Broadly diversified company ownership offers exposure to future production, innovation and profits, but no promise of stable prices. A fund domiciled in Ireland or Luxembourg can hold assets around the world. The legal domicile supplies a governance structure and access to a regulatory regime; the underlying assets still depend on corporate profitability, market valuations and the countries in which firms operate.

Leverage, high fees, complex redemption terms and concentration can greatly increase risk. A transparent, accessible and liquid portfolio may be economically more useful than a nominally prestigious investment structure that the holder cannot understand or redeem.

### 72. Gold, other metals and protection outside bank credit

Physical bullion can avoid the direct debtor risk of an uninsured bank deposit if the owner legally and physically controls the metal. Custody in a vault introduces another institution and contract. Market volatility, bid-ask spreads, theft, authentication, taxation and emergency transport remain material. Mining shares and metal-backed funds have different rights and exposures from privately held metal.

Gold's historical appeal partly reflects fear that governments will debase money or financial intermediaries will fail. It can diversify some shocks but need not increase value during every recession or inflation episode. It generates no productive cash flow by itself.

### 73. Housing: legally stable countries are not equivalent markets

A family home delivers utility in a specific social and geographical setting. Proximity to work, healthcare, transport and relatives may matter more than expected appreciation. Buying an apartment abroad requires an ownership eligibility check, clear title, tax calculation, maintenance budget, insurance and evaluation of future buyers.

A Danish or Swiss property can have strong legal administration and unfavourable access conditions for a foreign investor. A Canadian or Australian existing home may be subject to restrictions that make a proposed purchase unlawful. Expensive markets can impose high acquisition costs and offer poor rental yield even with predictable property law.

### 74. Land and farms: the comparison begins with water rights

Farmland in the United States, Canada, Australia, Brazil, Uruguay or Paraguay does not constitute a single comparable product. Foreign ownership rules, quality of land registers, access to water, environmental obligations, farm productivity, labour, transport and export policies vary. Returns are tied to crops, land prices and management rather than mere physical acreage.

An investment thesis should identify the legal buyer, title chain, surface and mineral rights, water rights, community claims, production costs and resale market. The fact that farmland produces tangible goods is valuable but does not remove political risk, weather exposure or the possibility of prolonged legal disputes.

## XII. The psychology of wealth and the search for a place beyond danger

### 76. The visible house and the invisible risk

Investors often assign greater security to what they can see and touch. A house remains visible after a market crash; a bond price displayed in a trading account changes immediately. Yet the house can lose buyers, require expensive repairs or suffer a disaster. Price discovery is not itself economic insecurity: the absence of a current market quotation can hide, rather than remove, risk.

Familiarity produces another distortion. Saving exclusively in domestic banks, government securities and property can feel comfortable because the laws, language and neighbourhoods are known. A major local currency, fiscal or natural disaster may simultaneously affect all three categories. Diversifying abroad can help, but unfamiliar intermediaries and legal systems create their own costs.

### 77. Bank runs and the collective logic of fear

Douglas Diamond and Philip Dybvig formalized how deposits withdrawable at short notice and assets invested over longer periods can create self-fulfilling bank-run dynamics. A saver can have a rational incentive to withdraw if everyone else appears likely to do so, even when the institution's assets would be productive if held to maturity.

Deposit insurance, supervision, resolution frameworks and lender-of-last-resort policies can reduce these incentives. Their credibility depends on clear rules and the public's confidence in actual implementation. Digital transfers can accelerate withdrawals, and social communication can amplify both truthful warnings and unfounded rumours.

### 78. The social meaning of property security

People do not seek only maximum financial returns. They seek a stable home, a way to care for relatives, autonomy in old age and continuity for children. A family farm may carry cultural identity and knowledge that cannot be priced easily. A savings account can have psychological value because it is readily understood, even if its inflation-adjusted return is low.

These motives are not irrational errors to be dismissed. They become economically problematic when emotional attachment conceals concentration, debt, deficient insurance or an unenforceable title. Secure planning integrates family needs with institutions and realistic liquidity.

## XIII. The future: five trajectories through mid-century

### 79. Institutional consolidation and stronger registries

In one trajectory, courts maintain independence, title systems become more accessible, disputes are resolved with due process and banking oversight remains credible. Better digital registries reduce fraud and costs provided records can be audited and mistakes corrected. The benefit is not simply faster transactions: investors have a clearer claim and a more reliable remedy.

This trajectory would become less plausible if executive authorities repeatedly disregard adverse judgments, beneficial-ownership records become unreliable or administrative restrictions are imposed without accessible review.

### 80. Geopolitical fragmentation and reduced asset mobility

In a more fragmented order, rival blocs may tighten sanctions, review foreign acquisitions, restrict strategic technology transfers and redirect payments infrastructure. Property can retain economic value but become less movable across jurisdictions. Firms may pay more for redundant supply chains, compliance checks and financing.

The scenario would be weakened by durable diplomatic agreements, reduced use of secondary restrictions, restoration of open transfer channels and demonstrated protection of lawful cross-border holders. More national financial autonomy can provide resilience in one direction while raising isolation costs in another.

### 81. Fiscal crises and the redistribution of economic claims

Higher sovereign borrowing costs, slow productivity growth and ageing populations could generate fiscal pressure. Governments may lawfully adjust taxation, benefit rules and regulations while lenders reprice assets. Banks and long-duration funds may lose market value even without a default. The owner of a high-priced property may face falling prices and higher annual expenses at the same time.

Fiscal pressure is not equivalent to arbitrary confiscation. The procedure by which burdens are distributed, whether changes are prospective, whether courts can review decisions and whether investors receive predictable treatment determine institutional consequences.

### 82. Climate shocks and depreciation of legally intact property

Floods, droughts, wildfire, storms and rising insurance costs can turn a formally secure title into an economically fragile asset. Farms may become less productive without irrigation, and buildings may require expensive adaptation or lose access to conventional insurance. Local governments may struggle to maintain roads and water infrastructure as the number of taxpayers changes.

Evidence supporting this scenario includes a persistent retreat of insurers, rising physical damage costs and declining utility provision. Investments in adaptation, zoning reform, efficient irrigation and resilient infrastructure could mitigate the effects, though not evenly across regions.

### 83. Digital custody, tokenization and technological risk

Distributed records and tokenized financial instruments may reduce the cost of transfers, improve reconciliation and widen access to some markets. They can also create dependencies on software, identity management, oracles, code audits, custodians and concentrated infrastructure providers. A token representing an apartment is not automatically equivalent to registered freehold title over that apartment.

Clear legal remedies remain essential. If code fails, someone must determine ownership, allocate losses and reconcile conflicting evidence. Institutional innovation can expand access without abolishing the need for courts, regulators and responsible intermediaries.

### 84. What evidence distinguishes these futures

| Observed change | Potential improvement | Residual risk | Evidence of deterioration |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Judicial independence and registries | Less arbitrary interference | Cost and delay | Repeated noncompliance with judgments |
| International tax transparency | Lower illicit flows | Mistaken freezes, privacy costs | No practical right to correct errors |
| Digital custody | Faster, cheaper reconciliation | Cyberattack and platform dependency | Persistent unrepaired outages |

## XIV. Stress tests and the limits of diversification

### 85. When a bank fails

Depositors face the legal scope of the insurance scheme, the time required for payout and the status of uninsured balances. Customer securities subject to valid segregation may recover differently from ordinary bank deposits, but records and legal access remain vital. A cash reserve divided between institutions belonging to the same banking group may not diversify coverage as expected.

### 86. When inflation destroys purchasing power

Nominal cash and fixed coupon bonds can lose real value while being paid according to contract. Inflation-linked securities, some business cash flows and assets with pricing power may mitigate some exposures, but they face their own valuation and political risks. The goal is to preserve the ability to buy necessary goods rather than merely to receive the contracted number of currency units.

### 87. When a government prevents a transfer

Capital controls, legally applicable sanctions and banking interruptions can restrict access. Holding an asset abroad can reduce exposure to particular domestic measures, but foreign custodians and payment systems are governed by their own laws. Legitimate diversification should be designed for compliance and resilience, not for concealing beneficiaries or circumventing sanctions.

### 88. When a house or farm loses its buyers

A property can remain intact and registered yet suffer from falling local demand, high financing rates or lack of credit. A large rural holding may need a rare buyer with operational expertise. The absence of a prompt sale can prevent the owner from meeting unrelated obligations. Borrowing against illiquid land can make this vulnerability acute.

### 89. When property enters an inheritance dispute

Multiple countries, different marital regimes, the absence of a coherent testament and inconsistently documented ownership can delay access to the estate. A good asset selection does not overcome poorly prepared succession. Families need legally valid records, beneficiaries with information about accounts and properties, and procedures compatible with relevant jurisdictions.

### 90. When two supposedly independent risks prove to be the same

A bank that holds large amounts of its own sovereign's debt is not economically independent of sovereign stress. Two properties in different countries may depend on the same agricultural commodity or international financing channel. A foreign fund and a local brokerage account may share the same custodian, depository and payment infrastructure. Diversification should examine common underlying exposures, not only distinct legal names.

## XV. The final boundary of protection: rights that survive a shock

The public authority is necessary to recognize ownership, adjudicate disputes and punish fraud. It is also the authority that can levy taxes, compel lawful acquisition for public purposes, regulate land use, impose sanctions and change legal conditions. Economic security therefore depends neither on the absence of government nor on blind confidence in it. It depends on clear powers, enforceable constraints, public procedures, independent review and accessible remedies.

A legal system that never changes may preserve unjust privileges. Another that changes through transparent legislation and reviewable procedures may offer greater real predictability. Institutional stability is not the absence of political choice; it is the existence of credible constraints on how political choices can affect rights. Personal wealth is safe only in a conditional sense: contracts must be enforceable, money usable, homes habitable, farms productive and future successors able to demonstrate their claims.

The most resilient arrangement is not a single supposedly untouchable jurisdiction. It is a configuration of lawful, documented rights, sustainable liabilities, credible intermediaries, appropriate liquidity, usable property and exposures that are unlikely all to fail for the same reason.
