# Global Macro — Oil supply recovers, but diesel scarcity and US inflation keep the cost of capital elevated

**14 November 2026 · Saturday edition · Information cutoff: after the completed Friday, 13 November sessions (Brasília time).** Saturday is not a new regular cash-market session. All market closes below are from Friday; official economic data retain their original observation periods. Neither a projected oil surplus nor an inflation forecast is presented as a completed outcome. [1][2][3][4][5]

The global energy shock is changing form rather than disappearing. The International Energy Agency (IEA) reported that oil supply reached **105.4 million barrels per day in October**, an increase of **2.3 million barrels per day from September**, and that observed global inventories rose **52 million barrels**. The agency's November outlook now points to a prospective surplus in the fourth quarter. Yet inventories held on land remain exceptionally low, diesel availability is constrained, and the cost of transporting oil from the Persian Gulf to Asia remains near recent highs. A higher volume of crude does not automatically produce an equally rapid expansion in usable refined products. [3]

At the same time, the United States reported **October producer-price inflation of 0.3% month on month and 6.5% year on year**. The monthly reading moderated sharply from September's 1.4%, but the annual rate was the strongest since November 2022. The US Treasury's ten-year nominal par yield rose to **5.15% on 13 November**, compared with **5.10% on 12 November** and **4.98% on 10 November**. Markets face two simultaneously plausible developments: a gradual restoration of physical oil supply and a longer period of restrictive financing conditions. [1][2]

## 2. The new oil balance: crude availability is not the same as diesel availability

The IEA's November report changes the near-term energy assessment. More oil is moving from the Gulf despite ongoing attacks on tankers and energy assets. The agency estimated Gulf exports excluding Oman at **18.4 million barrels per day in October**, still **26% below their prewar level**. Crude exports were recovering faster than product flows. Observed inventories increased as oil held on water grew, while on-land stocks continued to decline and remained at their lowest level since at least 2016. The resulting picture is neither a conventional shortage across all products nor a fully normalized market. [3]

Three supply-chain steps explain the apparent contradiction. First, crude supply depends on production wells, tankers and export terminals. Second, refineries must transform crude into gasoline, diesel, jet fuel and feedstocks; refinery maintenance, access to particular crude grades and damaged logistics change the product mix. Third, final users need distribution terminals, vessels, trucks and local storage. A bottleneck in either of the last two steps can keep diesel costly even while a crude benchmark declines. The IEA described tight diesel supply as an overriding concern for consumers, with European diesel pricing remaining far above its prewar level. [3]

The Brent benchmark settled near **US$95.84 on Friday**, broadly unchanged on the day but lower across the week. The IEA expects global demand to fall by **2.7 million barrels per day in 2026** to an annual average of **102.3 million barrels per day**, then recover in 2027. Those are forecasts for annual averages, not comparable observations of October supply and October demand. A simple subtraction of October supply from annual demand would produce a misleading estimate of an actual October surplus. The agency's prospective fourth-quarter balance is the correct source for the expectation of oversupply. [3][4]

Higher crude availability can lower the purchasing cost faced by importers; higher freight, insurance and diesel prices can offset part of that relief. Producers such as Brazil, Canada and Guyana benefit from export volumes but face weaker revenue per barrel when benchmarks fall. A fall in oil futures may therefore improve some importers' trade balances without causing an immediate reduction in transport invoices, headline inflation or the cost of borrowing.

```flow
Recovery in Gulf crude exports → more seaborne crude and higher observed global stocks → downward pressure on crude scarcity premium
Damaged shipping capacity + costly freight + constrained diesel production → persistent delivered fuel costs → transport prices and inflation risk
```

## 3. US inflation: a smaller monthly increase does not erase the annual burden

The October US producer-price release is a new observation, not an expectation carried forward from Friday's previous morning. Final-demand goods prices rose **0.5% in October**, while services rose **0.2%**. The index excluding food, energy and trade services increased **0.5%**. Transportation and warehousing services increased **2.0%** and truck freight prices **3.9%** in the month. The composition matters more than the relief implied by the 0.3% headline alone: persistent business-input costs can be transmitted into consumer prices with different timing across sectors. [1]

```chart

```

The year-on-year PPI rate of **6.5%** compares the October 2026 index with October 2025 and is not an annualized rate calculated from the single 0.3% monthly observation. A one-month deceleration can coexist with twelve-month inflation acceleration because the comparison month from the previous year drops out of the calculation. The BLS also reported that final-demand goods prices increased **10.2% year on year**; this does not mean household goods prices rose at that same rate, since producer and consumer price indices cover different transactions and weights. [1]

A more restrictive Federal Reserve response is plausible if cost pressure feeds into the consumer and personal-consumption-expenditure measures, but the producer-price release cannot by itself establish the next policy decision. The missing bridge is the observed transmission to the consumer basket, wage costs, demand and inflation expectations. A stronger dollar can also lower some imported input costs for US buyers while making dollar-priced energy and financing more expensive for foreign purchasers.

## 4. US bonds and the transmission from inflation to real investment

The US Treasury reported a **two-year nominal par yield of 4.88%** and a **ten-year yield of 5.15%** for 13 November. The ten-year yield was **4.98% on 10 November** and **5.10% on 12 November**; the regular market was closed on 11 November for Veterans Day. The comparison describes a rapid repricing of government borrowing benchmarks across the completed observations, not a change in a single lending contract. [2]

```chart
type: line
title: US Treasury nominal 10-year yield, selected completed sessions
unit: percent per year; US Treasury par-yield observations
2026-11-10 | 4.98
2026-11-12 | 5.10
2026-11-13 | 5.15
```

Higher sovereign yields affect businesses unevenly. A company whose debt matures in several years does not immediately refinance all liabilities at a higher rate. A construction project that must borrow now, however, faces an immediate change in expected financing costs. The burden also depends on the credit spread, maturity, collateral, hedging, project risk and the pricing of the firm's output. Higher Treasury yields should therefore not be equated with a measured, equal-sized rise in bank lending rates or corporate default risk.

The distinction is material for electricity networks, ports, industrial plants and artificial-intelligence data centres. Hardware may be ordered well before clients pay for computational capacity; power-grid connections and cooling systems can delay revenue. Higher discount rates lower the present value of distant cash flows and may make marginal projects less attractive, but listed technology shares falling is not proof of cancelled investment. Friday's **S&P 500 decline of about 0.4%** and **Nasdaq decline of about 0.9%** contrast with a **0.1% increase in the Dow**; concentration in technology and differing index composition matter. [5]

There is no verified evidence here that the latest daily change in US government yields caused a specific global contraction in corporate credit issuance, foreign portfolio investment or data-centre spending. The required confirmation would be contemporaneous issuance volumes, loan surveys, credit spreads and company spending revisions, not index moves alone.

## 5. Europe and the United Kingdom: growth persists but employment loses momentum

Eurostat's latest flash estimate places **third-quarter euro-area GDP growth at 0.4% quarter on quarter**, after **0.6%** in the second quarter. For the European Union as a whole, growth was **0.5%**, after **0.7%**. Euro-area employment was unchanged from the preceding quarter, while GDP was **1.3% higher year on year**. Output growth alongside flat employment is compatible with higher output per worker, shifts in hours worked or sector composition; the flash release does not separately identify those mechanisms. [6]

The United Kingdom reported a stronger **0.7% rise in real GDP in the third quarter**, after **0.5%** in the second. Services rose **0.9%**, manufacturing and other production **0.1%**, and construction fell **0.7%**. This divergence indicates that growth cannot be treated as a uniform regional recovery. The UK estimate is an early vintage and is subject to revision as fuller information arrives. [7]

European purchasers are exposed to the combination of dollar-priced imported fuel, maritime freight and financing costs. If crude becomes cheaper but diesel distribution remains disrupted, household relief can lag. In economies where services account for a larger share of growth, weaker employment momentum may also make consumers sensitive to a renewed rise in utility and transport prices. The response of national inflation rates and bond yields must be checked separately rather than inferred from euro-area GDP alone.

## 6. China: pressure at the factory gate, limited household-price pass-through

China's National Bureau of Statistics reported **industrial producer inflation of 5.7% year on year in October**, with a **0.7% monthly increase**. Mining and quarrying prices increased **27.9% annually**, raw materials **11.6%**, and processing-industry prices **3.5%**. These differences suggest that the strongest observed pressure was concentrated upstream, particularly in resource extraction and industrial inputs. [8]

Consumer prices, by contrast, rose **0.7% year on year**, and were flat month on month. Food prices declined **2.9% annually**, while non-food prices rose **1.5%**. The industrial PPI and household CPI have different coverage and do not describe the same purchase. A higher price for chemicals, electricity or metals may first be absorbed by intermediate producers, exporters, wholesalers or profit margins before reaching households. The speed and magnitude depend on inventories, contracts, exchange rates and competition. [9]

This matters to exporters and importers in Asia, Europe and Latin America. Chinese factories may face higher costs without being able to raise international selling prices proportionately. Downstream manufacturers importing components can face price pressure even when Chinese headline consumer inflation remains subdued. Conversely, a stronger yuan, weaker export orders or domestic competition could interrupt the transmission. Factory inflation cannot serve as a standalone proxy for China's consumer demand.

## 7. Brazil: fiscal expectations and inflation relief meet an adverse dollar environment

The Brazilian spot dollar closed around **R$5.6550 on 13 November**, while the central bank's **PTAX selling reference was R$5.6518**. These are different exchange-rate conventions. The Ibovespa completed Friday at **172,457.76**, a gain of **0.66%**, according to the Estadão closing bulletin. An intraday index reading, such as a 17:10 quotation, should not replace that final closing value. [12][13]

The Senate approved the base text of a fiscal package by **45 votes to 31**, with subsequent legislative steps still outstanding. Officials estimated a possible **R$60 billion impact on 2027 accounts**, but a legislative vote and estimated savings are not proof that the fiscal adjustment has entered into force or that each measure will deliver its projected amount. The pricing of long-term Brazilian debt depends on credibility, execution, primary balances and expectations about the inflation response, not the vote alone. [13]

The Central Bank's November Copom minutes described a more adverse external environment and moderation in domestic activity. The policy rate stood at **13.75% per year**. Brazil's September retail trade volume grew **0.2% from August**, but the quarterly comparison was **-0.3%** and the statistical authority treated the monthly movement as broadly stable. That combination illustrates why a positive one-month retail print does not reverse a more persistent slowdown. [10][11]

An improving global crude balance has mixed implications for Brazil. Imported fuel costs and some freight costs can eventually decline; lower international crude prices can limit oil-export revenues and petroleum-sector earnings, depending on volumes, contracts and domestic pricing. A more valuable dollar can raise the local-currency cost of energy and machinery imports even while the dollar price of those products declines. Brazil's consumer-price outlook therefore depends on both external commodity costs and the exchange-rate and fiscal channels.

## 8. Geography of the shock: producers, refiners and consumers do not face the same price

```map
Persian Gulf | Crude exports recover as security risks persist | IEA estimates October exports below prewar levels
Europe | Fuel distribution and diesel prices remain vulnerable | Crude price and product price diverge
United States | Producer inflation and Treasury yields affect credit conditions | No observed one-for-one loan-rate pass-through
China | Upstream industrial costs rise faster than household prices | Different baskets, lagged transmission
Brazil | Fiscal and currency risk mediate commodity-price relief | Spot USD/BRL and PTAX are different measures
```

There are at least four distinct exposures. A Gulf producer can ship more crude while paying more for maritime security. A European refinery can obtain crude but still face a diesel shortfall or exceptional freight costs. An American investment project can see lower crude prices while financing becomes more expensive. A Brazilian household can receive delayed fuel relief if currency depreciation offsets the dollar benchmark decline. Those differences prohibit a single statement that falling oil equals falling global inflation.

The distribution of financial pressure is equally uneven. Developed sovereign issuers, highly rated large firms, emerging-market governments and small borrowers do not face identical refinancing rates. The Treasury curve is a reference point for global dollar finance, but domestic currencies, hedges, regulation and balance-sheet structures change the final outcome.

No single Friday price movement establishes which scenario will prevail. The most decisive tests are **delivered refined-product costs**, **US core price transmission**, **credit availability**, **actual Brazilian fiscal implementation**, and **European employment and income**. The IEA's crude balance is a forecast conditional on shipping and security; it is not a guarantee against further supply shocks. [1][3][6][10]

## 10. A tighter causal assessment at the weekly boundary

The market's weekly boundary separates three different horizons. Measured October oil stocks describe what had already accumulated; the IEA's fourth-quarter surplus expectation is prospective; Friday's Brent settlement expresses a traded price shaped by both the physical balance and expectations. The US October PPI is a published historical monthly index, whereas the next monetary-policy decision remains contingent on additional data. Friday Treasury yields price future returns and risks, not a direct count of productive investment cancelled.

If shipping stabilizes and diesel output recovers, energy disinflation may eventually reach consumers and central banks even while crude benchmarks move little. If freight and refining remain constrained, the same crude surplus may coexist with elevated delivered fuel prices. Simultaneously, persistent services inflation and higher dollar financing costs can slow capital formation even without a fresh crude shock. The near-term global constraint has shifted toward the interaction between **refined-product logistics** and **the price of financing**, with the strength of that interaction still to be established by subsequent observations.
