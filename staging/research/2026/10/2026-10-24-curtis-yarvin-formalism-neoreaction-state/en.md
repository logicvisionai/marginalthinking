# Curtis Yarvin: from software engineer to architect of neo-reactionary state theory

Curtis Guy Yarvin, born in 1973, became known to a comparatively small audience of software engineers and political bloggers as Mencius Moldbug before emerging as a conspicuous reference in debates about the American executive, the administrative state and Silicon Valley's relationship to democracy. The critical question in his biography is not whether a blogger secretly directs governments. It is how a political argument first presented as an engineer's reconstruction of sovereignty crossed from internet essays into donor networks, political rhetoric and disputes about the functioning of public administration. Yarvin's proposals reject electoral sovereignty and institutional checks; their relevance is historical and political, not evidence that they work. [1][3][4][5]

His principal intellectual acts can be dated: the launch of *Unqualified Reservations* and the *Formalist Manifesto* in 2007; consolidation of the neoreactionary or NRx scene during the following years; Nick Land's *Dark Enlightenment* essay in 2012; and the *Gray Mirror* newsletter beginning in 2020. The biography intersects with Peter Thiel through technology-sector relationships and reported investment in the Urbit venture, with Nick Land through a different philosophical articulation of anti-democratic politics, and with Elon Musk through subsequent disputes over bureaucracy and DOGE. These links vary sharply in strength. Shared vocabulary is weaker evidence than a recorded financial transaction; even investment is not command over a person's beliefs. [3][5][6][7]

## Family, education and the technology workplace

Yarvin was raised in a family familiar with international institutions: his father worked in the United States Foreign Service. He lived abroad during parts of his childhood and pursued an accelerated education. He completed undergraduate study at Brown University and entered graduate computer-science study at Berkeley before moving into software work. Later accounts of his education stress the combination of unusual technical confidence with an outsider's view of academic and governmental institutions. The connection from this experience to a theory of regime change is an interpretation; neither mathematics nor computer science implies a preferred constitutional order. [5][6]

In California's software culture the ability to replace an architecture has special prestige. That habit helped supply Yarvin with metaphors—systems, code, ownership, debugging and a complete restart—but a constitution differs from a program. It distributes coercive powers among people with unequal interests and can neither discard its users nor return a population to a known safe backup. The attractions and limitations of computational language recur throughout his career. His rhetoric makes a dysfunctional political system sound like an obsolete technical platform, thereby compressing conflicts about legitimacy, pluralism and the lawful use of force into questions about management competence. [4]

## The 2007 formalist manifesto and its older antecedents

In the essay dated April 24, 2007, Yarvin proposed *formalism* as a political design in which actual power and recognized power would coincide. The core claim is that divided sovereignty produces a dangerous discrepancy between nominal constitutional authority and the institutions that effectively govern. His answer is not a better deliberative assembly: it is to make command explicit and centralized. Although he appropriates terminology from engineering and corporate finance, the underlying ambition belongs to older anti-parliamentary traditions and debates about sovereignty. Princeton's collection separately confirms the identity, date and authorship of the 2007 document. [1][2]

His analogy between a state and an enterprise is pivotal. A company can normally define its assets, board, managers and residual claimants; a polity contains children, non-shareholders, migrants, residents with no realistic exit, and people subject to criminal coercion. Contractual consent cannot be presumed merely because an administrator claims to be efficient. Moreover a corporate CEO can be removed under company law, while a ruler capable of commanding courts and armed forces could neutralize the very system presumed to constrain him. The proposal thus moves a governance problem rather than solving it. [1][4]

## Cathedral, epistemic authority and institutional criticism

Yarvin's concept of the *Cathedral* depicts universities, major media, credentialed professions and bureaucratic institutions as a mutually reinforcing generator of accepted beliefs. There is a serious empirical question concealed in the polemic: how do expertise, prestige, funding and recruitment shape public agendas? Scholarship on elite reproduction and institutional sociology examines some of those mechanisms. But the fact that organizations influence one another does not prove centralized control, unanimous ideology or intentional coordination across all journalists, academics and public officials. [4][5]

The Cathedral functions rhetorically as a unified adversary. That simplification allows disagreement to be treated not as plural politics but as an invisible regime defending itself. Falsification would require differentiating institutions, comparing dissenting decisions, identifying actual chains of authority and measuring independent appointments or editorial choices. Yarvin's broadest claims rarely specify such boundaries. The practical consequence is that criticism of institutional capture can become a warrant for removing institutional independence itself—a transition that needs to be assessed separately from the diagnosis. [4][5][7]

## Neocameralism, patchwork and the problem of exit

Across his essays Yarvin develops ideas often described as *neocameralism*: the state imagined as a joint-stock corporation, with management delivering security and predictability and those unhappy with the arrangement supposedly free to leave. His *patchwork* vision is of smaller territorial jurisdictions whose competitive performance would discipline government. These designs borrow the economic language of shareholders, incentives and exit while departing sharply from republican equality and judicially enforceable citizenship. [4][7]

The strongest counterexample is an individual who cannot move: a poor resident, a caregiver, a detainee, or someone whose property, language, disability or family situation makes relocation impossible. Exit markets also depend on other states accepting migrants, transparent rules, transport, money and limits on coercion. If landowners or governors control all local infrastructure, market entry and exit can be restricted. Even an affluent person moving between jurisdictions does not prove that the system protects those excluded from mobility. A plausible institutional assessment would specify residency rights, independent adjudication and rights surviving changes of manager; Yarvin's simplified firm metaphor omits these central public-law constraints. [4]

## Urbit, Tlon and the material world of venture finance

A second biography runs alongside the polemics. Yarvin developed ideas associated with Urbit, a project concerned with personal computing, network identities and alternative software infrastructure. Urbit's detailed engineering history is distinct from his constitutional program: a decentralized server system does not necessarily encode monarchism. Investors may fund a technology they think viable without endorsing every published political statement of its creator. The biographical record therefore cannot equate all code contributors, venture employees and financial backers with NRx adherents. [5][6]

Reporting on Tlon records Yarvin's account of a $250,000 Founders Fund investment, as well as other Silicon Valley investors. The firm declined to comment on that figure in the cited reporting; it should be attributed as a report rather than generalized into a secret political command arrangement. Peter Thiel's published interest in technological escape from politics offers a conceptual comparison, but his 2009 essay, Yarvin's 2007 formalism and Urbit's codebase are three different kinds of evidence—an argument, a proposal for state power and a venture investment. The economic mechanism worth examining is the conversion of relatively limited patronage into time, software development and intellectual visibility. [6][8]

## Land, Dark Enlightenment and ideological differentiation

Nick Land supplied a philosophically distinctive *Dark Enlightenment* formulation in 2012, after years of radically different work on capitalism, cybernetics and desire. Land was not simply Yarvin under a second name. His accelerationist prehistory relates to Deleuze and Guattari and the Warwick cultural-theory milieu; Yarvin's preferred lens is formalized sovereignty and administrative reconstruction. Their later convergence concerns hostility to political egalitarianism and liberal democratic legitimacy. The relation is an intellectual encounter between partly compatible but not interchangeable projects. [4][9]

The distinction matters for a precise genealogy. The left accelerationism articulated by Alex Williams and Nick Srnicek in 2013 sought a different political transformation, not government by an hereditary or corporate sovereign. A reader may observe that distinct authors share enthusiasm for industrial or computational capacity without thereby proving common normative ends. Calling every technology entrepreneur an accelerationist or every accelerationist a neoreactionary is analytically empty. The concrete questions are who should own productive resources, who can contest decisions and what outcome counts as freedom. [9][10]

## Gray Mirror and the executive-restoration hypothesis

In 2020 Yarvin reappeared for a wider audience through *Gray Mirror*. His writing shifted from remote sketches of possible governmental forms to more explicit contemporary arguments about leadership and replacing entrenched state personnel. His proposal associated with the acronym RAGE—retire all government employees—appears in his own account of administrative transition, though he also discussed retaining experienced personnel within a new chain of command. These claims should be read in full: what sounds like a personnel policy presupposes a radical redistribution of constitutional authority and public employment protections. [3][6]

His distinction between democracy's formal appearance and the operational control of agencies is an intelligible point of constitutional inquiry, but a leader who purges professional civil servants may reduce capacity, disrupt services and replace expertise with partisan loyalty. This is not a logically necessary consequence of reform; it is a measurable risk based on turnover, continuity of public service, service quality, judicial compliance and procurement transparency. One cannot establish a successful alternative merely by demonstrating friction in the status quo. [3][7]

## From online theory to the politics of 2024–2026

Coverage of the 2024 campaign and subsequent 2025 administration documented politicians citing Yarvin and commentators noting apparent parallels between his proposals and calls for an aggressive executive. The Washington Post reported links among Yarvin, Thiel's networks, J. D. Vance and debates around DOGE. Those are distinct layers of evidence: an explicit citation establishes awareness; social access permits influence; similarity in policy does not by itself prove authorship. Nor was Yarvin satisfied with DOGE: 2025 reporting describes his public criticism of its execution. An analysis that casts him as the sole author of executive restructuring contradicts that complication. [5][6][7]

Elon Musk's 2025 involvement in DOGE marks a practical case for comparison, not evidence of a precise Yarvin implementation. Staffing cuts, federal litigation, fiscal savings claimed by an agency and verified budgetary changes are different variables. By May 2025 Musk's departure was public, while reporting showed that earlier savings objectives had been sharply reduced. The test of a claimed leap in efficiency is not the intensity of disruption but audited net savings, lawful service delivery, expertise retention and durable results after leadership changes. [6]

## Social hierarchy, democratic equality and responsibility

Yarvin's arguments have attracted criticism for hierarchy, anti-egalitarianism and recurring polemics about groups, culture and race. These themes cannot be omitted because they bear on who would enjoy political standing in the regimes he describes. The relevant contrast is between equal legal citizenship—which does not require assuming all citizens possess identical expertise—and a system in which the governed lose institutional ways to contest unequal treatment. Critics and defenders disagree over the realism of his historical analogies; the anti-democratic direction of the formalist argument is plainly stated in the primary text. [1][4][5]

Two interpretations compete. Admirers depict concentration as a cure for dispersed accountability, rewarding performance through jurisdictional exit. Critics argue that electoral competition, civil-rights protections, separation of powers, collective organization and independent courts exist precisely because rulers are neither infallible nor sufficiently disciplined by the possibility of departure. A discriminating comparison would examine measurable service outcomes *and* coercion, civil liberties, corruption, migration costs and reversal mechanisms. A tidy organization chart cannot settle constitutional legitimacy. [4][7]

## An intellectual biography through the primary corpus, 2007–2008

The 2007 *Formalist Manifesto* and the 2008 *Open Letter to Open-Minded Progressives* should be treated as separate stages rather than interchangeable evidence. The first defines a political method: identify the holders of real coercive power, align their legal title with that power and stop pretending that dispersed constitutional symbols govern. The second elaborates an adversarial history of American institutions, assigning media and universities a central role in the reproduction of ideological legitimacy. Princeton University holds catalog records for both texts, establishing their respective chronology and authorship independently of later partisan commentary. [1][2][11]

The movement from formalism to the Cathedral matters causally. Formalism alone could be interpreted as a demand for transparency about power in any regime, perhaps even one compatible with democratization. The subsequent work makes a much stronger claim: representative institutions are structurally incapable of acting as the constitutional texts suggest, while a durable knowledge-producing establishment allegedly governs behind them. The diagnosis prepares the ground for the proposal to replace distributed authority rather than reform it. The historical argument rests on selective readings of American progressivism, the New Deal and modern administrative institutions; their existence, however, does not prove a single covert command hierarchy.

Yarvin's appeal to software programmers operates at this junction. Software architecture encourages identifying an owner for each responsibility, isolating failures and designing deterministic interfaces. Public power faces different constraints: a judge must be independent from a prosecutor, statistical agencies must resist politically convenient manipulation, and the opposition needs protected procedures for contesting decisions. What a programmer calls duplication may be a constitutional safeguard against a catastrophic single point of failure. The underlying debate is not whether engineers are competent to discuss politics, but whether an architecture designed for a purpose-limited artifact can establish legitimacy over unwilling human subjects.

## Patchwork and the economic impossibility of frictionless political exit

In his 2008 *Patchwork* discussions, Yarvin imagines jurisdictions closer to managed firms, where residents would select among territorial providers of governance. The analogy borrows from Tiebout-style public-finance ideas about households choosing communities with different public-service and tax bundles, but its political assumptions are stronger. Municipal choice under constitutional protection is not the same as a state whose executive can redefine rights and citizenship. The former presupposes a system of courts and citizenship outside the locality; the latter risks making those protections optional managerial policies. [12]

A genuine market in jurisdictions needs an outside option. For an ordinary family, international exit is a capital-intensive decision involving housing, linguistic skills, recognition of qualifications, health care, retirement rights and visa availability. If all feasible destinations require a similar payment or restrict immigration, competition is incomplete. Residents possess location-specific capital: family networks, property, social insurance and occupational accreditation are costly to abandon. Their loss is not a minor transactional inconvenience but an endogenous feature of territorial government.

The firm's shareholders and the governed also face asymmetric incentives. A shareholder may profit from increasing asset prices, while tenants suffer higher rents; an investor may welcome coercive stabilization of labour, while workers need bargaining protections. If the supposedly profitable government raises revenue through monopoly services, control of infrastructure or confiscation, its commercial valuation could increase even as residents' welfare falls. Profit is not a sufficient social objective for a territorial authority. The economic test would require independent welfare and rights measures, not merely shareholder returns or migration flows.

## Sovereignty, Hobbes, Schmitt and the missing theory of public law

Concentrating ultimate authority evokes older debates about the locus of sovereignty, including Hobbes's concern with civil conflict and Carl Schmitt's controversial emphasis on exceptional decisions. Yet analogous problems do not establish direct intellectual borrowing. Hobbes famously seeks an order able to secure peace against violent insecurity; modern constitutionalism distributes certain powers precisely to reduce arbitrary coercion. Yarvin's command-centred framework must be evaluated against both sets of dangers. The strongest argument for hierarchy—fast resolution of conflicts—comes with a corollary risk: a mistaken central decision can acquire national scope before any independent institution can halt it. [1][11]

Constitutional protections also carry economic value. Independent courts make contracts credible across changes of government; stable civil-service procedures preserve information and competence; transparent elections enable peaceful replacement of incompetent leaders. All mechanisms can fail and be captured, but their defects require targeted evidence. Abolishing them to reduce delay may destroy the institutional capital that makes investment and social cooperation possible. A ruler can promise long time horizons, but investors will price the possibility that the ruler changes his mind without an enforceable appeal.

Historical comparisons must therefore separate authoritarian effectiveness in a narrow industrial project from comprehensive social performance. A state may build infrastructure rapidly while restricting dissent and misallocating other resources. Evaluating a governmental form needs equivalent baselines for income, human rights, mortality, external debt, fiscal capacity, succession risk and ability to correct mistakes. A one-year surge in construction is not evidence for the superiority of unaccountable rule over a generation.

## The Cathedral theory tested against independent decision centres

Yarvin's image of a coordinated knowledge elite mixes several distinct processes: institutional isomorphism, class reproduction, professional licensing, bureaucratic inertia and explicit political coordination. Sociology recognizes that organizations can become similar because they recruit from the same institutions or respond to shared incentives. Such convergence can occur without meetings or instructions from a common director. Investigating it requires examining personnel mobility, grant patterns, editorial governance, legislative committees, court rulings and evidence of contrary institutional action. [11]

The strongest useful form of the Cathedral hypothesis would identify specific policies that survive electoral opposition because independent actors enforce them. It would then compare alternative explanations: bipartisan consensus, statutory commitments, international obligations, professional expertise and commercial interests. The weaker form interprets every contrary newspaper story or administrative court judgment as evidence of a unified conspiracy, creating an unfalsifiable model. Treating disagreement as proof of hidden agreement renders a political theory immune to history.

This distinction is essential to the relationship between Yarvin and public expertise. Scientists, journalists, universities and bureaucracies can certainly be wrong, partisan or financially compromised. The remedy for a particular error may involve public audits, competing research, freedom-of-information laws, stronger anti-corruption rules or independent reviews. Nothing in the existence of institutional bias logically implies that replacing independent institutions with one executive will yield more accurate knowledge. Centralization may instead magnify the influence of whatever beliefs prevail at the top.

## Civil-service replacement and the measurable structure of public capacity

The 2020s proposals about executive restructuring move from theory to an administrative problem. Civil servants perform activities with different legal status and timescales: benefits processing, tax collection, air-traffic management, inspections, diplomacy and scientific statistics. Removing employees en masse has effects that depend on occupational structure, institutional memory, contracted replacements and legal continuity. These are not secondary difficulties to be solved after a regime 'reboot'; they constitute the state's operating capability. [3]

The U.S. Office of Personnel Management identifies statutory merit principles under 5 U.S.C. 2301 and prohibited personnel practices under 5 U.S.C. 2302. Those rules express an institutional decision to limit some forms of partisan appointment and retaliation. Their implementation can be disputed, but the legal structure cannot be treated as if it were a discretionary corporate human-resources policy. Claims of savings from layoffs have to be adjusted for severance, litigation, fraud risk, procurement substitutions, delayed benefits and deteriorated performance. [13]

A policy can produce short-run cuts that appear efficient in an accounting table while increasing long-run costs. If a department dismisses specialized engineers, its payroll may fall even while dependence on monopoly contractors raises total project expenditure. A more robust evaluation asks which government function was reduced, whether the legal mandate remained, how performance changed and what the full cost trajectory became. In the absence of these measurements, the rhetoric of management conceals rather than demonstrates efficiency.

## Influence as documented transmission, not retrospective resemblance

Public profiles from 2024–2025 report that Yarvin's work circulated among political and technology elites. That is historically important: his writings became available to people with access to campaign finance, staffing and public attention. But an individual's exposure to an essay is one link in a long decision chain. Establishing substantial influence over a government policy would require evidence of timing, explicit adoption, drafting participation or correspondence, and comparison with other sources of the same proposal. Political parties had criticized administrative bureaucracy long before Yarvin began blogging. [5][6][7]

The venture connection with Thiel likewise needs limits. Founders Fund's reported investment into Tlon was a particular transaction associated with a software project. Financial support can allow an author to devote resources to coding and writing, and shared social networks may increase visibility. But a venture-fund partner and a founder are separate people with legally distinct responsibilities. The leap from investment to direction of an entire political movement requires evidence not provided by the transaction.

Musk's DOGE experience supplies an especially revealing comparison. Both writers and administrators may discuss replacing public employees, yet resemblance in target does not imply agreement on constitutional legitimacy, technical methods or ultimate ends. Yarvin criticized parts of DOGE, an observation inconsistent with a story in which all parties executed one unbroken plan. The meaningful historical question is where radical rhetoric intersects with orthodox conservatism, managerial fashion and actual agency powers—and where it fails to do so. [6]


## Urbit as an engineering artifact: Nock, Hoon, Arvo and Ames

The Urbit project offers unusually concrete evidence for evaluating Yarvin as a programmer rather than solely as a political writer. A 2016 technical paper co-authored by Curtis Yarvin, Philip Monk, Anton Dyudin and Raymond Pasco describes a complete software stack: Nock as a compact functional virtual machine, Hoon as a typed language, Arvo as an operating-system architecture, Ames as a networking layer, and Clay as a versioned data system. Those are technical components, not metaphors for sovereign government. The paper states its date, authors and architecture, allowing engineering work to be reconstructed separately from later journalistic mythmaking. [14]

The central design idea is deterministic computation over state and events. Contemporary Urbit documentation describes an Arvo transition as taking a state and an event and producing an updated state and effects. This gives developers explicit semantics, reproducibility and an approach to long-lived personal computing. The choice has engineering tradeoffs: a novel language and runtime create barriers to onboarding, compatibility and maintenance, while a tightly specified execution model may improve certain forms of reliability. Assessment requires tests of performance, vulnerability history, usability, ecosystem adoption and resilience under load. None of those properties demonstrates that citizens should lack electoral representation. [15]

The separation is especially revealing when compared with Yarvin's political desire for visible ownership. A personal server operated by its user can strengthen control over data relative to centralized platforms; a government monopolizes lawful force over people who do not necessarily want its services. The power to migrate one's digital identity is not equivalent to emigrating from one's country. A computer system can be forked or replaced without depriving unwilling users of citizenship or family connections. The technical analogy therefore ends precisely where coercion begins.

## Carlyle and the Italian elite theorists: an older genealogy than Silicon Valley

James Rosenberg's study at the Institute for Human Sciences identifies Thomas Carlyle and strands of Italian elite theory as important to Yarvin's synthesis. Carlyle's admiration for heroic leadership and skepticism toward parliamentary procedures supply a history of concentrated executive competence. Gaetano Mosca, Vilfredo Pareto and Robert Michels asked how minorities organize, acquire legitimacy and govern larger populations; their theories influenced different and often incompatible traditions of political sociology. It would be incorrect to treat every theorist of elites as an advocate of dictatorship. [4][16]

A useful distinction lies between descriptive and prescriptive elitism. The descriptive claim is that complex organizations require small groups to coordinate specialized decisions. It may be empirically plausible even within representative democracies. The prescriptive claim is that because elites always exist, elections, equal rights and organized opposition should be dispensed with. That conclusion does not follow. Modern democracy does not require all citizens to perform every administrative task; it requires protected methods for selecting, criticizing and replacing officeholders while guaranteeing fundamental rights.

The Cathedral argument borrows strength from elite sociology by describing the circulation of prestigious experts and the resources that sustain institutions. But elite theory also studies conflicts among elites, competition for legitimacy and their replacement. An educational establishment can fragment; a ruling company can split into opposing factions; and shareholders themselves can have conflicting interests. A theory of a single cohesive knowledge elite must explain these observed divisions rather than treating them as strategic theatre without direct evidence.

## Succession risk, information asymmetry and administrative monopoly

Yarvin's model of a unified sovereign often takes the incumbent leader's competence as a key assumption. The succession problem is harder: when leadership changes, who decides that an heir is legitimate, what prevents seizure by security forces, and where can residents appeal? Corporate law solves some analogous problems through shareholder rules and courts external to the firm. If the ruler also controls the courts, the analogy loses its enforcement mechanism. The concentration that supposedly eliminates conflict among public agencies may relocate conflict to succession struggles without an impartial arbiter. [1][12]

Information asymmetry produces a second difficulty. A leader needs reliable indicators of output, crime, prices, infrastructure and public satisfaction. If subordinates fear removal for reporting bad news, they may conceal failures. Independent statistics, press scrutiny and protected whistleblowing are not merely obstacles to unified management: they can supply signals that improve decisions. In systems engineering, redundancy and independent verification may prevent catastrophic failures. In politics, their equivalents also have constitutional and economic functions.

The same logic applies to jurisdictional competition. An administrator can advertise low taxation and rapid permits, but residents cannot evaluate hidden surveillance, contingent liabilities or long-term property security merely from migration advertising. Without an independent source of information, a competitive patchwork may become a market for promises difficult to enforce. This weakness does not prove all representative institutions work well; it identifies missing mechanisms in a specific proposed alternative.


## Hypotheses, documented connections and historical significance

Yarvin's demonstrated contribution lies in giving a recognizable terminology to a strand of postliberal, anti-democratic tech-adjacent thought. His direct intellectual relationship with Land is better established than any alleged unitary chain of command linking Land, Thiel and Musk. The contemporary network is heterogeneous: Land developed a theory of impersonal capital and computation; Thiel invested in technology and wrote about exit; Musk built companies dependent in part on public contracts and later exercised public-administrative influence. Their agendas may overlap over elite management and suspicion of bureaucracy, but they diverge on theory, tactics, interests and institutional position. [4][6][8][9]

A falsifiable version of the Yarvin thesis would predict that jurisdictions with concentrated executive authority, credible exit and unambiguous ownership consistently deliver superior public outcomes without corresponding deterioration in rights or corruption. The counter-hypothesis predicts that insulating authority from opposition removes error-correction and increases arbitrary rule. By 2026 the essays have undeniable discursive reach; they have not supplied a controlled demonstration of the political model's superiority. An account of his influence therefore ends with documented texts, investments, citations and policies, not mythology about hidden mastery.

## Political authority in observed institutions: comparator evidence, not proof of formalism

Yarvin's proposals for concentrated executive sovereignty and competitive jurisdictions can be examined against existing political systems without treating any country as an implementation of his theory. Freedom House's 2025 country assessments record political-rights scores of 34/40 for the United States, 19/40 for Singapore and −2/40 for China. The corresponding civil-liberties scores are 50/60, 29/60 and 11/60. These values come from expert-coded rights criteria, not measurements of public-sector efficiency, economic development or Yarvin's influence. Their scales are separate, so each dimension is displayed independently. [17][18][19]

The contrast is analytically consequential: administrative capacity, stable property rules and political freedoms need not move together; a proposition that rule by a single corporate-style owner necessarily improves government cannot be inferred from aggregate growth or digital infrastructure. Freedom House is one normative measurement framework whose expert judgments are contestable. Its country reports provide a baseline for studying institutional trade-offs rather than a causal experiment or a demonstration that Singapore or China is governed by Yarvin's preferred constitutional design.
