# Lula and Brazil's 2026 election: political history, US influence in Latin America and the changing global balance of power

The projected re-election of Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva in Brazil's presidential runoff on 25 October 2026 has significance beyond the continuity of one administration. It brings together three long-running conflicts: the ability of a profoundly unequal society to change and renew its government through elections, disagreement over the role of the state in production and social welfare, and the position of a major Latin American economy amid increasingly intense competition between the United States and China. Lula's projected defeat of Flávio Bolsonaro, the political heir of former president Jair Bolsonaro's movement, takes place in a fragmented electorate and alongside a newly elected Congress whose balance of forces is substantially different from that of the presidential contest. [1][2][3]

Datafolha projected Lula's victory on election night. At the moment of its projection, with 86.41% of polling sections counted, Lula held 51.45% of valid votes against Flávio Bolsonaro's 48.55%. Those figures described the provisional count and the basis for a projection, not the fully finalized official result. At 9:26 p.m. Brasília time, the TSE's directly published tally recorded **99.87% of polling sections counted**, with **64,144,599 votes (51.76%) for Lula** and **59,774,442 (48.24%) for Flávio Bolsonaro**. The margin exceeded 4.37 million votes and made the outcome mathematically irreversible given the 658 sections outstanding, even though complete tabulation and subsequent formal procedures remain distinct events. [20] The first-round result already showed the extraordinary competitiveness of the opposition: Flávio Bolsonaro received 47.03% of valid votes on 4 October, ahead of Lula's 45.16%. The reversal in the runoff suggests that turnout and the decisions of voters outside the two leading blocs were consequential; precise attribution requires individual-level or longitudinal evidence rather than an inference from totals alone. [1][2]

A presidential election authorizes an executive government without delivering ownership of the entire political system. The Liberal Party, or PL, elected 121 of the 513 deputies who will take office in 2027, while the Workers' Party–Communist Party–Green Party federation elected 88. Conservative, centrist, regionally anchored and economically liberal factions retain powerful legislative influence. Re-election, governing capacity and political hegemony are therefore distinct outcomes. The durability of Lula's next administration will depend on negotiated laws, credible fiscal choices and the preservation of legitimate opposition as much as on the result of the presidential ballot. [3][4]

## 1. Independence, slavery and the unequal foundations of Brazilian political society

Brazil became independent in 1822 without immediately ending monarchy or slavery. Abolition came only in 1888, shortly before the monarchy fell in 1889. Formal legal changes did not automatically redistribute land, income, education or social power. Formerly enslaved populations and poor rural households entered subsequent decades with severely unequal opportunities to acquire assets and political influence. The consequences became embedded in regional development and the distribution of public services.

The struggle over state power continued through oligarchic republican rule, industrialization, military governments and the democratic Constitution adopted in 1988. Landowners, exporters, industrial groups, professional associations, trade unions and social movements developed organizations able to shape economic policy. In several periods, ambitious reforms took place without broad democratic participation or under explicit repression.

The electoral rise of a former industrial worker in 2002, his return in 2022 and the projected renewal of his mandate in 2026 are historically meaningful against this background. They do not automatically constitute a transformation of the social hierarchy. They do show that leaders formed outside traditional landowning, military and technocratic elites can command repeated national majorities within a competitive democratic framework.

## 2. Vargas, industrial development and the changing functions of government

Getúlio Vargas's era transformed the relationship between industry, labor and the central state. Building a more diversified economy required infrastructure, domestic credit, protected markets and a workforce increasingly concentrated in urban production. Labor legislation and administrative institutions became central to a developmental project that also included authoritarian episodes, restrictions on opposition and extensive state direction.

The historical record challenges a simple opposition between state intervention and private markets. Brazil's industrial expansion depended simultaneously on entrepreneurial investment, foreign technology, public enterprises, regulation and domestic demand. Disagreements concerned who would finance production, which firms received protection, how wages were determined and whether industrial growth improved life for workers as well as owners.

Developmentalism influenced actors beyond the left. Conservative politicians and military governments also promoted industrial capacity and strategic infrastructure. Contemporary arguments about public credit, reindustrialization, oil production and technology policy continue to draw on this inheritance, although the productive structure and global trade environment have changed enormously.

## 3. Brazil's 1964 coup and documented American involvement

The overthrow of João Goulart in 1964 remains central to evaluating the interaction between domestic political conflict and foreign influence. The Library of Congress records that US policymakers sought a pro-American and anti-communist government, worked with Brazilian military allies and prepared Operation Brother Sam to provide logistical assistance to forces attempting to remove Goulart. The additional material assistance proved unnecessary for the coup's completion. [5]

Two forms of historical reduction are misleading. Portraying the military takeover as purely imposed by Washington erases the initiative of Brazilian officers, politicians, business organizations and other domestic participants. Denying American involvement, meanwhile, conflicts with the surviving documentary record. The evidence demonstrates coordination and preparation within a broader alignment of interests.

The dictatorship expanded selected forms of industrial activity and infrastructure while repressing political opponents, suppressing democratic competition and strengthening security institutions. Its economic record included both growth and debt, alongside deeply unequal distributions of opportunity and political voice. These historical experiences continue to influence debates about the military's constitutional role, civil liberties and the risks of relying on external patrons in domestic conflict.

## 4. A history of US hemispheric policy before the Cold War

American influence in Latin America predates the twentieth-century confrontation with socialism. The Monroe Doctrine, announced in 1823, opposed renewed European colonization in the Western Hemisphere. In the early twentieth century, Theodore Roosevelt's corollary interpreted American security interests more expansively and justified interventions in other countries in the region. The US National Archives explicitly traces the doctrine's evolution and its application to places such as Haiti, Nicaragua and the Dominican Republic. [6]

As Washington's economic and military strength grew, the region became connected to American investment, shipping, finance and security planning. Relations were not exclusively coercive. Trade and technology transfers supported selected industries and brought consumers access to goods and services. Yet power was asymmetric. Governments facing dependence on a large export market or lender could have their choices constrained by tariffs, credit conditions and diplomatic pressure.

The history shows why expressions of national sovereignty resonate across Latin America. It also shows why slogans about foreign domination are insufficient as explanations of contemporary events. The necessary analysis identifies the concrete tools by which a stronger state influences another, the participation of local actors and the economic interests that make cooperation or coercion attractive.

## 5. Guatemala, Cuba and Chile in the regional Cold War

The Cold War brought a powerful ideological dimension to American policy. Washington feared Soviet influence and perceived revolutionary movements as possible security threats, while Latin American societies were experiencing real disputes over land, inequality, education and political inclusion. US responses differed across countries, from diplomatic support and economic assistance to covert operations and military collaboration.

The 1954 overthrow of Guatemala's government and the subsequent confrontation with Cuba remain prominent examples of intervention and geopolitical alignment. The Chilean case is supported by substantial declassified documentary evidence: American officials pursued policies intended to obstruct Salvador Allende and exert economic pressure before the September 1973 military coup. The National Security Archive makes primary materials available for examination. [7]

Foreign influence did not abolish domestic agency. Chilean officers, political parties and economic elites made decisions of their own. A credible explanation therefore links international pressure to local institutions and conflicts. The legacy is an enduring regional sensitivity to external security doctrines, especially when economic disputes and political accusations are framed as tests of geopolitical allegiance.

## 6. Democratization, sovereign debt and inflation

Brazil's transition toward civilian democratic rule occurred during a serious economic crisis. The debt shock of the 1980s reduced financing options, while inflation undermined wages, business planning and investment. Political institutions had to expand participation while managing international obligations and rebuilding administrative credibility. The 1988 Constitution strengthened social rights and public responsibilities under difficult fiscal conditions.

Inflation stabilization in 1994 dramatically improved the capacity of ordinary households to hold income in the national currency. The Real Plan relied on institutional design and economic coordination rather than a single political decree. Stabilization was a necessary condition for many later social policies, but it did not eliminate inequality, regional disparities or low productivity.

Elections then increasingly revolved around the combination of macroeconomic credibility, inclusion, reform and development. Brazilian presidents had to deal with creditors, business groups, governors, social organizations and an institutionally fragmented Congress. This environment formed the setting in which the Workers' Party developed from a movement of political opposition into a competitor for national executive office.

## 7. Labor unions and the formation of Lula's political identity

Lula's leadership in the industrial unions of the São Paulo metropolitan region gave him a political background unlike that of presidents produced by military hierarchies or established governing elites. Metalworkers faced conflicts over wages, working conditions and collective bargaining under the final years of military rule. Large factories helped create organized networks through which workers could act together.

The Workers' Party drew support from unions, social movements, intellectuals, neighborhood networks and religious communities engaged in grassroots organizing. Lula learned to negotiate with employers, competing factions and politicians whose interests did not coincide with those of organized labor. This combination of confrontational mobilization and negotiation helped him develop alliances beyond the industrial left.

The economic structure that produced his original following has since changed. Services, informal work, digital platforms and different geographical patterns of manufacturing dominate much of the labor debate. A contemporary labor-oriented presidency must respond to precarious earnings, low productivity and the challenges faced by workers whose relationships with employers do not resemble the factory floor of the 1970s.

## 8. The 2003–2010 presidencies and the economics of inclusion

Lula's first two administrations combined cash-transfer policies, real increases in the minimum wage, expanded education and credit, and continuity in central elements of macroeconomic management. A favorable international commodity cycle raised export earnings and improved the environment for investment and fiscal receipts. Domestic policies also affected consumption and access to services. The growth outcomes cannot be credited entirely to either external prices or presidential decisions.

Bolsa Família became a central part of targeted assistance to low-income households. Expanded higher education and other public programmes aimed to widen opportunity, although public-service quality and geographical access remained unequal. Increased public and state-linked investment likewise produced productive assets while raising questions about governance, procurement and the allocation of public resources.

Internationally, Lula pursued dialogue with emerging economies and sought greater Brazilian visibility in multilateral organizations. This approach did not require abandoning relations with the United States. It attempted to increase bargaining options by diversifying partners. Diplomatic visibility, however, needs to be distinguished from actual investment commitments, new exports or changes in international decision-making authority.

## 9. Dilma Rousseff, recession and coalition breakdown

The transfer of power to Dilma Rousseff initially continued important elements of the governing coalition but took place as economic conditions weakened. Industrial performance, fiscal management, administered prices and credit policy became sources of controversy. A profound recession and falling confidence intensified tensions among firms, workers and political parties. The mass demonstrations of 2013 also signaled widespread frustration with urban services, representation and corruption.

Rousseff's 2016 impeachment was conducted through constitutional procedures, but its political and legal interpretation remains contested. Defenders emphasize fiscal-law violations and the responsibilities of executive leadership. Critics argue that the process was disproportionate and driven by a political coalition that no longer supported the president. The procedural existence of impeachment cannot settle every argument about its substantive legitimacy.

The episode damaged the Workers' Party's reputation among many voters and helped reorganize the opposition. The memory of recession and corruption did not disappear with Lula's return to office. It continues to influence how people evaluate economic policy and the credibility of political promises.

## 10. Corruption investigations, due process and institutional legitimacy

Brazil's Lava Jato investigations exposed serious corruption and relationships between companies and public officials, while generating controversies about prosecutorial conduct, judicial impartiality and political effects. Lula's criminal convictions were subsequently annulled by the Supreme Federal Court, allowing him to contest elections. A procedural annulment is not an assertion that all investigated misconduct was imaginary, and an annulled conviction cannot legitimately be presented as a valid final penal judgment.

The controversies created durable political narratives. Some voters saw the investigations primarily as an attack on entrenched corruption. Others concluded that selective legal processes and political objectives contaminated justice. An accurate historical account must distinguish particular judicial rulings from generalized claims about every court or prosecutor.

Institutional mistrust has become a major political force. Citizens who distrust the judiciary, Congress, parties or the press may interpret unfavorable decisions as proof of bias. That creates a challenge for any elected president: protecting the independence of investigative institutions while demanding transparent procedures and equal protection under law.

## 11. Jair Bolsonaro and the transformation of the Brazilian right

Jair Bolsonaro's presidential victory in 2018 drew on disenchantment with traditional parties, anti-PT sentiment, public-safety anxieties and cultural conservatism. His coalition included religious movements, advocates of economic liberalization, military networks and citizens who believed existing institutions had failed to represent them. These groups did not agree on every question, but shared opposition to important aspects of the Workers' Party project.

Bolsonaro's administration changed political rhetoric and generated major confrontations over environmental policy, public health and the relationship between executive power and independent institutions. Supporters emphasized conservative values, economic reforms and attacks on entrenched political practices; critics emphasized institutional conflicts and disputed policy outcomes. Both approaches need to be examined through actual decisions and measurable consequences.

The emergence of Flávio Bolsonaro as the main conservative presidential candidate in 2026 demonstrates that the movement has organizational resources independent of Jair's personal occupancy of office. Party structures, congressional caucuses, governors and digital networks can sustain its influence following a presidential defeat.

## 12. The razor-thin result of 2022 and democratic stability

The official results of the 2022 presidential runoff gave Lula 60,345,999 votes, or 50.90% of valid votes, against Jair Bolsonaro's 58,206,354, or 49.10%. The margin was only 1.80 percentage points. It demonstrated the legitimacy of Lula's electoral mandate while showing that large portions of the country preferred a different political project. [8]

The attacks on Brazil's institutions on 8 January 2023 sharpened the debate about constitutional stability. The invasion and destruction of governmental facilities were incompatible with peaceful democratic competition, while the resulting investigations and prosecutions also raised questions about evidence, proportionality and due process. Defending democracy requires rejecting violence and preserving legal safeguards.

Lula's 2023–2026 government brought together a coalition broader than the traditional left, symbolized by Geraldo Alckmin's presence as vice president. Yet the unity of an electoral alliance aimed at protecting institutions does not imply agreement over taxation, fiscal priorities, environmental rules or crime. The administration's challenges showed how differences temporarily suppressed during campaigning can reappear in government.

## 13. The first round of 2026 and the resilience of conservative opposition

On 4 October 2026, Flávio Bolsonaro led the presidential first round with 47.03% of valid votes, while Lula received 45.16%, according to Brazil's electoral court. This result prevented the incumbent from treating the campaign as a straightforward reaffirmation of his previous victory. It also confirmed the organizational weight of conservative and anti-PT constituencies after years of polarization. [2]

Second-round votes are not automatically transferred according to party labels. A citizen supporting a third candidate may choose one finalist, abstain or cast a blank or null ballot. Turnout can change because of transport, mobilization, economic conditions or personal circumstances. A shift in the final ranking thus cannot be attributed to a single campaign argument without evidence about individual decisions.

The difference between affirmative support and defensive choice is important. Some Lula voters may support his economic record or welfare policies; others may see him as preferable to the alternative while retaining strong reservations. Similar differences exist among Flávio Bolsonaro's supporters. An electoral majority creates lawful authority to govern but does not remove these disagreements.

## 14. Election night, extended voting hours and procedural confidence

The runoff on 25 October was complicated by reports of transport disruption and adverse weather. Supreme Federal Court Justice Cristiano Zanin ordered the voting period extended to 7 p.m., shortly before the originally scheduled closure. Some polling places had already begun closing procedures, and electoral authorities needed to reconcile operational instructions with the resumed count. These events require careful differentiation between administrative problems, legal disputes and unproven allegations of misconduct. [1][9]

A credible electoral system does not require the absence of complaints. It requires public rules, equally available legal remedies, transparent counting procedures and an evidence-based treatment of allegations. Narrow margins can intensify suspicion, but closeness alone does not demonstrate manipulation or fraud.

Datafolha projected a Lula victory during the count. A projection, a mathematically irreversible result and the formal proclamation are not identical procedural moments. Exact vote shares cited from an election-night snapshot should never be silently presented as the final national total.

## 15. Congress in 2027: a presidential win without parliamentary control

The 2026 Chamber election gave the Liberal Party 121 deputies and the PT–PCdoB–PV federation 88, according to the Senate and Chamber's own published breakdowns. Additional seats went to União Progressista, PSD, Republicanos, MDB and numerous other organizations. The president's core federation thus lacks the votes necessary to pass legislation without negotiations. [3][4]

Coalition formation is not determined exclusively by an ideological scale. Legislators represent productive sectors, religious organizations, territorial constituencies and local political arrangements. They can support a government on one policy and oppose it on another. Budget amendments, committee leadership and administrative posts can facilitate agreements, while also creating risks to transparency and the efficient allocation of public resources.

The Senate will be important for appointments, legislation and political oversight. The leadership of both chambers can influence the agenda and practical capacity of the presidency. The election therefore distributes authority between institutions rather than producing one ideologically uniform national mandate.

## 16. Regional polarization and the geography of political preferences

Brazil's regional voting patterns reflect unequal development, demographic differences, historical parties and diverse economic structures. The Northeast gave Lula a substantial advantage in 2022, while conservative candidates often performed strongly in parts of the South, Southeast and Center-West. Preliminary 2026 results displayed continuing territorial differences, although a definitive map must use the completed official tally. [8][9]

Such differences cannot be reduced to one social label. The Northeast contains industrial centers, large cities, energy projects and highly productive agriculture, not merely beneficiaries of public transfers. The South includes diverse industrial workers, public employees and farms of different sizes. Religious membership and rural residence can correlate with voting without establishing a uniform causal mechanism.

Policies affect people differently because their economic exposures differ. Minimum-wage adjustments can matter greatly to households receiving indexed benefits, while commodity export prices and transport costs can be central to agricultural municipalities. Democratic governing requires engaging those differences without depicting voters in another region as less legitimate citizens.

## 17. Employment, household income and the material basis of electoral loyalty

Economic performance can shape evaluations of a government alongside party loyalty and social identity. Brazil's statistical agency recorded a 5.6% annual unemployment rate in 2025, the lowest in its comparable series beginning in 2012. The World Bank described improvements in lower-income households' income and poverty conditions while continuing to identify pronounced inequality. These changes provide plausible reasons why voters would value continuity in employment and social protection. [10][11]

The aggregate numbers do not fully describe household experience. High interest rates raise consumer-credit costs; food, rent and transport can strain budgets; informal workers may earn volatile incomes. An economy with lower measured unemployment can still leave many people dissatisfied with job quality and purchasing power.

Conservative voters likewise express material concerns, including taxation, government efficiency, personal security and uncertainty about business conditions. A coherent analysis evaluates the real incidence of policies on income and investment. It neither treats every government programme as successful because it was announced nor dismisses economic grievances because they come from political opponents.

## 18. Fiscal sustainability and monetary constraints

Brazil's next administration will govern within debt and financing constraints unaffected by the electoral result. The IMF's July 2026 assessment projected growth of 2.4% that year while emphasizing high debt, rigid expenditure and inflation risks. International reserves and a floating exchange rate remained important buffers against external shocks. [12]

Maintaining social programmes and supporting productive investment requires adequate revenue and credible spending priorities. The economic outcome of additional public expenditure depends on what it finances, how efficiently it is implemented and the stage of the business cycle. Cutting spending across the board can damage services and productive capacity; open-ended spending commitments can increase financing costs and inflation pressure.

The central political challenge is to reconcile social obligations with a sustainable fiscal framework. A credible medium-term arrangement may lower risk premiums and make private and public investment easier. But rules that ignore recession and distributional realities may intensify hardship. These trade-offs will be mediated by Congress, the Central Bank, markets and social constituencies.

## 19. Brazil's strategic position as a major but not dominant power

Brazil's population, agricultural production, mineral resources, industrial capacity and domestic market give it substantial geopolitical weight. However, its ability to influence prices and rules is different from that of a major military superpower or issuer of a globally dominant currency. It plays important roles in food, energy and commodities while depending on foreign technology and capital in several strategic sectors.

Strategic autonomy means preserving meaningful policy choices amid external pressures. It does not require uniform hostility to the United States or an automatic alliance with China. Brazil can cooperate with Washington on trade and research while negotiating independently in BRICS or with European and Asian partners.

The value of diplomacy depends on alternatives backed by real capabilities. Export diversification helps if firms can reach new markets competitively. Domestic production improves autonomy when companies can develop, maintain and replace critical equipment. The ownership of natural resources alone does not guarantee mastery over processing, logistics or industrial technologies.

## 20. American power: finance, technology, culture and security

US influence in Latin America is a product of geographical proximity, corporate investment, military capacity, technological leadership and the international role of the dollar. Historical episodes show that Washington has sometimes promoted cooperation and sometimes intervened to support preferred political arrangements. The Monroe Doctrine, the Roosevelt Corollary and declassified Cold War records are distinct evidence about mechanisms that varied across time. [5][6][7]

It would be inaccurate to infer that Washington determined every Latin American election or that all trade with the United States represents political coercion. Domestic electorates and institutions have agency. External actors can nonetheless change the costs of domestic policies through credit terms, sanctions, market access or diplomatic leverage.

The United States remains a major commercial and technological partner for Brazil. The USTR recorded approximately $94.2 billion in bilateral goods trade in 2025 under its own statistical conventions. US demand is particularly relevant for several industrial activities even though China is a larger overall buyer of many Brazilian commodities. [13]

## 21. The 2026 tariff dispute between Brazil and the United States

In July 2026, the US government announced additional 25% tariffs on selected Brazilian goods under Section 301 of US trade law. The USTR cited concerns about digital trade, electronic payments, intellectual property and other practices. Brazil challenged the measures through consultations at the World Trade Organization. The competing legal positions should be represented separately; initiating a dispute does not establish which side is right. [14][15]

Brazil's Ministry of Development and Trade reported $17.4 billion of goods exports to the United States in the first half of 2026, down 13% from the comparable 2025 period. The US share of Brazilian exports declined from 12.1% to 9.4% across those first-half periods. National and US statistics may use different conventions, so their figures must not be combined uncritically. [16]

Tariffs can reduce exporters' margins, delay contracts and redirect trade. Their incidence also depends on whether American importers have substitutes, how prices respond and how firms reorganize supply chains. Brazil's options include negotiation, dispute settlement and market diversification. Simply promising retaliation or alignment with another power does not resolve the commercial effects on affected firms and workers.

## 22. China as a market and an industrial dependency

China's demand for soybeans, iron ore, petroleum and other Brazilian exports has become integral to the trade relationship. It supports earnings and activity in large productive sectors, but it can also reinforce dependence on raw-material exports if Brazil does not improve processing, technology and industrial productivity. Brazilian trade statistics show China's large share of exports in several recent months. [17]

China is also a supplier of manufactured products, electrical equipment and digital technologies. Imports can lower production costs and accelerate infrastructure deployment while exposing Brazilian industries to intense competition. The effects vary among firms, workers, consumers and regions. Investment projects need to be evaluated through actual ownership, contracts and local added value.

Greater American-Chinese rivalry complicates this relationship. Trading with China does not make Brazil a member of a Chinese military alliance, just as maintaining US relations does not require abandoning BRICS. A sustainable strategy preserves commercial options while resisting demands for unconditional geopolitical alignment.

## 23. BRICS and the limits of multipolar rhetoric

BRICS provides a diplomatic forum for states dissatisfied with aspects of the distribution of international economic influence. It supports discussions about development finance, multilateral representation and changes in settlement arrangements. Its participants nevertheless have different political systems, foreign-policy interests and commercial relationships.

The organization is not a customs union comparable to the European Union or a collective-defense institution. Announcements about using national currencies do not automatically replace the dollar's deep securities markets, extensive payment networks and role in international contracts. Financial architecture changes only when institutions and market participants can operate alternative arrangements at sufficient scale and confidence.

For Brazil, BRICS membership can expand dialogue and financing choices. It does not grant unilateral authority over other members' policies. The most useful measures of success are projects financed, transaction costs reduced, market access increased and bargaining positions improved.

## 24. South American integration and political fragmentation

South America contains markets that could benefit from better energy links, transport corridors and cross-border logistics. Yet integration efforts have repeatedly been hindered by changes in government, conflicting national regulations and insufficient investment. Mercosur remains commercially important while facing divergent priorities among its members.

Lula's previous foreign policy favored regional dialogue, suggesting continuity in that direction under another mandate. But ideological differences among current presidents cannot be dissolved through a summit. Integration creates economic benefits only when transport and customs systems work, contracts are reliable and firms can trade competitively.

Railways, roads, ports, electricity interconnections and payment systems can produce gains even when governments differ politically. Projects should be judged by trade flows, costs, environmental impacts and financial viability rather than by the frequency of declarations endorsing regional unity.

## 25. The Amazon, environmental governance and global leverage

The Amazon basin plays an important role in climate, biodiversity, water cycles and the lives of its inhabitants. Brazilian deforestation policy consequently has effects that reach beyond its borders and attracts international attention. Environmental diplomacy can support financing and scientific cooperation, but domestic land conflicts and economic opportunities must be addressed.

Protection requires distinguishing illegal clearing, lawful agricultural activity, Indigenous territorial rights, mining and transport infrastructure. These activities have different legal and ecological implications. Overseas demands can be constructive when they provide credible support and respect domestic rights; they can generate resentment when they impose costs without local compensation.

The forest's geopolitical significance should not obscure the material needs of residents. Effective policy involves institutions, monitoring, enforcement and sustainable livelihoods. The evidence of success lies in observed forest conditions and economic outcomes rather than promises made at international meetings.

## 26. Energy, food and minerals in the world economy

Brazil is an important producer of grains, animal protein, iron ore, petroleum, biofuels and renewable electricity. Changes in these markets affect trade balances, inflation and food security internationally. Energy and mineral resources can give a country bargaining opportunities, especially during periods of supply disruption.

Yet large export volumes do not imply independent control over international prices. Producers face competition, shipping costs, insurance, credit constraints and demand fluctuations. Capturing more industrial value requires investment in processing and logistics, and firms capable of selling competitive products rather than only raw inputs.

Lula's diplomacy can seek to convert productive resources into strategic relationships, but such relationships depend on long-lived capabilities. A country that exports minerals while importing high-value industrial components may remain vulnerable despite its natural endowment. The balance of power is shaped by whole supply chains.

## 27. Constitutional legitimacy and the rights of the opposition

The projected electoral victory of Lula does not eliminate the claims of millions who supported his opponent. Democratic stability requires continued representation, political speech and lawful competition for those voters. It also requires opposition leaders to accept valid electoral procedures and reject violent attempts to overturn a constitutional outcome.

The presidency operates within limits imposed by Congress, the judiciary, the federation and independent oversight. These institutions can protect against abuses while at times obstructing elected governments. Institutional conflict is not inherently undemocratic. It becomes dangerous when actors refuse common rules or use state power to destroy legitimate competition.

The next term will test whether political rivals can negotiate over the budget, security and economic policy without denying one another's place in democratic life. A narrow majority is a legitimate majority, not a mandate to eliminate dissent. A strong parliamentary opposition has rights and responsibilities, not a justification for permanent institutional sabotage.

## 28. Religion, digital networks and identity polarization

Religious institutions and social communities remain important for political mobilization. Evangelical and Catholic voters are internally diverse, as are workers, farmers and urban professionals. Religious leaders may hold conservative positions on social issues while supporting welfare spending or improved public services.

Digital communication has accelerated the spread of campaign messages and allegations. Platform rankings, influencers and closed groups can strengthen group identity, but precise effects on voting behavior require careful evidence. It would be unjustified to explain an election outcome solely through social media operations or foreign information campaigns without credible causal research.

Polarization becomes self-reinforcing when political leaders derive continuous advantage from treating opponents as threats rather than competitors. Conversely, cooperation can be encouraged by concrete policies that improve livelihoods. Neither result follows mechanically from the size of an online audience.

## 29. Public safety and conservative economic constituencies

Crime and insecurity affect millions of households and influence trust in government. Conservative parties often propose tougher punishment and expanded police authority, while left-leaning groups emphasize prevention, social conditions and legal safeguards. Effective public safety depends on investigation, intelligence, criminal justice, local government and prison management.

Longer sentences alone may have limited effects where investigations fail or organized criminal groups maintain control over illegal markets. Preventive programmes likewise cannot replace operational capacity against violent organizations. Evaluation should distinguish homicide, extortion, trafficking, corruption and public perceptions of insecurity.

Lula's re-election would not eliminate the underlying concerns that help sustain conservative opposition. Addressing them requires measurable reductions in violence and respect for rights. Political actors who ignore these concerns leave a substantial share of public demand unanswered.

## 30. Industrial policy, productivity and national autonomy

Brazil faces low productivity growth alongside international competition in semiconductors, artificial intelligence, electricity systems and defense technologies. Universities and scientific skills are necessary but insufficient for sustained industrial competitiveness. Firms also need predictable regulation, finance, infrastructure and qualified workers.

Industrial policies may coordinate investment where markets underprovide long-term research or essential capabilities. Yet poorly designed subsidies can entrench weak producers. Public procurement, concessional credit and tax preferences should have measurable objectives, transparency and regular evaluation.

Another Lula administration could offer continuity to selected programmes, but cannot determine investment independently of borrowing costs or parliamentary choices. Genuine industrial autonomy requires learning embedded in productive enterprises and the ability to compete in domestic and export markets.

## 31. Three conditional paths for 2027–2030

In a negotiated-governance path, the presidency builds issue-specific coalitions, supports a workable fiscal framework and advances productive investment and service improvements. The country maintains diverse external partnerships, while economic outcomes reduce some of the sources of political dissatisfaction. Lower polarization would arise from better institutions and material conditions rather than a sudden disappearance of ideological differences.

In an institutional-gridlock path, a conservative Congress repeatedly obstructs central executive priorities, credit costs remain high and budget conflicts intensify. Businesses postpone investment amid uncertain policy. Government supporters interpret obstruction as political persecution, while opponents treat every concession as evidence of weakness. The presidency retains legal authority but achieves little structural reform.

In an external-shock path, trade restrictions, energy prices or geopolitical crises damage export markets, prices and the exchange rate. Brazil's ability to absorb the shock depends on reserves, fiscal space, productive diversification and diplomacy. These scenarios describe mechanisms conditional on future choices, not forecasts with invented probabilities.

## 32. Political succession after a fourth presidential term

A projected fourth, nonconsecutive Lula term also introduces a succession problem. Constitutional rules prevent a president re-elected for a consecutive term from immediately running again for a third consecutive term. The governing coalition will need leaders and programmes able to win support independently of Lula's personal biography.

The conservative field has a similar organizational question. Flávio Bolsonaro's strong showing demonstrates continuity of the electoral movement, but future leadership may be contested by governors, legislators and other conservative figures with distinct agendas. A presidential defeat does not automatically dissolve parliamentary power or local political networks.

Peaceful succession is a deeper indicator of democratic maturity than a single electoral triumph. Institutions become durable when losing candidates retain the opportunity to compete and winning governments accept limits. International partners also place value on predictable rules that survive changes in administration.

## 33. Brazil as an intermediate power in a fragmented century

The world's changing distribution of power is more complex than a simple replay of the Cold War. The United States and China possess major concentrations of finance and technology, while the European Union, India, Gulf economies, Russia and other states influence production, security and markets. Brazil participates in several overlapping economic networks rather than belonging exclusively to one geopolitical camp.

Latin American history warns against ignoring asymmetrical power. The United States has been both a source of trade and investment and an external actor in documented interventions; China's expansion has created commercial opportunities and new dependencies. Brazilian diplomacy is strongest when it can choose among credible partnerships without surrendering domestic priorities.

The historical significance of Lula's projected 2026 re-election will ultimately be measured beyond the ballot count: whether Brazil protects political competition while improving productivity, public security, education, social mobility and the resilience of its international position. Without tangible economic progress, discontent can keep reinforcing polarization. Without shared constitutional rules, gains achieved under one government can be threatened by every successive crisis.
