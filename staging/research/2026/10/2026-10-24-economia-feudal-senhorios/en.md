# The economy of feudal lordship: land, peasant obligations, surplus extraction and institutional change

**Information cutoff: 24 October 2026.** Scope: comparative western and central European lordship, approximately the ninth to fifteenth centuries. Feudalism is a disputed analytical label rather than one universal medieval constitution.

## Feudalism is not the same as manorialism

Historians distinguish aristocratic vassalage and fiefs from the organization of agricultural labor in a manor. Fealty and land grants helped coordinate military and political elites; a manor comprised farms, tenants, demesne lands, dues, local jurisdiction and claims on peasant labor. The two institutions interacted but neither uniformly covered all medieval Europe. Oxford's Constance Bouchard cautions that 'feudalism' combines incompatible meanings and may distort the historical record. Rural lordship existed in different forms, and free peasants could hold land in zones where aristocratic vassalage was also present. [1][2]

## The manor as a production and distribution organization

A manor's demesne was land controlled directly by the lord, while tenant holdings supported households that might owe harvest labor, rents and services. Cropland, meadows, woods and water resources were used under local rights that did not map neatly to absolute modern private property. The lord could control mills, ovens, courts and markets, sometimes collecting dues on their use. Production had to reproduce seed, equipment, animals and food before a transferable surplus emerged. Rents imposed in labor, produce or cash affected the choices available to peasants; a harvest failure could increase arrears and reduce food security. [3]

## Serfdom, legal status and negotiation

A serf was not identical to an enslaved person treated as transferable chattel; rights, marriage obligations, inheritance and movement rules differed across communities. Some tenants could buy or negotiate commutation of labor services into rent; others were bound by customary duties and manorial courts. Free peasants lived beside dependent tenants, while landless laborers also worked for wages. A simple population pyramid conceals the variation. Actual obligations require estate records, judicial rolls and local custom rather than applying one general textbook model to every region. [1][3]

## Where economic surplus went

Peasant households retained some output for food and reproduction; lords collected rents, dues and direct demesne products; kings, princes and ecclesiastical institutions sought taxes and tithes; towns bought food and supplied tools. The pressure to extract surplus could deter investment when peasants expected the additional output to be taken. But lower rents could allow consumption and local specialization. Chris Wickham argues that active exchange can coexist with a feudal economic logic quite unlike capitalist profit reinvestment. The central mechanism is competition over who receives peasant produce and tax payments, not absence of markets. [2][4]

## The role of violence, courts and legitimate authority

A landowner's control over dues often depended on local courts and the possibility of coercion. Military protection and justice were sometimes presented as the counterpart of peasant services, but the distribution was not automatically fair or reciprocal. Peasants might petition, bargain collectively, evade demands or revolt. Some manorial rules limited arbitrary claims, while other jurisdictions enabled excessive extraction. The economic effects depend on property security and predictable obligations, not merely the formal title of the lord. Where multiple authorities competed, households might face overlapping claims. [3][5]

## Commons, forests, mills and collective resources

Agricultural production was not confined to privately enclosed fields. Common grazing, wood gathering and shared water access helped households feed animals and withstand shocks. Rights could be layered and seasonal: a person might rent a plot, have grazing privileges and owe a share of harvest to a separate authority. A lord's enclosure or increased monopoly toll could shift income from commoners to the estate even without greater physical production. Understanding these transfers requires village bylaws and usage evidence. Modern assumptions about absolute ownership can misrepresent complex medieval rights. [3][5]

## Cash rents, towns and commercial exchange

A feudal economy did not necessarily prohibit commerce or currency. Lords demanded some dues in cash, especially when buying military equipment or urban luxuries. Peasants then had to sell products, take wage work or borrow to acquire coin. Markets could grow because extraction increased cash needs. Wickham shows that local exchange might be extensive without following the same accumulation logic as capitalism. Treating every market transaction as proof that feudal relations disappeared would conceal continuing lordly jurisdiction and asymmetric property rights. [2][4]

## Crisis, the Black Death and divergent paths

The Black Death of the 1340s radically changed labor scarcity across Europe. In parts of western Europe, surviving workers negotiated higher wages and commutation of obligations, while some lords attempted legal controls to hold down wages. Eastern and central regions followed different institutional trajectories, including more coercive rural labor in later centuries. Jedwab, Johnson and Koyama document the regional variation in wages and long-run incomes. No 'single date of the end of feudalism' can explain these divergent outcomes. [6]

## Modern comparisons: rents, institutions and limits of analogy

The feudal manor and a contemporary corporation both organize assets and payments, but legal status, political coercion and market entry conditions differ profoundly. A modern rental agreement is not equivalent to hereditary servile obligations; a medieval lord's court was not merely a landlord-tenant contract. The useful comparison is a distributional question: when one agent controls land, processing equipment or access to exchange, how much of additional output goes to the producer versus the controller? Answers require measurement of rents, obligations, crop yields and legal alternatives. [2][4]

| Position or institution | Productive role | Claim on agricultural output | Legal constraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tenant household | Cultivation and livestock | Food and marketable surplus | Labor dues, cash rent or customary service |
| Demesne | Directly managed estate production | Output allocated to lord | Labor requirements and ecological variability |
| Lord and manor court | Property claims, local administration | Rents, dues and certain fees | Custom, conflict and competing authorities |
| Church and prince | Religious and political institutions | Tithes and taxation | Jurisdiction and uneven compliance |
| Town merchant | Tools, exchange and transport | Trading margin | Toll regimes, credit and market entry |
