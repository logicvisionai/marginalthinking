# Global Macro — October business surveys widen the gap between output, purchasing power and financing costs

**24 October 2026 — Weekend edition. Information cutoff: Friday 23 October's flash PMIs, US Treasury par-yield curve, British retail data, University of Michigan consumer survey and Brazilian IPCA-15. Saturday has no regular equity-market close. Flash PMIs are preliminary diffusion measures, not GDP growth rates.**

The new information is a **simultaneous divergence in October business activity**, not another restatement of September inflation. The US flash composite PMI held at **57.6**, albeit below September's 58.4, while the euro-area index slowed from **53.1 to 51.9**. The UK accelerated to 53.1, Japan remained at 52.3 and India rose to 56.7. These levels do not rank countries by GDP growth: they describe different samples of firms reporting changes in activity. [1][2][3][4][10]

The cross-asset tension is that US businesses still report vigorous output, hiring and order books while selling-price pressure and supplier delays are intensifying. European businesses report slower orders and weak hiring alongside energy-related costs. The US Treasury's indicative **2-, 10- and 30-year yields closed Friday at 4.69%, 5.00% and 5.33%**, each four basis points above Thursday. That is evidence of a nominal yield change, not proof that inflation expectations, real rates or term premia individually caused it. [1][2][6]

## Business surveys show a divided global cycle

US output expansion remained historically rapid in October despite the composite's decline of 0.8 index point. The S&P Global survey reports stronger backlogs, long delivery times and new orders, including precautionary purchasing ahead of possible shortages. Employment growth was particularly strong in manufacturing. Its model-based interpretation of activity as consistent with roughly 5% annualized GDP growth is a **survey inference**, not an official quarterly national-accounts release. If inventory accumulation is substituting for final demand, future orders could weaken even while current production remains high. The survey's selling-price measure reached its strongest pace since August 2022; the magnitude is a diffusion signal, not the CPI inflation rate. [1]

The euro area's preliminary composite declined 1.2 points, but **51.9 remains above the 50 threshold**. S&P Global associates some weakness with France, where businesses reported disruption amid strikes, unrest and political uncertainty, while noting softer growth elsewhere. This is attributed survey evidence, not independent verification of the extent of every protest or its causal contribution to GDP. High selling-price pressure and close-to-stalled hiring complicate the ECB's choices: a policy response to imported inflation could weaken domestic demand already losing momentum. French sovereign refinancing is an additional potential transmission channel, but no synchronized French-German spread series was verified for this edition. [2]

The UK's flash composite instead rose from **52.0 to 53.1**, with manufacturing and services both improving. Official retail volume data show **September down 0.4% month on month**, after a revised **0.7% August gain**, while third-quarter volumes were **0.7% above the second quarter**. A monthly fall does not erase quarterly growth; October firms' expectations cannot be used to overwrite September household purchases. The divergence warrants monitoring whether higher business output converts into real consumer demand as fuel and borrowing costs rise. [3][5]

Japan's flash composite remained **52.3**, with services catching up to manufacturing but softer hiring and confidence. Input costs accelerated more than selling prices, implying possible margin compression rather than automatic pass-through. India's composite increased from **55.9 to 56.7** as services and export orders strengthened, while employment growth eased and input-price inflation moderated. India's figures are taken from contemporary reporting of the HSBC/S&P Global survey; the original full release was not independently retrieved, a limitation that matters for granular subindex claims. [4][10]

## Treasury yields rise across maturities without a new slope signal

The US Treasury's **23 October** nominal constant-maturity par yields were **4.69% at two years, 5.00% at ten years and 5.33% at thirty years**. On **22 October** the corresponding observations were **4.65%, 4.96% and 5.29%**. The 10-year minus 2-year spread stayed at **31 basis points**; the 30-year minus 10-year spread stayed at **33 basis points**. These are fitted indicative bid-side yields observed near 3:30 p.m. Eastern, not transaction prices for a particular security. [6]

A parallel four-basis-point rise cannot identify its own cause. To distinguish a real-rate shock from inflation compensation, one needs matched-maturity inflation-protected yields and breakevens; to identify funding stress, one needs auction demand, dealer balance sheets, credit spreads and market depth. If real yields lead, long-duration equities, smaller firms and refinancing-sensitive borrowers face tighter discount rates. If inflation compensation leads, currency credibility and import-cost exposure become more important. No same-time, verified cross-market credit/VIX/MOVE series was obtained; treating missing observations as stable would be misleading.

## Household affordability diverges from corporate momentum

The University of Michigan's final October consumer sentiment index registered **48.0**, compared with **48.1 in September** and **53.6 a year earlier**. The 0.1-point monthly move is **not statistically meaningful** against the survey's stated 4.8-point threshold at 95% confidence. Its reported **18% fall in durable-goods buying conditions**, linked by the survey director to higher financing costs and affordability, is more informative. Consumer sentiment is an attitude measure, not realized household spending. [7]

A strong corporate PMI and weak durable-goods buying conditions can coexist. Capital expenditure, technology investment, backlogs and higher nominal selling prices can sustain company revenues while households postpone cars, appliances and housing. Stock-index strength concentrated in a few large firms therefore says little about median real income. The UK's September retail-volume decline reinforces the need to separate output surveys from consumption accounts, rather than treating them as mutually exclusive evidence.

Brazil's **October IPCA-15 rose 0.32% month on month**, down from **0.70% in September**, with **4.62% over twelve months**. Its sampling window and geography differ from the full IPCA. Domestic monthly disinflation does not remove potential oil and FX pass-through, and Friday's currency or equity move cannot by itself establish structural capital inflows. Brazil belongs in the global comparison through import costs, real rates and fiscal expectations, not as an obligatory centre of the narrative. [8]

## Capital is selective, not uniformly risk-seeking

LSEG Lipper data reported for the week ending **21 October** recorded **$5.07 billion of net subscriptions to US equity funds**, down from **$13.37 billion** a week earlier. Underneath the total, **large-cap funds received $8.63 billion** and **small-cap funds lost $4.24 billion**; **bond funds lost $969 million** and **money-market funds gained $14.82 billion**. These are reported flows for a defined fund universe, not the entire world portfolio, and not a proprietary allocation by any particular asset manager. [11]

The distinction between **flows, market valuation and ownership** is essential. The numbers indicate simultaneous preference for large liquid equity exposure and cash-like buffers, not a universal rotation into risk. S&P Global's separate October investment-manager survey found stronger technology-sector preference alongside a neutral short-horizon overall return view. That is a **survey of expressed views**, not verified holdings; a single survey and a weekly flow release do not establish broad institutional consensus. Both sources are useful precisely because they reveal selectivity and limitations. [9][11]

Friday's reported oil-price rise, linked in market commentary to renewed Middle East supply-route fears, is a potential inflation-transmission channel, not verified evidence of an equivalent physical loss of barrels. A risk premium, actual disrupted throughput and delivered import prices are separate observations. Threats or claims by armed actors must be attributed; future military operations are scenarios, not facts. The EIA October outlook is a forecast vintage, not Friday's observed price. A sustained disruption could raise freight, fuel and production costs and reduce importers' real purchasing power with lags. [12]

```flow
Strong US output and backlogs → supply and freight constraints → attempted price pass-through → inflation expectations and nominal financing costs
Slower euro-area orders plus reported French disruption → weaker revenue and tax base → sovereign refinancing exposure → private credit sensitivity
Oil-route risk premium → potential delivered fuel and freight costs → household real income and operating margins → different monetary-policy reactions
```

## Dislocations, counterevidence and the next test

Three asymmetries matter for research rather than trade instructions. First, strong US output and weak household durable-goods affordability can coexist for months; weaker final sales, growing finished inventories and declining real consumption would challenge the earnings optimism, while broad-based real wage and spending gains would weaken that concern. Second, Europe's combination of softer orders and elevated prices limits the room for painless monetary tightening, particularly where sovereign refinancing costs and domestic political disruption intersect; without verified matched sovereign spreads, its precise market dislocation remains unquantified. Third, India shows easing surveyed cost pressure alongside faster activity while the US shows the reverse; differences in currencies, hedging, liquidity and market access prevent a simple relative-asset conclusion. [1][2][7][10]

Two systemic risks follow. A prolonged physical energy-route interruption could turn a price premium into measurable import costs and consumer inflation; and higher real funding costs could suppress smaller firms and households before headline PMIs or large-company earnings weaken. Neither is proven by Friday's price moves. The contrary evidence is substantial: UK and Indian output accelerated, US orders remained firm and the euro-area PMI still signalled expansion.

The reader learns today that the October activity surveys **diverge across regions and across corporate and household balance sheets**, while the US yield curve moved upward without a new slope signal. The next discriminating evidence is **final PMI orders and selling prices, matched nominal/real yields and credit spreads, physical energy throughput, and real household purchases**. This is institutional analysis, not personalized investment advice.
