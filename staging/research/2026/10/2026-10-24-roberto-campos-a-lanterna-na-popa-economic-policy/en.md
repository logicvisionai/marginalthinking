# Roberto Campos: A Lanterna na Popa and the contradictions of Brazilian development

Roberto de Oliveira Campos (1917–2001) participated in almost the full institutional arc of postwar Brazilian economic history: foreign economic diplomacy, Bretton Woods, the National Economic Development Bank (BNDE), President Juscelino Kubitschek's Targets Plan, embassies, the Government Economic Action Programme (PAEG), monetary and tax reforms, legislative politics, and a later defence of economic liberalization. His career defies a simple division between the developmental state and the free market. He helped build planning institutions and state-directed credit before becoming an influential critic of interventionism. This evolution must be read in the context of the governments he served, especially the dictatorship established in 1964. [1][2][3]

*A Lanterna na Popa* (*The Lantern at the Stern*), published in 1994, is indispensable testimony but also an act of retrospective self-interpretation. The metaphor of a lamp turned backward suggests experience revisited in the light of positions adopted later in life. Memoirs are useful for reconstructing motives, personal relationships and internal disagreement, but do not replace statutes, policy files and economic series. The diplomat, BNDE executive, military-regime minister and liberal legislator must be compared with the later autobiographer. [1][4]

## Cuiabá, seminaries, humanities and entry into the foreign service

Campos was born in Cuiabá on 17 April 1917, the son of Valdomiro and Honorina de Oliveira Campos. His early life unfolded in a Mato Grosso geographically and economically distant from the main industrial centres of Rio de Janeiro and São Paulo. He studied in Catholic seminaries at Guaxupé and Belo Horizonte, trained in theology and philosophy, and briefly taught school. He entered Brazil's foreign service through an examination in 1939, during the centralizing Estado Novo dictatorship. The FGV CPDOC biographical record documents this formative sequence. [1]

The humanities contributed to his characteristic intellectual style: sustained argument, historical allusion, irony and hostility to doctrines he regarded as closed systems. They did not predetermine a coherent liberal economics. His diplomatic career exposed him to balance-of-payments problems, import requirements, international lending and investment projects that could not be solved through doctrinal opposition between public and private ownership. [1]

## Washington, world war and the Bretton Woods system

In 1942 he was transferred to Washington and began studying economics at George Washington University. He participated in economic diplomatic contacts associated with the 1944 Bretton Woods conference, where the IMF and International Bank for Reconstruction and Development originated. These institutions emerged from an attempt to avoid a repetition of the monetary fragmentation of the 1930s and facilitate postwar reconstruction and finance. Their structure also reflected the predominant influence of the United States and the dollar. [1][2]

Brazil's position was distinctive. Its exports remained heavily concentrated in primary products, while industrialization required imported machinery and foreign exchange. Negotiating external credit meant preparing projects, identifying repayment flows and estimating the trade earnings required to service debt. The combination of national planning and international financing in Campos's early career was more pragmatic than the later rhetoric of state withdrawal might suggest: foreign creditors expected guarantees, and government agencies decided which investments deserved them.

## The Joint Brazil–United States Commission and project finance

In the early 1950s Campos worked with the Joint Brazil–United States Economic Development Commission. Its projects addressed transport, energy and other constraints on industrial capacity. Physical infrastructure required financial engineering, coherence between imported machinery and available foreign currency, and clarity about who would pay foreign debt. Selecting a railway, power station or port was also a political allocation of resources among regions and firms. [1][3]

Approved external credit, loan disbursements, completed infrastructure and social productivity are not interchangeable outcomes. Some investments relied on public institutions because individual firms lacked access to sufficiently long-term capital. Building professional project-analysis capacity and development banking was itself part of Campos's institutional contribution, notwithstanding his later opposition to entrenched state privileges.

## The BNDE: scarce capital, long maturities and public choices

Campos served in BNDE management and eventually became its president in the late 1950s. Established in 1952, the bank provided a channel for long-maturity investments not readily supported by Brazil's short-term private lending structure. State intermediation made it possible to finance infrastructure and industry, but administrative selection introduced risks of political capture, forecasting errors and opaque subsidies. [1][2]

The return from directed finance depends on what was funded. Productive power and transport systems may reduce costs economy-wide; poorly selected projects may absorb scarce savings without comparable benefit. An economic biography must therefore distinguish loan maturity, public guarantees, inflation adjustment, foreign-exchange exposure, social spillovers and company profitability. Campos embodied the tension between building financial planning institutions and later opposing excessive public business privileges.

## Kubitschek's Targets Plan: industrial capacity amid imbalance

Under President Juscelino Kubitschek, Campos worked with the Development Council and the Targets Plan. Energy, transport, basic industry and automotive manufacturing were complementary objectives. Factories needed roads, steel, electricity, imported equipment and suppliers; the development programme coordinated these investments through fiscal and exchange incentives, public works and foreign firms. It was not merely a list of construction projects, but an attempt to sequence interdependent capacities. [1][3]

Industrial growth coexisted with inflation, fiscal deficits and external constraints. Public expenditure was partly financed through mechanisms that increased monetary pressures, in a system lacking modern inflation-control institutions. Industrial gains and the purchasing-power costs of inflation affected different social groups. Campos later attacked fiscal indiscipline, but his earlier role belonged to an administration that preferred rapid capacity building under substantial financing constraints. His memoirs interpret this tension through a later ideological lens.

## A Lanterna na Popa as evidence and retrospective argument

Published by Topbooks in 1994, *A Lanterna na Popa* combines an account of childhood and diplomatic service with essays on economic disputes, portraits of heads of government and recollections of private conversations. The Brazilian Academy of Letters dates the work and identifies figures such as Kubitschek, Castello Branco, João Goulart and Jânio Quadros among its subjects. It provides testimony from a participant in decision-making settings rarely visible to ordinary observers. [2][4]

The memoir requires independent corroboration. A participant may reconstruct actions as reasonable responses to the alternatives he remembers and portray opponents as blinded by dogma. Recollections can compress separate meetings, attach later outcomes to earlier intentions and omit conflicting evidence. Comparing the memoir with FGV records, original statutes and contemporaneous economic research is more informative than either uncritical admiration or blanket dismissal.

## The 1961–1964 crisis and the rupture of constitutional government

Brazil entered the early 1960s with high inflation, balance-of-payments stress, distributive conflict and growing political polarization. As ambassador to Washington from 1961 to 1964, Campos negotiated and assessed external credit amid debates over government economic policy. The 1964 military coup removed President João Goulart and opened an authoritarian regime that later deepened censorship, repression and political exclusion. Campos accepted the planning portfolio under President Castello Branco, an important political choice that cannot be dissolved into administrative expertise. [1][5]

The PAEG took shape under a government that had not emerged from a competitive presidential election. Reforms can create durable financial institutions, yet their procedure and allocation of costs remain part of the historical evaluation. Suppressing organizations and opposition changes wage bargaining, distribution and access to justice. Later growth does not eliminate the political responsibility associated with those constraints.

## PAEG: inflation control and institutional reconstruction

The Government Economic Action Programme of 1964–1967 placed Campos at Planning alongside Finance Minister Otávio Gouveia de Bulhões. It aimed to slow inflation, improve fiscal financing, reform taxes and banking, and restore the conditions for investment. Disinflation was not merely a matter of reducing the money supply: it involved sovereign debt instruments, public accounts, regulated prices and wage-indexation formulas. Research from Ipea describes the reforms as changes to the economy's institutional and supply-side conditions. [5][6]

Timing matters. Financial changes in 1964–1967 may have enabled credit expansion and investment later, but growth in 1968–1973 also reflected policies under subsequent military governments, world economic conditions and Brazilian industrial dynamics. Crediting the entire later boom to Campos mistakes chronological precedence for exclusive causation. Ignoring institutional effects because they arose under dictatorship would be a different error. Distribution and democratic legitimacy need to be evaluated alongside productive outcomes.

## Central Bank, Monetary Council and financial reform

Law 4,595 of 31 December 1964 created the National Monetary Council and the Central Bank of Brazil. Before the reform, monetary responsibilities had been distributed among institutions including Banco do Brasil and the Superintendência da Moeda e do Crédito. The reform created or consolidated means of managing reserves, rediscount facilities, compulsory deposits and bank supervision. The central bank began operations in 1965; its statutory creation should not be confused with the far later adoption of modern institutional autonomy. [7][8]

Operational independence matters because governments may pressure central banks to finance deficits or hold credit artificially cheap. Yet formal rules alone do not ensure independence in practice. Campos later commented on the difference between a statute's design and the institution's subsequent operation. The financial reform must be evaluated through lending, inflation, sovereign borrowing and regulatory capacity rather than treated as self-validating merely because the central bank still exists.

## ORTN, monetary correction and the ambiguity of indexation

Law 4,357 of July 1964 authorized inflation-adjustable Treasury obligations, the ORTN. Such instruments can maintain real principal values during persistent inflation, making savers more willing to lend to the government instead of holding goods or foreign currency. But if automatic adjustment spreads across prices, wages and contracts, it may also perpetuate inflationary expectations and make later disinflation more difficult. [9]

The trade-off is between protecting financial contracts against inflation and preserving the economy's ability to reduce inflation. ORTN did not by itself cause Brazil's subsequent hyperinflation, but it contributed to a larger indexed-finance architecture whose effects would outlive its original designers. Longer-term outcomes depended on fiscal policy, exchange rates, external shocks and the behaviour of prices.

## FGTS: labour mobility, funded protection and lost tenure

Law 5,107 of 13 September 1966 created the severance guarantee fund, FGTS. Its initial legal design offered a funded account alternative to the established employment-tenure provisions of the Consolidation of Labour Laws, affecting dismissal costs, employer liabilities and the financing of housing. The original law provided an option under specified terms; subsequent legislation changed the coverage and design of the scheme. Parliamentary records and the official FGTS historical chronology allow the successive legal arrangements to be distinguished. [10][11]

The policy creates opposing incentives. Predictable deposits can reduce employer uncertainty and allow labour mobility; workers gain an earmarked financial asset but lose some of the job security associated with the previous tenure regime. Power asymmetry is especially important when collective bargaining is constrained by authoritarian rule. FGTS cannot be classified unconditionally as either emancipation or deprivation without comparing wages, turnover, coverage, access to balances and workers' ability to find another job.

## Wage policy and the social distribution of disinflation

PAEG wage-setting formulas limited the transmission of past inflation to nominal wages. When actual inflation exceeded anticipated rates embedded in adjustment rules, purchasing power could fall, particularly among employees unable to renegotiate compensation. Stabilization costs were therefore distributed unevenly between wage earners, asset owners and groups able to reprice goods or contracts rapidly. [5][6]

The distributional mechanism is not an ancillary issue. Workers meet current consumption needs from wages, while some financial claimants obtained indexed instruments protecting principal. A multi-year fall in inflation may coexist with severe short-run losses for some households. Under restrictions on unions and democratic opposition, the bargaining counterfactual was not readily available. PAEG's economic and political legacy must reflect those differences.

## Taxation, social insurance and administrative state capacity

The period also restructured taxes and social insurance. The National Social Insurance Institute (INPS) was created in 1966, merging earlier pension institutes, while fiscal changes sought to improve revenue collection and rationalize intergovernmental competences. These reforms increased the state's ability to administer economy-wide policy, even as centralized authority could concentrate decisions in offices with limited democratic accountability. [12]

A commitment to private markets need not logically exclude a state capable of collecting revenue, managing monetary stability and financing core infrastructure. The tension appears when the same figure helps expand the state's coordinating role and later treats public intervention primarily as an obstacle. Campos's trajectory shows that the design and accountability of institutions matter more than an abstract argument about public-sector size.

## The 1968–1973 boom and disputed credit for its origins

Brazil grew rapidly between 1968 and 1973 under military rule. FGV's historical atlas associates that performance partly with earlier PAEG reforms and partly with later economic policy, favourable international conditions and renewed domestic demand. In *A Nova Economia Brasileira* (1974), Campos and Mário Henrique Simonsen argued that stabilization measures had laid groundwork for subsequent acceleration. The interpretation was made by participants in the debate and needs to be compared with independent historical evidence. [6][13]

GDP growth does not automatically demonstrate better income distribution, democratic rights or sustainable external finance. Industrial investment, foreign borrowing, rural migration, regional inequality and real wages moved through different mechanisms. One should distinguish genuine productive gains from the authoritarian institutional setting in which they occurred, without assigning the whole boom to one earlier minister or converting macroeconomic success into political absolution.

## London, the Senate and the Chamber: explicit economic liberalism

Campos later served as Brazil's ambassador in London, became senator for Mato Grosso in 1983–1991 and subsequently federal deputy for Rio de Janeiro through the 1990s. These legislative years overlapped the democratic transition, persistent inflation, sovereign-debt troubles and renewed debate over state-owned enterprises. He advocated privatization, deregulation, price stability and broader competition. [1][2]

The contrast was historical rather than merely psychological. In the 1950s scarce finance and missing infrastructure encouraged public coordination; in the 1980s and 1990s debt, inflation and some inefficient public monopolies made planning institutions targets of criticism. Global policy change and Brazil's own experience shaped Campos's later outlook. His autobiography presents continuity in retrospect, though the actual career contained contingent choices in changing institutions.

## The 1988 Constitution and competing ideas of economic citizenship

The constitutional assembly of 1987–1988 coincided with the restoration of representative democracy and disputes about social rights, taxation, property and development. Campos opposed provisions he regarded as overly interventionist or expensive. The resulting constitution enlarged democratic and social protections after two decades of authoritarian rule. Disagreement about financing public services does not erase the constitutional transition's importance in restoring civilian control and political rights. [1][2]

Campos's liberal argument emphasized the possibility that fiscal complexity and protected monopolies could inhibit investment and competition. Other political traditions held that universal services and labour rights address unequal market access and social vulnerability. Neither side is settled by counting taxes or public enterprises. The relevant evidence concerns service coverage, quality, productivity, revenue capacity and distributive consequences.

## A writer and polemicist in Brazilian economic debate

Campos published technical and political writing across decades: *Economia, Planejamento e Nacionalismo* (1963), *A Moeda, o Governo e o Tempo* (1964), *A Técnica e o Riso* (1967), *O Século Esquisito* (1990) and *A Lanterna na Popa* (1994). The Brazilian Academy of Letters' bibliography documents the sequence. His wit helped transmit economic arguments beyond specialist audiences, though memorable aphorisms could also simplify genuine conflicts among price stability, industrial planning and distribution. [4]

The autobiographical voice presents an observer alert to Brazilian inconsistency and his own policy disputes. Literary talent does not turn memoir into administrative evidence, just as criticism of his ideology does not erase his institutional role. Campos's public influence followed from his ability to describe economic problems in accessible terms while participating directly in organizations central to their management.

## The contradiction of defending liberty from within an authoritarian regime

Campos served as minister in a government installed through a coup. PAEG policy was made in conditions where the opposition and organized labour lacked the contestatory resources of a pluralist democracy. Later arguments for economic liberty therefore coexist with an earlier role in an authoritarian state. Economic analysis cannot remove that history without narrowing liberty to the freedom of selected market actors. [1][5]

There is an important difference between freedom to conduct business and freedom to participate in binding public decisions. Financial and tax institutions created under a dictatorship can later operate legitimately under democratic law, but their origin and early allocation of costs remain historical facts. Good economic results do not make political coercion legitimate, and criticizing coercion does not require denying productive outcomes.

## Campos's economic-historical place

Campos helped construct development-credit mechanisms, designed or defended monetary and labour reforms, and became one of Brazil's best-known critics of the entrepreneurial state. His life tracks a transition from major infrastructure gaps to a diversified, indebted and inflation-prone economy. He did not create BNDE, the Targets Plan or PAEG single-handedly: governments, economists, legislators, international institutions, businesses and workers shaped each programme. [1][5][6]

*A Lanterna na Popa* preserves the recollections of an official exceptionally well placed to witness Brazilian state-making. Its value rises when the memoir is read against the documents identifying who bore policy costs, how benefits were distributed and which institutions outlived their architects. The tension between planning and liberalization is not simply a personal inconsistency; it reflects the dilemmas of a peripheral economy that needed capital, stable money, international competitiveness and broader social rights simultaneously.
