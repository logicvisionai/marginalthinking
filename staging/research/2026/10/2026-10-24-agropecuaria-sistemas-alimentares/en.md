# Agriculture and livestock in Brazil and the world: productivity, trade and food security

**Information cutoff: 24 October 2026.** Distinguish observed FAOSTAT 2024 statistics, FAO food insecurity estimates for 2025, and early Conab forecasts for 2026/27.

## Agricultural size has several incompatible meanings

A country may be the largest producer of a particular crop by tonnage without leading in agricultural value added, exports, nutritional diversity or yields. A comparison that adds sugarcane tonnes to meat and grain weights cannot measure food energy or farmer income. China and India have very large domestic consumption and diverse production; the United States combines high-productivity crops and livestock with export capacity; Brazil is prominent in soybeans, maize, sugar and meats; Southeast Asia supplies rice and palm oil. The European Union is a multi-country bloc. Rankings require a specified crop, year, unit and territory. [1]

## Completed world production records through 2024

FAO's series reports approximately 3.1 billion tonnes of world cereals, 374 million tonnes of meat, 985 million tonnes of milk and 100 million tonnes of eggs in 2024. The total harvested area of principal crops reached around 1.5 billion hectares. These outputs are physical measurements of different goods, not additive equivalents of calories or household welfare. Yield increases reflect breeding, fertilizers, technology, irrigation and skills alongside land expansion. Greater yield may conserve land per unit produced, yet may induce expansion when prices and land law encourage clearing. [1]

## Brazilian agronomic transformation and its costs

Embrapa was created in 1973 and helped develop tropical-soil research, adapted seeds, livestock health and extension of engineering knowledge. Cerrado soil correction, mechanization and two successive annual harvests in suitable regions supported the growth of maize and soybeans. Research is one explanation among global prices, private capital, transport and credit. Embrapa itself notes concentrated rural wealth, damaged pastures, water inefficiency and agrochemical risks, contradicting any simple portrayal of cost-free progress. [2]

## What Conab's October 2026 figures actually say

The first Conab 2026/27 survey, released 15 October 2026, projected 367.1 million tonnes of grains and 85.4 million hectares sown, including 181.6 million tonnes of soybeans and roughly 148 million of maize. These are forecasts, not harvested volumes. Conab's preceding-season 2025/26 September estimate was 361.7 million tonnes. Soybeans and maize feed livestock, biodiesel, oil, ethanol and exports, so the gross grain aggregate is not equal to direct human food supply. Rice and beans have different domestic-demand profiles. [3][4]

## Livestock, plant production and competing demands

Poultry, pigs and cattle vary in land, grain, water and feed conversion. Ruminants can transform forage and residues that humans cannot eat directly, while feedlot production also demands grain that has alternative uses. Animal disease, food-safety controls and veterinary access can halt trade regardless of aggregate farm output. Farm size alone does not determine sustainability or contribution to nutrition. Large holdings may benefit from capital-intensive machinery; smaller producers may specialize in diverse perishables. Land rights, technical advice and market access shape productivity and income. [1][2]

## Food availability is not affordability

SOFI 2026 estimated 645 million people experienced hunger in 2025, equivalent to 7.8% of the world's population; 2.1 billion faced moderate or severe food insecurity and around 2.7 billion could not afford a healthy diet. These are overlapping but distinct indicators, not independent populations to add together. Grain warehouses cannot feed those with insufficient purchasing power unless distribution and income allow access. Nutrition also depends on dietary variety, sanitation and health. Exporting large volumes does not establish domestic food security. [5][6]

## Climate, logistics, credit and the next decade

Farm production requires fertile soil, seed, fertilizers, rainfall or irrigation, storage, transport, credit and stable trade rules. Drought, conflict, shipping disruptions, disease and currency depreciation can make a crop scarce or expensive after it was produced. Fertilizer imports and energy costs affect planting decisions long before harvest receipts. Climate adaptation requires local measurement of soil water and farm practices; the IPCC identifies substantial land-climate interactions. A meaningful food-security policy tracks retail food prices relative to wages, healthy-diet affordability, grain inventories, water, crop yields, sanitation and verified forest conversion. [7]

## Comparisons across agricultural systems must respect the denominator

Agronomic yield is output per hectare harvested, labor productivity is output per worker-hour and value added is the value of finished farm goods less purchased intermediates. A very large country can be the leading producer in total tonnes while having modest yield, and a smaller country can have high productivity without exporting much. Crop and livestock statistics are also expressed in different units. International comparisons should therefore report the year, crop and denominator before making a ranking. FAOSTAT provides comparable country and commodity definitions but historical revisions remain possible. [1]

Large domestic markets can reduce export shares even where absolute production is high. Conversely, concentrated exporters are vulnerable to overseas sanitary rules, shipping disruption and currency changes. China, India, the US, Brazil and the EU serve different combinations of household markets and international supply. Regional profiles matter more than a false league table adding rice, beef, sugarcane and milk as if their weight measured equivalent nutrition.

## Fertilizers, soils and the biological production function

Crop output depends on plant genetics, soil structure, nutrient supply, water, temperature and crop-management decisions. Nitrogen fertilizer relies heavily on energy inputs, phosphorus and potassium on mineral deposits and transport. A higher input price changes how much fertilizer farmers apply, affecting future yields and farm margins. Soil acidity correction and adapted cultivars helped make parts of Brazil's Cerrado productive, but poorly managed fertilizer and pesticides impose runoff and biodiversity costs. Technology choice should be evaluated through output, input costs and measured effects on water and soil, not yield alone. [2][7]

Farms face different access to credit, machinery, technical assistance and irrigation. A drought-resistant variety may improve reliability rather than peak-year yield. This distinction matters for food security: lower variance in harvests can be socially valuable even without record output. Soil conservation and cover crops may require short-run spending to preserve medium-term productivity; landowners and short-term tenants can have different incentives.

## Rural credit and working-capital risk

Planting requires payments months before sale: seed, fertilizer, diesel, workers, rent and insurance are financed from retained cash or loans. When interest rises, the crop's breakeven selling price also rises; a record harvest can lower market prices and squeeze producer cash flow. Storage rights provide an option to delay selling after harvest, but warehouses require capital and energy. Small producers without facilities may sell immediately to meet debt obligations. [3][4]

Public credit subsidies should be evaluated according to additional investment achieved, incidence across farm sizes, defaults and environmental safeguards. A program that expands output but benefits only owners of highly appreciated land may not improve nutrition or social welfare proportionately. Insurance can spread weather shocks but cannot replace measures of soil and water exposure or remove correlated national drought.

## Logistics, biosecurity and trade exposure

A farm is only one node in the food chain. Drying, refrigeration, slaughter inspection, storage, railways, waterways, trucks and ports determine how much arrives usable and at what price. Animal disease outbreaks can halt exports, lower local farm-gate prices and increase costs of containment, while consumers may experience different retail responses. The value of a logistics project should be assessed through observed loss reduction, freight prices, consumer affordability and regional market access, not only larger handling capacity.

Agricultural policy must also consider geopolitics. Imported fertilizer and energy products expose Brazil and other countries to supplier concentration, freight disruption, foreign exchange and sanctions. Long-term contracts and diversified inputs can reduce vulnerability, but may cost more in tranquil conditions. The relevant trade-off is expected continuity of supply versus current cost, with public transparency about who bears the risk.

## Agricultural labor and the distribution of value

High-capacity combine harvesters raise crop output per worker while changing demand for machine operators, technicians and seasonal labor. Family agriculture can supply labor-intensive fruits and vegetables, whereas large grain operations may gain economies from mechanization. Neither organizational form automatically achieves better environmental or social outcomes. Equal access to land records, extension and finance affects the ability to adopt technology. A national export surplus can coexist with rural households suffering precarious employment and inadequate income. [2]

International comparisons should track farm household income, rural wages, land tenure security, health and access to infrastructure. A production-focused policy can raise total supply without changing how land rents, transport margins and retailer profits are distributed. Economic assessment needs to ask who receives each increment of income.

## Environmental and dietary outcomes through 2035

Climate shocks and water scarcity affect yields, disease and supply stability. Some irrigation investments can reduce variability, but they also require electricity and can stress aquifers. Integrated crop-livestock-forestry practices may recover soil and pasture in specific Brazilian contexts, yet the net benefit must be measured against capital cost and credible counterfactual land use. The OECD–FAO Outlook is a scenario exercise, not a guarantee that all households will afford food in 2035. [7]

A monitoring set should include real healthy-diet cost relative to wages, cereal stocks-to-use, child nutrition, soil recovery, verified forest conversion, water availability, production per hectare and per worker, livestock disease and credit defaults. If repeated record crops coexist with unaffordable food and poor nutrition, the purely tonnage-based strategy is inadequate. If redistribution raises purchasing power but reduces farm incentives and logistical capacity, supply may deteriorate. Both production and equitable, reliable access are necessary.
