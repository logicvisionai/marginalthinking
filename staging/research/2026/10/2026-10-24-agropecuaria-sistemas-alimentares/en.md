# Agriculture and livestock in Brazil and the world: productivity, trade and food security

**Information cutoff: 24 October 2026.** Distinguish observed FAOSTAT 2024 statistics, FAO food insecurity estimates for 2025, and early Conab forecasts for 2026/27.

## Agricultural size has several incompatible meanings

A country may be the largest producer of a particular crop by tonnage without leading in agricultural value added, exports, nutritional diversity or yields. A comparison that adds sugarcane tonnes to meat and grain weights cannot measure food energy or farmer income. China and India have very large domestic consumption and diverse production; the United States combines high-productivity crops and livestock with export capacity; Brazil is prominent in soybeans, maize, sugar and meats; Southeast Asia supplies rice and palm oil. The European Union is a multi-country bloc. Rankings require a specified crop, year, unit and territory. [1]

## Completed world production records through 2024

FAO's series reports approximately 3.1 billion tonnes of world cereals, 374 million tonnes of meat, 985 million tonnes of milk and 100 million tonnes of eggs in 2024. The total harvested area of principal crops reached around 1.5 billion hectares. These outputs are physical measurements of different goods, not additive equivalents of calories or household welfare. Yield increases reflect breeding, fertilizers, technology, irrigation and skills alongside land expansion. Greater yield may conserve land per unit produced, yet may induce expansion when prices and land law encourage clearing. [1]

## Brazilian agronomic transformation and its costs

Embrapa was created in 1973 and helped develop tropical-soil research, adapted seeds, livestock health and extension of engineering knowledge. Cerrado soil correction, mechanization and two successive annual harvests in suitable regions supported the growth of maize and soybeans. Research is one explanation among global prices, private capital, transport and credit. Embrapa itself notes concentrated rural wealth, damaged pastures, water inefficiency and agrochemical risks, contradicting any simple portrayal of cost-free progress. [2]

## What Conab's October 2026 figures actually say

The first Conab 2026/27 survey, released 15 October 2026, projected 367.1 million tonnes of grains and 85.4 million hectares sown, including 181.6 million tonnes of soybeans and roughly 148 million of maize. These are forecasts, not harvested volumes. Conab's preceding-season 2025/26 September estimate was 361.7 million tonnes. Soybeans and maize feed livestock, biodiesel, oil, ethanol and exports, so the gross grain aggregate is not equal to direct human food supply. Rice and beans have different domestic-demand profiles. [3][4]

## Livestock, plant production and competing demands

Poultry, pigs and cattle vary in land, grain, water and feed conversion. Ruminants can transform forage and residues that humans cannot eat directly, while feedlot production also demands grain that has alternative uses. Animal disease, food-safety controls and veterinary access can halt trade regardless of aggregate farm output. Farm size alone does not determine sustainability or contribution to nutrition. Large holdings may benefit from capital-intensive machinery; smaller producers may specialize in diverse perishables. Land rights, technical advice and market access shape productivity and income. [1][2]

## Food availability is not affordability

SOFI 2026 estimated 645 million people experienced hunger in 2025, equivalent to 7.8% of the world's population; 2.1 billion faced moderate or severe food insecurity and around 2.7 billion could not afford a healthy diet. These are overlapping but distinct indicators, not independent populations to add together. Grain warehouses cannot feed those with insufficient purchasing power unless distribution and income allow access. Nutrition also depends on dietary variety, sanitation and health. Exporting large volumes does not establish domestic food security. [5][6]

## Climate, logistics, credit and the next decade

Farm production requires fertile soil, seed, fertilizers, rainfall or irrigation, storage, transport, credit and stable trade rules. Drought, conflict, shipping disruptions, disease and currency depreciation can make a crop scarce or expensive after it was produced. Fertilizer imports and energy costs affect planting decisions long before harvest receipts. Climate adaptation requires local measurement of soil water and farm practices; the IPCC identifies substantial land-climate interactions. A meaningful food-security policy tracks retail food prices relative to wages, healthy-diet affordability, grain inventories, water, crop yields, sanitation and verified forest conversion. [7]
