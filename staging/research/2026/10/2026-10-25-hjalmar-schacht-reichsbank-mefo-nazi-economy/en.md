# Hjalmar Schacht: from currency stabilization to financing Nazi rearmament

Horace Greeley Hjalmar Schacht (1877–1970) exercised substantial monetary power under radically different German political regimes. In 1923 he helped coordinate stabilization during the final phase of the Weimar hyperinflation, initially as currency commissioner and then as president of the Reichsbank. After Adolf Hitler came to power, Schacht returned as central-bank president in 1933 and became Reich economics minister in 1934, designing credit arrangements that financed rearmament, concealed public obligations and concentrated foreign exchange on strategic imports. His financial competence acquired political meaning through the beneficiaries and purposes chosen for it. [1][2][3]

The International Military Tribunal at Nuremberg acknowledged his central role in military financing but acquitted him of the crimes alleged because a legally required inference about his participation in planning aggressive war was not established beyond reasonable doubt. That decision is not a finding that rearmament was harmless or that his political choices lacked historical consequences. Later disagreements with Nazi leaders must be reconstructed without erasing his earlier cooperation. [4][5]

## Tinglev, German banking and the young economist

Born on 22 January 1877 in Tinglev, then German territory and now part of Denmark, Schacht grew up in a merchant family that experienced financial insecurity. He studied several disciplines and completed a doctorate at Kiel in 1899 on the theoretical content of English mercantilism. He worked in trade associations and subsequently at Dresdner Bank, reaching deputy management in 1908. His professional world combined financial markets, export business, institutions and political connections long before the Nazis appeared. [1][6]

The experience gave him knowledge of credit, exchange, corporate relations and government borrowing. German universal banks contributed to industrial finance and international trade. Their leadership positions created contact with executives, ministries and foreign creditors. Treating Schacht solely as an appendage of Hitler misses decades of independent career development; treating banking as politically neutral misses the uses to which those capabilities were later put.

## World War I debt and the breakdown of the paper mark

Germany financed much of World War I by borrowing rather than imposing taxes sufficient to cover war spending. Defeat in 1918 left liabilities, reparations disputes and severe social and political conflict. The Ruhr occupation and state-funded passive resistance aggravated financial imbalances in 1923, while monetary emission destroyed the mark's function as stable unit of account and store of value. Bundesbank histories explain how paper-mark depreciation almost eliminated many financial assets and liabilities. [7]

The distributional effects were uneven. Wage earners received money losing purchasing power rapidly, holders of fixed-value savings were ruined, and certain debtors or owners of real assets were protected to different degrees. Commerce struggled when reliable prices, contracts and working-capital calculations became impossible. Stabilization required both a credible unit of account and the political capacity to restrict monetization of government deficits.

## Rentenmark 1923: institutional cooperation rather than a miracle by one man

Schacht was appointed currency commissioner in November 1923 during the introduction of the Rentenmark, an arrangement prepared by multiple officials and supported by controls on note issuance and a system of pledged economic assets. After Reichsbank president Rudolf Havenstein died, Schacht secured the presidency in December. Contemporary texts and later German institutional scholarship credit him with an important role without making him sole creator of the reform. [1][8]

The ratio of one stabilized mark to one trillion old paper marks expresses the collapse of the nominal unit. It did not restore the purchasing power of people whose old savings had already been destroyed. Ending hyperinflation required control of emissions, budget adjustment and credible monetary administration. Schacht's subsequent public reputation benefited from the success of a coordinated reform whose private losses were distributed very differently across society. [7]

## Dawes, reparations and the return of international lending

The 1924 Dawes Plan reorganized Germany's reparations timetable and facilitated external finance. Schacht participated as a negotiator and developed international relationships that strengthened his standing. Foreign loans could help rebuild infrastructure and corporate liquidity but exposed Germany to sudden reversals of dollar and other cross-border funding. A financial system can appear stable while relying on future foreign-currency earnings to repay principal and interest. [1][9]

The distinction between productive borrowing and external dependence turns on currency, maturity and cash-flow timing. Expansion financed in liquid markets becomes fragile when creditors retreat. The 1929 crisis brought those vulnerabilities into view. Schacht's criticism of reparations therefore had genuine economic components, but also helped draw him into increasingly nationalist attacks on the Weimar political order.

## The Young Plan, resignation and Schacht's shift to the right

Schacht opposed the Young Plan proposals for reparations settlement at the end of the 1920s and resigned from the Reichsbank presidency in 1930. He had co-founded the liberal German Democratic Party after World War I, but left it in 1926 and moved closer to nationalist and conservative-right movements. The historical records show political choices beyond his central banking duties. [1][9]

Reparations, foreign debt and domestic austerity mattered in the collapse of support for parliamentary government. Yet economic grievances did not make the Nazi seizure of power an inevitable response. Various parties offered different policies; Schacht chose to associate with those attacking the republic's legitimacy. Calling him a permanent non-partisan technocrat would conceal this crucial interval.

## Harzburg, business networks and the political ascent of Hitler

The Harzburg Front of October 1931 assembled radical conservatives, nationalists and Nazis in an explicitly anti-parliamentary movement. The German Historical Museum records Schacht among prominent participants. He also became an important interlocutor linking industrial and financial circles to Hitler's campaign for political power. His lack of formal membership in the Nazi Party did not amount to neutrality toward Hitler's appointment as chancellor. [10][11]

Established elites sometimes expected to exploit mass radical politics to defend industry against communism and union pressure, believing they could constrain the new leadership. The catastrophic failure of those assumptions does not absolve the decision to support an anti-democratic movement. Political responsibility attaches to acts undertaken when alternatives were still contested, not only to what the supporter later said about the consequences.

## Back to the Reichsbank: unemployment policy and military demand

Hitler reappointed Schacht president of the Reichsbank in March 1933 amid unemployment and depression. Public spending, works programmes and increasing military orders helped revive activity as Germany also intensified political repression. Schacht brought the prestige of the 1923 currency stabilization to Nazi economic policy and provided access to financing arrangements. His appointment gave industrialists and lenders grounds to regard the regime as financially manageable. [1][2]

Employment recovery cannot be attributed to one official or interpreted without changes in conscription, coercion, labour statistics and the business cycle. A factory manufacturing equipment for civilian transport and the same factory producing arms may raise measured industrial output while creating radically different outcomes for welfare and peace. The destination of credit was therefore integral to the regime's economic significance.

## MEFO bills: the legal and accounting architecture of hidden rearmament

Schacht's MEFO arrangement used bills issued by armaments suppliers and accepted through a nominal private company, *Metallurgische Forschungsgesellschaft*. The Reich ultimately guaranteed the claims, and banks could rediscount them with the Reichsbank. This allowed manufacturers to receive finance while major military obligations were kept outside transparent budget lines and ordinary central-bank disclosure. The Nuremberg prosecution recorded approximately 12 billion Reichsmarks of MEFO bills issued before the scheme ended for new instruments in April 1938. [12][13]

A bill is a promise to pay; it does not create iron ore, labour or machinery. Financing makes orders and production possible by rearranging claims on future fiscal resources. If spending outruns the capacity to supply inputs, inflation or shortages follow. Price controls and rationing may conceal rather than remove those pressures. The scheme materially supported military expansion and reduced public scrutiny of its costs.

## Maturity, rolling debt and the central bank's contingent liability

MEFO was neither costless money nor a collection of harmless private invoices. The bills had maturities, could be rolled over and were backed by the public sector. As long as financial institutions held them, the Treasury could delay direct settlement. When holders sought payment or central-bank rediscounting, the Reichsbank had to absorb claims or the government had to finance redemption through taxes and conventional borrowing. [12][14]

At Nuremberg in May 1946, Schacht argued that the scheme was intended to be repaid from later tax receipts and blamed Hitler for directing those resources into continued armament. Prosecutors questioned how a programme on that scale could be divorced from the military expansion it had enabled. The defendant's explanation is testimony, not an independent sustainability audit. The crucial historical balance-sheet question is the relation between government cash flows, the cost of maturing liabilities and the real goods financed.

## The New Plan: foreign exchange, controlled imports and autarkic ambitions

As economics minister from August 1934, Schacht introduced the New Plan, tightening foreign-exchange control and prioritizing imports needed for industrial and military production. Germany faced shortages of dollars and other hard currencies but required oil, rubber, ores and machinery inputs. Administrative allocation of foreign exchange favoured designated industries and restricted alternative uses. German documentary history describes the resulting state control of external trade. [2][15]

Controls did not create foreign currency or free raw materials. They assigned scarcity according to state priorities, influencing which firms could operate and what households could consume. Export schemes, blocked accounts and bilaterally negotiated settlements allowed activity to continue without immediate payment in convertible currency. The apparent stability of the financial system concealed opportunity costs: resources diverted to armaments were unavailable for civilian housing, medical services and consumption.

## Bilateral clearing and unequal commercial bargaining

A bilateral clearing agreement balances purchases and sales between two countries through bookkeeping and special accounts, reducing immediate hard-currency settlement. It can enable trade when markets lack currency liquidity. It can also constrain weaker partners if exporters receive credits usable mainly for buying goods from a dominant industrial country. Pricing, terms and the power to delay settlement shape the distribution of benefits. [15]

Such agreements cannot be judged as identical across all countries. Their significance under the Nazi regime came from the priority of securing materials required for rearmament and strengthening state control over trade. Payment systems are instruments whose political purpose depends on how they are designed and enforced.

## War-economy authority, Göring and the Four-Year Plan

Schacht received additional authority over war-economy preparations in 1935. His attention to raw materials and industrial mobilization was directly connected to military strategy. Hermann Göring's Four-Year Plan from 1936 increasingly prioritized synthetic substitutes, autarky and rearmament regardless of the financial constraints Schacht emphasized. His influence on economic policy subsequently declined. [2][4]

Their disagreement was not a simple conflict between Nazism and democracy. Schacht had already defended and financed rearmament; he increasingly resisted its pace and certain costly industrial investments. Fiscal caution amid competition for authority did not undo his earlier contribution or imply rejection of military expansion in principle.

## Resignation, the 1939 Reichsbank memorandum and lost independence

Schacht left the economics ministry in 1937 but stayed on in a ministerial position without portfolio and remained president of the Reichsbank until January 1939. A memorandum dated 7 January 1939 urged curbs on spending and inflationary finance. Bundesbank historical commentary credits Wilhelm Vocke with drafting the board's letter and notes that Hitler dismissed the bank's directors. It was an institutional action, not the solitary signature of Schacht. [4][16]

The dispute exposed the frailty of central-bank independence under authoritarian rule. A technical authority can warn of debt and inflation but cannot compel a government to accept limits when political leaders control appointments, legal changes and sanctions. The same bank that warned against financing had helped deliver enormous credit to military industry. Both stages belong to its history.

## War, opposition contacts and the 1944 arrest

By the outbreak of World War II Schacht no longer held command of the Reichsbank. He lost his remaining nominal ministerial office in 1943. After the failed attempt on Hitler's life in July 1944, Schacht's contacts with conservative opponents contributed to his detention, including imprisonment in concentration camps. The German Historical Museum distinguishes those contacts from direct involvement in the assassination attempt itself. [1][6]

His subsequent persecution does not provide retrospective immunity for earlier choices. An individual may support an authoritarian regime for years and later clash with it over finance or leadership. Responsibility for creating its military capacity is analytically separate from responsibility for issuing specific later orders. The difference prevents both unfounded allegations and apologetic erasure.

## Nuremberg: legal acquittal and historical responsibility

The International Military Tribunal considered Schacht's role under charges involving conspiracy and crimes against peace. It concluded that his involvement in German rearmament was central but the evidence did not prove beyond reasonable doubt that he knowingly participated in the specific plan to wage aggressive war required for conviction. He was therefore acquitted in 1946. That finding is narrower than a general moral declaration of innocence. [4][5]

German denazification proceedings then produced a separate judgment, including an initial sentence to eight years in a labour camp, followed by reversal and release in 1948. Criminal liability, the applicable legal standard and the historian's explanation of causes are distinct. A judgment may fail to establish a required criminal mental state while documentary evidence still demonstrates that an official funded armaments expansion on a large scale. [3][6]

## Postwar memoirs, the Düsseldorf bank and foreign advice

After release, Schacht published accounts attempting to explain his actions, including *Abrechnung mit Hitler* (1948) and *76 Jahre meines Lebens* (1953). He offered economic advice in Egypt, India, Indonesia, Pakistan and Syria, and founded a foreign-trade banking enterprise in Düsseldorf in 1953. Institutional biographies document these engagements and his death in Munich in 1970. [1][6][17]

Memoir-writing was intertwined with the need for legal and public self-defence after the Nazi defeat. Personal accounts may clarify beliefs and internal conflicts but must be compared with budget records, financial documents and independent testimony. Foreign advisory work likewise proves a government consulted him, not that every recommendation was adopted or successful.

## Monetary competence under democracy and dictatorship

Schacht's record defeats the assumption that financial techniques have an inherent moral purpose. Stabilization in 1923 helped restore a unit of account after ruinous inflation. After 1933, credit bills, public guarantees and import controls helped allocate factories and resources to the military expansion of a totalitarian government. The techniques involved finance and state institutions in both periods; the political objectives, beneficiaries and human consequences differed profoundly. [7][12][15]

Military procurement may reduce unemployment and raise measured industrial output without improving sustainable civilian welfare. Steel, labour, research and energy used to produce weapons are not simultaneously available for consumer products or housing. War destroys further human and physical capital. Employment statistics detached from coercion and the composition of output cannot establish that Nazi economic policy was a replicable model of prosperity.

## Schacht, Campos and Delfim: comparing instruments without equating regimes

Schacht's experience has a limited methodological parallel with Brazilian debates involving Roberto Campos and Antônio Delfim Netto. Monetary control, sovereign borrowing, external credit and import priorities pose related accounting and economic problems. But indexed debt under Brazil's military regime, industrial-development lending and Nazi rearmament had different purposes, coercive structures and consequences. Similar financial instruments do not create a common ideology or equal political responsibility. [7][15]

The shared analytical task is to separate budget expenditure from contingent liabilities, financial claims from actual productive resources, and a stable currency from a just political order. Schacht offers an extreme warning about the danger of central banks financing military projects without effective political restraint.

## The historical verdict: stabilization, complicity and constrained dissent

Schacht did play an important role in the successful stabilization of 1923. He also materially financed and organized Nazi military expansion until the leadership's accelerating demands exceeded what he was prepared to support. Nuremberg limited a specific criminal accusation, while historical records establish the actual financing mechanisms. Neither the acquittal nor his later arrest can cancel earlier political collaboration. [2][4][12]

A precise account follows institutions, decisions, credit flows, maturities, resource constraints and the people affected. It rejects both the image of a merely innocent monetary genius and the unsupported assertion that he directed all crimes of the Nazi regime. Schacht's career illustrates that considerable economic ingenuity may be mobilized either to repair a monetary order or to endanger international peace, depending on the government it serves.
