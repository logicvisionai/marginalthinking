# The political economy of lordship and manors: land, rent, coercion and the historical limits of feudalism

**24 October 2026 · Historical economic research; cutoff: 24 October 2026.**

Feudalism is a contested umbrella for institutions requiring separation: military patronage among elites, manorial production, peasant legal status and jurisdictional extraction. This study traces output, rent and bargaining without imposing one textbook hierarchy everywhere in Europe. [1][2][3]

## Definitions and units

| Concept | Operational definition | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Fief | Grant or holding among elites in specified cases | Not necessarily a manor |
| Manor | Agricultural estate and sometimes local jurisdiction | Different tenant statuses |
| Corvée | Days of compelled service | Not physical units of grain |
| Rent | Output, money or services owed | Burden varies with prices |
| Commons | Resources governed under customary access | Not necessarily open access |

## Why fief, manor, seigneurie and serfdom cannot be synonyms

A fief might be a grant in a relationship between elites; a manor was a local agricultural and sometimes judicial organisation; seigneurial power involved the right to claim payments or exercise authority; serfdom concerned legal constraints upon a person's status. These sometimes intersected but could not be reduced to one pyramid. Bloch's broad description of dependencies differs from Ganshof's narrower legal-military feudalism; Reynolds and subsequent scholarship questioned the extent of a standard fief-vassal contract before later historians systematised it. [1][2]

Confusing these categories produces false economic claims. A free tenant could work land belonging to a lord; a knight granted revenues might have no direct control over a tenant's daily farming; common pastures might be governed collectively rather than owned outright by the castle. Land ownership, military duties, court jurisdiction and personal freedom are different legal variables.

Every case should specify period, local terms and surviving charters. A general statement that 'all medieval Europeans were serfs of kings' is demonstrably too broad. Historical models are useful only when their assumed rights correspond to institutions documented in a named territory.

## Demesne and tenant plots as a two-level agricultural structure

A manor could distinguish the lord's demesne from plots cultivated by households holding customary tenures, together with access to grazing or woodland. Tenants' obligations varied: workdays, grain, poultry, money and fees. The number of demesne acres alone does not reveal output available to the lord, because much labour and knowledge came from tenant households. Nor does holding inherited rights to cultivate imply full modern private ownership. [3][4][5]

The tenant's household faces a production and time-allocation problem. Work on the lord's estate competes with weeding, sowing and harvesting its own land at the season when time is most valuable. A fixed number of corvée days can have sharply different opportunity costs in dry or rainy seasons. Lords need enforcement and oversight to obtain service from people who do not necessarily retain the product.

A governance account must record the bundle of rights, rather than call a serf's work 'free labour' because no cash changes hands. Forced time is a real economic input; its market opportunity cost can exceed the cost the lord sees in the estate ledger. This discrepancy makes historical productivity comparisons difficult without pricing obligations explicitly.

## Corvée, rent in kind and cash dues: alternative sharing of risk

The economic effect of a due depends on its form. Fixed grain payments shift crop-risk burden toward tenants; proportional rents share part of yield variation; fixed money payments make the ability to pay sensitive to market grain prices; corvée duties require time rather than a physical unit. Entry fees and inheritance charges can further influence household investment. [3][4]

The explicitly hypothetical 100-unit chart divides a harvest into seed and losses (20), household consumption (40), rent and dues (25), and trade or reserves (15). It is not an observed medieval European budget; additional corvée days are deliberately excluded, because days cannot be simply added to grain units. This distinction is essential when computing extraction rates in historical datasets.

The strength of a claim about exploitation depends on what rights a family held, what the court could enforce and what happened during harvest failures. A landlord might formally demand more than the harvest surplus and negotiate remission under pressure. Treating all manors as extracting a fixed 25 percent would be an invented statistic; the hypothetical chart teaches an accounting mechanism only.

## Manors exchanged goods and were not universally self-sufficient

Many rural estates produced food and basic materials, but tenants attended markets and purchased salt, iron, fabric and services. Landlords sold grain, wool and livestock while procuring prestige goods and imported inputs. The intensity varied with roads, towns, and access rights. A manorial institution is not necessarily isolated from trade; merchants and seigneurial dues could coexist. [4][6][9]

Markets could also change extraction. Lords who needed cash might commute labour duties into money rent or sell larger shares of produce. Such arrangements gave tenants different schedules and incentives while exposing them to price changes. If grain prices fell, a family might need to sell more output to pay an unchanged nominal rent. The spread of currency did not automatically eliminate dependency.

A self-sufficiency claim would require proving a household or manor exchanged nothing with outsiders, a much stronger proposition than saying most calories were produced locally. Historical accounts are full of gradations. The distinction matters when comparing the transition toward specialised commercial agriculture.

## Common pasture, woodlands and collective limits on extraction

Common grazing, water and woodland often had customary rights regulating how much households could take and under what conditions. A pasture governed by enforceable rules is not the same as open access without restraint. Some institutions prevented overgrazing; others privileged households with cattle or influence in village councils. Enclosure and exclusive control redistributed resources, often changing bargaining power. [3][5]

Common lands mattered to subsistence and agricultural productivity: grazing supports draft animals, woodland provides fuel and building material, and water enables irrigation or milling. A farm's productive potential cannot be understood from its private plots alone. Losing access to commons could make a formally unchanged cash rent far harder to pay.

Policy analogies must respect historical differences. A present conservation scheme may also allocate collective resource rights, but medieval enforcement occurred under unequal status and lordly jurisdictions. To evaluate welfare, examine tenants' access, ecological regeneration, animal holdings and ability to challenge decisions, not a slogan about 'tragedy of the commons'.

## Mills, ovens, courts and seigneurial monopolies

Some lords claimed compulsory use of mills, ovens or markets, levying fees that could convert efficient infrastructure into a stable monopoly revenue stream. Building a mill may lower work needed to process grain; compulsory access and pricing determine who captures the savings. Jurisdictional claims over courts added fines and legal advantages. These rights are not uniformly present in all locations and must be identified in local records. [1][5]

A peasant seeking to use another mill might incur fines where a lord held exclusive rights. That reduces competition and alters technical incentives. The net social effect combines output gains from centralised equipment with transfer payments and the costs of monitoring and coercion. A fee's existence does not show all revenue was profit; owners paid construction, maintenance and workers.

The common economic structure is a fixed facility paired with privileged access rights. A modern public utility may display similar technical scale economies, but judicial rights and freedom to choose were not those of contemporary regulated systems. A rigorous analogy specifies precisely which mechanism is comparable and which legal legitimacy is not.

## Military vassalage versus agrarian surplus extraction

Fief and vassalage often concern service and support among elites, including military equipment and obligations, rather than direct rules for every tenant on the land. Granting revenue to a knight might create a claim ultimately funded by peasant production, but the link through law and intermediaries varied greatly. Reconstructing a strict king-noble-knight-serf ladder for all Europe is unsupported by the varied documentary record. [1][2]

The economic question is who funds military duties and whose rights are subordinated to those obligations. A lord may obtain revenue to maintain warriors, but peasants may owe taxes to another institution simultaneously. Such layering creates conflict and bargaining over multiple claims to one harvest.

This is the reason to distinguish the narrow feudovassalic institution from the manor. A scholar interested in military patronage tracks oaths and grants; one interested in food, rent and labour must examine villages and estate books. They are connected but not identical units of analysis.

## Work incentives and the economic cost of coercion

Under fixed rents, tenants can sometimes retain the gain from better harvesting methods. Under labour dues, extra output from work on the lord's demesne may accrue primarily to the lord. Monitoring, compulsion and legal restrictions change marginal effort and welfare. But these are theoretical mechanisms; no universal ranking of productivity between all medieval serfs and tenants follows without estate-level evidence. [3][4]

A landlord's ledger can understate the cost of forcing people to work. Time unavailable for household land, the inability to seek higher wages and legal risks borne by tenants represent economic and human costs. A family might invest less in tools or nutrition if rights are insecure; that reduces long-run productivity while protecting a lord's immediate revenue.

Outside labour demand changes bargaining. A nearby town seeking workers may attract tenants or pressure lords to lower dues; local courts may instead enforce movement restrictions. Legal freedom, market access and demography must be modeled together. Mere circulation of more coins does not explain the persistence or disappearance of coercion.

```flow
Land, seed, animals and work → harvest → subsistence and surplus → rents and markets
Customary rights and courts → work duties and tolls → enforcement and bargaining → distribution of gains
Plague, hunger and urban demand → relative wage shifts → negotiation or restriction → regionally diverse results
```

## Famine, debt and the distribution of survival resources

Repeated crop losses can drain seed stocks, animal herds and household savings. A tenant owes dues even when climate shocks leave little marketable surplus, unless custom, negotiation or relief changes the payment. Borrowing food or selling livestock can solve today's shortage but weaken tomorrow's capacity. Such dynamics make the manor's accounts subject to multi-year risks rather than one harvest. [4][5]

Food availability and access differ. A landlord can hold grain reserves while tenants lack the rights or purchasing power to obtain them. Market trade may ease scarcity if roads remain open, yet tolls, warfare and deficient income can block distribution. Historical famine is therefore a joint ecological and institutional event, not necessarily unavoidable aggregate lack of grain.

The relevant microdata are prices, crop yields, dues, inventories, debt claims and actual household consumption. A claim that one social order was 'efficient because it guaranteed protection' would need proof of relief and survival relative to realistic alternatives. No model should convert the suffering of dependent workers into a mere reduction in rent collection.

## The Black Death, wage bargaining and early erosion of English servility

The Black Death reduced labour supply and disrupted estate operations in much of Europe. Mark Bailey's reconstruction of English records identifies substantial retreat of personal serfdom already in the **1350s and 1360s**, revising accounts which located most change later. Scarcer workers could demand better terms, move or negotiate money rents, yet states and lords also tried to restrict wages and mobility. [3][7]

The economic comparative static is straightforward: lower labour supply relative to land tends to raise marginal value of work, but legal power mediates whether workers obtain higher pay. A lord can switch from labour services to paid leases when supervision and coercion become costly. The actual chronology varies by village and the kind of right at stake.

The plague was mass mortality and a major destruction of well-being, not a desirable policy shock. Some survivors earning more is compatible with smaller population, damaged output and lost families. To explain long-term labour institutions, researchers must combine wages, tenancy clauses, demographic estimates and court decisions.

## Commutation into cash and the uncertainty of gradual transitions

Moving from labour service and in-kind duties to cash rents can let tenants schedule work more autonomously while giving landlords liquid revenue to pay taxes and purchase goods. The economic effect depends on whether amounts are fixed, prices fluctuate and tenants can sell crops at fair markets. A nominal cash rent is not necessarily less burdensome than an in-kind claim. [4][6]

Commercialisation also connects households to a wider monetary system, with risks from falling crop prices, currency changes, costly market access and debt. Some landlords reimposed personal restrictions or raised dues when commodity markets made extraction profitable. There was no uniform transition from coerced barter to free monetary capitalism.

A sound statistical study would compare real dues, mandated working days, grain prices and legal mobility over time for named estates. Historical accounts using only the rising share of cash payments may mistake a change in the instrument of extraction for a change in its intensity. A household's welfare depends on the whole bundle of rights and resources.

## East–west divergence and the role of political power

Parts of western Europe experienced erosion of personal dependency after medieval demographic shocks; some eastern European agrarian regions subsequently strengthened constraints on workers. Research on European divergence stresses that commodity demand, political elite strength, towns and state formation changed outcomes. The same rise in potential wage bargaining can be overridden where elites retain power to bind labour. [7][8]

Trade and freedom are not synonymous. If landlords produce grain for export, a stronger external price can increase the private incentive to compel service. Conversely, multiple employers and enforceable exit rights may let tenants retain more productivity gains. The effect depends on legal constraints and bargaining, not a universal "eastern culture" or "western destiny".

A comparison must qualify time and place. Countries' modern wealth cannot be inferred mechanically from one medieval tenure form because industrialisation, empire, war, education and policy subsequently transformed the institutions. The valuable historical proposition is conditional: factor scarcity changes incentives, while power determines who can act.

## Agrarian capitalism and the future of institutional comparison

The transition to agrarian wage work, secure tenancies and commercial specialisation was gradual, contested and regionally uneven. Some landowners consolidated estates; other communities preserved rights over commons; towns offered alternatives while landlords and governments changed labour laws. A single date of "the end of feudalism" is not an empirically meaningful turning point for every region. [3][4][9]

The relevant welfare record includes real earnings, workdays, land access, rents, health and legal status, not simply the size of the lord's harvest. A technically improved estate could produce more while tenants consumed less if obligations rose. Equally, a change to fixed rent could broaden investment incentives even without new machinery. The origin of modern economic institutions lies in such interacting changes.

For the comparative research agenda, reconstruct a named estate's bundles of rights and trace changes before and after shocks, then compare with towns or regions facing different constraints. Do not equate a modern worker's employment contract, a legally enslaved person and a medieval tenant, even when unequal bargaining offers some useful analytical parallels.

## A falsifiable model of manorial production, dues and bargaining

The economic model has three separate levels. Production converts land, labour, seed and animals into output. Extraction reallocates the harvest and working time by rents, monopoly fees, courts and personal duties. Bargaining determines how those claims shift with crop prices, diseases, alternative employment and political enforcement. These are related mechanisms, not a claim that all villages had the same institutions. [1][3][5]

A valid empirical design would compile harvests, legal status, payments, working days and prices from actual estate records, specifying missing entries and changes in accounting practice. A proposition that tenants gained freedom after plague would need evidence of changed obligations, not just higher nominal wages. A proposition that a manor was closed to trade would require proof that market purchases and sales were absent. Both hypotheses admit disconfirming observations.

The resulting lesson is not a romantic story of protective lords nor a universal numerical extraction rate. Economic power depends on who can compel labour, which assets families control and where they can seek alternative income. This distinct feudal/manorial dossier complements—rather than duplicates—a broader history of medieval commerce, currency and urban production.
