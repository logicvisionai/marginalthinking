# Global Macro — Strong US growth meets falling real income and a reassessment of AI financing

**30 October 2026 — early Brasília edition. Information cutoff: 05:00 BRT (08:00 UTC).** US output, income, financial-market and European policy observations refer to releases or completed sessions on 29 October. The Bank of Japan decision was released at 12:02 JST on 30 October, before this cutoff. Subsequent US and European releases, later Asian market prices and future policy meetings are not presumed.

The new economic evidence is a divergence between the production measured over a quarter and the purchasing power available to households at its end. The US Bureau of Economic Analysis estimates that real GDP grew at a **3.4% annualised rate in the third quarter**, up from **2.2%** in the second; real final sales to private domestic purchasers increased **4.9%**. Yet the separate September monthly accounts show **real disposable personal income falling 0.3%** and real consumption virtually unchanged, while twelve-month PCE inflation reached **3.6%**. Quarterly output and monthly household cash flow measure different things, but their combination weakens the proposition that strong aggregate growth guarantees a broadening consumer recovery. [1][2]

The market response was not a simple vote against growth. On 29 October the Nasdaq Composite fell **1.37%**, the S&P 500 **0.30%**, and the small-company Russell 2000 rose approximately **1.0%**. US Treasury's indicative ten-year par yield declined from **5.05% on 28 October to 4.98% on 29 October**. That coexistence—solid measured activity, lower sovereign yields, and selling concentrated in expensive technology exposure—is more consistent with an earnings-and-capital-allocation reassessment than with a uniform funding panic. Index prices cannot reveal the identity of sellers or prove a permanent rotation. [3][4]

Europe and Japan add an institutional constraint. The ECB kept its deposit rate at **2.50%** on 29 October while explicitly warning that expensive energy may simultaneously raise inflation and weaken growth. On 30 October the Bank of Japan kept its overnight rate near **1.25%** by an **8–1 vote**, but separately decided to accelerate the disposal of its ETF holdings beginning in January 2027. The Japanese decision changes a future central-bank equity-supply schedule; it is not an immediate sale of the full planned amount. [5][6][7]

## A quarterly expansion can coexist with a weaker household exit rate

The advance GDP estimate reports investment, consumer spending and government spending contributing to third-quarter growth, with exports falling and imports increasing. Imports subtract from the GDP accounting identity, even when some imported capital goods help build domestic productive facilities. The BEA attributes part of the investment increase to information-processing equipment, principally computers and peripherals. This establishes a composition of measured expenditure; it does not identify the profitability of each AI project or prove that imported equipment was used productively. Inventory accumulation also contributed to investment, but inventory additions need not repeat next quarter. [1]

The second-quarter comparison is important because the acceleration is not solely an artefact of comparing the third quarter with an abnormally negative quarter. Nevertheless, the **4.9%** increase in real final sales to private domestic purchasers should not be treated as a forecast for fourth-quarter household income. It includes private fixed investment alongside consumption and is a quarterly annualised rate. The separate September report measures a one-month change in household purchasing power and spending. Mixing the two frequencies in one numerical chart would create a false comparison. [1][2]

In September nominal personal income rose **0.2%** and nominal consumption **0.5%**. The overall PCE price index rose **0.5%** in that month; real disposable income fell **0.3%** and real consumption was less than **0.1%** higher, displayed as **0.0%** in the BEA summary table. The personal saving rate stood at **3.8%**. Nominal spending growth thus cannot be read as equivalent growth in the quantity of goods and services purchased. Nor does one month of declining real income establish a recession when quarterly output is expanding. [2]

The monthly twelve-month PCE inflation rate of **3.6%**, and **3.1%** excluding food and energy, should not be compared directly with the **2.1%** third-quarter annualised PCE deflator as though one series disproved the other. One is a change over twelve months, the other a quarterly annualised price movement, and their baskets and vintages are reported under distinct accounting conventions. The correct inference is narrower: prices continued to erode household purchasing power in September even though the preceding quarter recorded strong real activity. [1][2]

## The bond rally is not evidence that credit has become cheap

The official US Treasury curve fell across maturities between 28 and 29 October: the two-year yield declined from **4.67% to 4.61%**, the ten-year from **5.05% to 4.98%**, and the thirty-year from **5.37% to 5.32%**. The ten-year-minus-two-year spread moved from **38 to 37 basis points**. This is a small daily flattening after the previous day's steepening, not a structural reversal of the level of dollar borrowing costs. Treasury constant-maturity rates are interpolated from indicative bid-side quotes around the close, not executable simultaneous trades. [3]

A falling benchmark yield can reflect demand for duration, changes in expected policy, lower expected growth, altered term compensation or a combination of these. The observed curve alone cannot allocate those contributions. Borrowers also face credit spreads, collateral requirements, lending standards and refinancing calendars. A lower government yield may reduce a reference rate while leaving risky-company funding expensive or inaccessible. The contrast between strong GDP and technology-share declines is therefore compatible with investors differentiating projects by expected cash generation rather than withdrawing from every asset class. [1][3][4]

Money-market fund data provide an additional, differently dated test. The Investment Company Institute reported **$7.901 trillion** of US money-market fund assets for the week ended **28 October**, down **$43.29 billion** from the preceding week. Government funds accounted for a **$37.23 billion** decline, prime funds **$3.07 billion**, and tax-exempt funds **$2.99 billion**. ICI describes asset changes as primarily driven by flows and a proxy for net new cash flows; they are not a complete cross-border allocation ledger, and changes in reported asset values are not equivalent to the amount of Treasury securities sold. This weekly reduction does not establish a general run from cash into equities, particularly when the Nasdaq was falling. [8]

## AI capital expenditure has reached a harder profitability test

The previous two editions examined a supplier's long-term memory contracts and the divergence between policy rates and longer yields. The incremental evidence is the **29 October market reaction to corporate earnings**: Alphabet fell roughly **12.6%** as investors questioned the relationship between expensive AI spending and near-term cash generation; the Nasdaq declined while the Russell 2000 advanced. A supplier's signed purchase commitments can coexist with customer cash-flow pressure. The commitment is evidence of demand for components, not proof of sufficient economic returns on data-centre infrastructure or software adoption. [4][9]

There are at least three distinct accounting moments. A cloud company can commit to buy hardware, pay for and construct capacity, and later earn revenue from services that use it. The first is a contractual obligation, the second is capital spending, and the third is realised operating income. If equipment is imported, the expenditure also appears in the national accounts through offsetting import and investment entries. Neither the GDP investment contribution nor an equity-market valuation establishes the final return. Operating cash flow, utilisation, depreciation, financing cost and customer retention would be needed for that assessment. [1][9]

The strongest alternative interpretation is that the market is merely repricing one earnings disappointment while the wider AI investment cycle remains productive. The positive Russell 2000 return and robust third-quarter private domestic demand are evidence against a universal collapse. Conversely, a persistent sequence of lower hyperscaler free cash flow, reduced equipment orders, wider financing spreads and cancelled power or grid connections would support a broader investment retrenchment. A single technology-heavy session cannot decide between these cases. [1][4][9]

## Europe faces the opposite mix of confidence and financing pressure

The ECB's **29 October** hold left the deposit facility at **2.50%**, main refinancing operations at **2.65%**, and marginal lending facility at **2.90%**. Its statement attributes upside inflation risks and downside growth risks to the duration and magnitude of the energy shock, while saying wage responses have not yet become material. The ECB also notes that financing conditions have tightened since its previous meeting. These are official policy assessments, not a measurement of every euro-area loan contract. [5]

European Commission surveys give an independent demand-side observation: October economic sentiment fell **0.6 points to 96.9** in both the EU and the euro area. Employment expectations, however, rose **0.7 points to 98.2** in the euro area, while the flash consumer confidence indicator was **−17.9**. The mix is not consistent with an unqualified labour-market collapse. It is consistent with weak household confidence coexisting with firms that have not yet cut expected staffing at the same pace. Survey levels are balances or composite indices, not observed GDP or job creation. [10]

The financing channel is especially relevant for indebted governments and energy-importing manufacturers. Higher imported fuel and transport costs squeeze margins and real wages; tighter sovereign yields can then raise refinancing costs as debt matures. Fiscal support may cushion households but can require additional issuance. The net effect depends on existing debt maturity, subsidy design and the persistence of the energy shock. The ECB's continued portfolio run-off is not equivalent to a new discretionary rate increase, and the existence of its transmission-protection instrument is not evidence that it has been activated. [5][10]

## Japan separates the policy rate from central-bank equity ownership

The Bank of Japan's 30 October statement kept the uncollateralised overnight call rate around **1.25%**, with one dissent in favour of **1.50%**. In a separate unanimous decision, it increased the intended pace of ETF sales from about **¥330 billion** to **¥860 billion a year on a book-value basis**, effective **January 2027**. The BOJ estimates that the revised pace corresponds to approximately **0.1%** of Tokyo Stock Exchange Prime Market trading value, compared with **0.05%** previously. The bank may adjust or suspend sales in response to market conditions. [6][7]

That policy changes the expected supply of equity-fund units from an unusually large public holder without proving an immediate market-price effect. The BOJ estimates approximately **43 years** to dispose of the September 2026 stock at the new book-value pace, versus **112 years** at the old pace; the figures are mechanical estimates, not promises that all future sales occur at unchanged prices or volumes. This is a market-structure and balance-sheet decision distinct from the overnight rate. It should not be conflated with private Japanese investors repatriating foreign securities. [6][7]

Japan's imported-energy exposure means that yen depreciation can increase domestic fuel costs while higher rates can raise government and private refinancing expenses. But the evidence does not show a one-way exchange-rate or capital-flow response. Insurers, banks, pension funds and foreign investors face different mandates and hedging costs. An acceleration of BOJ ETF disposals could affect local equity supply gradually; its actual price impact requires transaction and liquidity evidence after implementation. [6][7]

```flow

AI equipment commitments → data-centre construction and financing → utilisation and customer revenue → realised investment returns
Energy import costs → European and Japanese inflation pressure → policy and refinancing constraints → household purchasing power
BOJ ETF disposal schedule → gradual public-holder sales from January 2027 → possible local liquidity effect, conditional on execution
```

## Commodity exposure and the tests that would change the assessment

The World Bank's **28 October** Commodity Markets Outlook projects a **23%** rise in overall commodity prices in 2026, with **32%** higher energy prices and food commodities rising around **10%** in 2026 and **7%** in 2027. These are forecasts conditional on assumptions about conflict, supply and weather, not observed 30 October spot returns. Its baseline Brent average of **$91 per barrel for 2026** should not be substituted for an intraday oil quote. The same forecast warns that energy and fertiliser costs may affect food affordability in import-dependent countries. [11]

US EIA natural-gas storage data released **29 October** show the Mountain region at **258 billion cubic feet** for the week ended 23 October, **9.5% below** its year-earlier stock, while the East held **913 billion cubic feet**, **0.2% above** a year earlier. Regional storage differences demonstrate why a national energy-price narrative is insufficient: transport capacity, regional weather and access to fuel alternatives shape the effect on consumers and industry. These stock levels are not the same as global oil supply or a forecast of winter prices. [12]

The near-term central case is **selective investment and persistent price pressure**, rather than simultaneous global contraction or uninterrupted AI-led expansion. It would be weakened by a sustained rebound in US real disposable income and real consumption, broad-based improvements in European confidence, stable AI customer free cash flow despite capex, and easing energy costs. It would strengthen if September's income squeeze persisted, AI commitments were revised down, credit spreads widened despite lower Treasuries, and energy-price increases passed into wages and core services. No numerical probability is assigned without a defensible distribution. [1][2][5][9][11]

The next evidence checkpoints are the revised US GDP and October personal income releases scheduled for **25 November**, subsequent corporate cash-flow disclosures, European inflation and confidence data, realised BOJ ETF sales after January 2027, and observable fuel shipments and inventories. The core distinction remains between what has been produced, what households can purchase, what investors have actually financed, and what assets central banks have merely scheduled to sell. [1][2][6][7]
