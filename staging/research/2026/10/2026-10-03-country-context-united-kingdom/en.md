# United Kingdom: services power, fiscal constraint and the reconstruction of a post-Brexit growth model

The United Kingdom in 2026 combines several forms of structural strength that are often analysed separately: a global financial centre, a large services-export base, strong universities and scientific institutions, advanced aerospace and defence capabilities, a major life-sciences sector, a flexible currency and central bank, extensive offshore energy resources, and a state able to borrow in its own currency. It also carries a set of constraints that interact with those strengths: weak productivity growth since the global financial crisis, high public debt and debt-service costs, persistent goods-trade deficits, housing and infrastructure bottlenecks, regional inequality, a smaller manufacturing share than several continental peers, and a post-Brexit trade regime that changed the cost of economic integration with the European Union.

The economy entered the second half of 2026 with stronger headline growth than the March fiscal forecast had expected. The Office for National Statistics revised real GDP growth to 0.6% quarter on quarter in the first quarter and 0.5% in the second quarter. But productivity remains the more important structural variable: output per hour in the second quarter of 2026 was only 2.3% above its 2019 average and was 0.2% lower than a year earlier on the current Labour Force Survey measure. The country can generate growth; the unresolved question is whether it can generate sustained output per worker quickly enough to carry an ageing society, larger defence commitments and a public debt ratio close to the size of annual GDP.

The UK's post-Brexit model is therefore not a choice between "Europe" and "the world". Its economic geography makes Europe the nearest large market, while its comparative advantages in finance, professional services, higher education, technology, defence and culture are global. The viable strategy is to reduce unnecessary trade and investment frictions with Europe while using regulatory autonomy, industrial policy and global networks where they create measurable gains.

## The constitutional system concentrates sovereignty in Parliament but distributes practical power across institutions and nations

The United Kingdom has no single codified constitutional document. Parliamentary sovereignty, statute law, common law, conventions, the monarchy, an independent judiciary, devolved governments and a professional civil service form the operating constitutional system. The Prime Minister leads His Majesty's Government and is normally the principal political figure in the House of Commons. Andy Burnham became Prime Minister on 20 July 2026.

Economic execution is more distributed than the phrase "unitary state" suggests. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland possess devolved institutions with different powers over health, education, transport, planning, taxation and other areas. English local and combined authorities also matter for housing, transport and regional investment. The Bank of England sets monetary policy independently within the statutory framework, while the Office for Budget Responsibility provides independent fiscal forecasts.

```flow
UK Parliament + central government
  -> taxation, fiscal rules, defence, trade, national regulation
Devolved governments
  -> health, education, transport, planning and selected taxes
Local / combined authorities
  -> housing, local infrastructure, development and services
Bank of England
  -> monetary policy and financial stability
OBR + independent regulators + courts
  -> fiscal scrutiny, sector rules and legal constraints
Firms + households + capital markets
  -> investment, employment, consumption and financing feedback
```

The advantage is institutional adaptability: major policy can be changed without a constitutional amendment process as rigid as in many states. The risk is policy volatility. When governments alter tax, planning, energy or industrial rules frequently, formal flexibility can reduce the private sector's confidence in long-horizon investment.

## The economic model shifted from industrial empire to a high-value services economy without losing all manufacturing depth

Britain's nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century power rested heavily on manufacturing, shipping, finance and empire. Deindustrialisation after the 1960s reduced the share of traditional manufacturing, while London and the south-east became increasingly specialised in finance, professional services, technology, media and high-value corporate functions. North Sea oil and gas altered the external account and energy system from the 1970s, while European integration increasingly connected UK production and investment to continental supply chains.

The result is often caricatured as "finance replacing industry". The reality is more complex. The UK retains globally relevant aerospace, pharmaceuticals, automotive engineering, defence, chemicals, food manufacturing and specialised machinery. What changed is the economy-wide weight of manufacturing relative to services and the geography of high-productivity activity.

This creates a structural asymmetry. Services exports can generate very large surpluses with limited physical transport, but many regions outside the strongest metropolitan clusters depend more heavily on manufacturing, logistics and public-sector employment. National success in finance or software therefore does not automatically resolve regional productivity gaps.

| Structural layer | Durable strength | Main constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Financial and professional services | global network, legal system, deep capital markets | regulatory competition and concentration in London |
| Advanced manufacturing | aerospace, defence, automotive, pharmaceuticals | scale, energy costs and supply-chain investment |
| Universities and science | strong research base and global talent attraction | commercialisation and regional diffusion |
| Flexible currency | independent monetary adjustment | exchange-rate and imported-inflation volatility |
| North Sea + offshore wind | energy expertise and maritime infrastructure | declining mature fields and network investment |
| EU proximity | large nearby market and supply chains | post-Brexit border and regulatory frictions |

## Growth improved in the first half of 2026, but the productivity regime remains weak

The ONS estimated that real GDP rose 0.6% in the first quarter of 2026 and 0.5% in the second. The second-quarter release also showed real household disposable income per head rising 1.0% after falling 0.8% in the first quarter. This is a meaningful cyclical improvement.

The productivity figures are the central warning. Output per hour only modestly exceeds the pre-pandemic level after more than six years. That is consistent with the broader post-2008 record in which productivity growth slowed sharply relative to the decades before the global financial crisis.

Weak productivity transmits into almost every other constraint. It limits real wage growth, makes public services more expensive relative to the tax base, raises the fiscal cost of ageing, reduces the affordability of defence expansion and makes regional convergence harder. Faster GDP growth driven mainly by more labour or temporary demand is therefore less valuable than growth driven by capital deepening and better output per hour.

## The services surplus is a strategic asset, but the goods deficit reveals the physical side of the economy

The UK's external accounts display a persistent duality. It typically runs a large deficit in goods and a large surplus in services. In the fourth quarter of 2025 the ONS estimated a goods deficit of about £58.0 billion and a services surplus of about £54.3 billion. Across 2025, the total trade deficit widened to £21.8 billion as imports of goods and services rose slightly faster than exports.

The services surplus reflects genuine comparative advantages: finance, insurance, legal services, consulting, accounting, architecture, engineering, education, media, technology and intellectual-property-related activities. These exports are less constrained by physical distance than manufactured goods and often benefit from English law, language and the concentration of specialised firms.

The goods deficit is not automatically a weakness. A high-income service economy can import manufactured goods efficiently. But persistent dependence on imported energy, machinery, electronics, vehicles or strategic components can create resilience and exchange-rate exposure. The policy question is not whether the UK should "make everything". It is which productive capabilities have enough economic or security value to justify domestic or allied capacity.

## Brexit changed the cost structure of UK–EU integration rather than ending integration

The European Union remains geographically and economically central to the UK. Supply chains, aviation, energy interconnectors, financial services, tourism, food, pharmaceuticals and manufacturing continue to cross the Channel. Brexit changed the legal framework under which those flows occur.

Leaving the single market and customs union created additional customs, regulatory and administrative steps for goods trade. The effect varies by sector: a software export can face little physical friction, while fresh food, automotive components or chemicals may face more documentation, standards and border procedures. Firms with enough scale can absorb fixed compliance costs more easily than small exporters.

At the same time, the UK regained greater formal discretion over regulation, subsidies, immigration and trade agreements. The economic value of that autonomy depends on use. Regulatory divergence creates value when it enables faster innovation or better rules; it destroys value when it creates duplicated compliance without meaningful domestic benefit.

The structural baseline is therefore selective reconnection rather than restoration of the pre-2020 regime. Reducing avoidable friction in food, professional qualifications, research cooperation, energy and supply chains can coexist with independent policy in other domains.

## London is a global capital-market asset, but national investment depends on whether finance reaches productive assets

The City of London remains one of the world's leading financial centres. Banking, insurance, asset management, foreign exchange, legal services and market infrastructure generate exports, tax revenue and high wages. The broader ecosystem also gives UK firms access to sophisticated financing.

Yet a deep financial centre does not guarantee high domestic investment. The UK has repeatedly faced the paradox of global capital-market strength alongside weak business investment and infrastructure gaps. Pension-fund allocation, risk appetite, planning delays, scale-up finance and uncertainty over policy all influence whether savings reach productive assets.

The challenge is therefore intermediation rather than a shortage of financial sophistication. A successful capital-market strategy would increase long-duration financing for housing, grids, transport, advanced manufacturing, technology scale-ups and clean energy without weakening prudential standards. The indicator is not the volume traded in London; it is the amount of productive capital formed in Britain.

## Fiscal space is constrained by both a high debt stock and unusually expensive debt service

The Office for Budget Responsibility's March 2026 forecast described a difficult fiscal starting point. Public sector net debt was projected at roughly 94.5% of GDP in 2025-26 and around the mid-90s through the end of the decade. Borrowing was projected to remain elevated before gradually declining. Debt interest spending was forecast at roughly £110 billion in 2025-26 and to rise in nominal terms thereafter.

```chart

Projected borrowing 2030-31 | 1.6
```

The debt structure makes inflation and interest rates especially important. The UK has a meaningful stock of inflation-linked gilts, and refinancing occurs at market rates that are much higher than during the decade after the global financial crisis. The OBR noted that debt interest has roughly doubled as a share of GDP relative to the decade before the pandemic.

This does not imply an imminent sovereign-financing crisis. The UK borrows in sterling, has a deep domestic financial system and an independent central bank. It does mean that every additional permanent spending commitment has a larger opportunity cost. Defence, health, pensions, industrial subsidies and infrastructure increasingly compete inside a tighter interest-cost envelope.

The March OBR baseline also predates the July 2026 change of prime minister. It should therefore be treated as the inherited fiscal reference point, not as a complete forecast of policy under the current government.

## Sterling and an independent central bank provide flexibility, but energy shocks expose the cost

The Bank of England maintained Bank Rate at 3.75% in September 2026. The Monetary Policy Committee noted that CPI inflation had reached 3.1% in August and was likely to rise further after renewed increases in global energy prices. Three of nine members voted for a rate increase.

Monetary independence gives the UK an adjustment instrument unavailable to euro-area members. The Bank can set rates for British inflation and labour-market conditions, while sterling can move in response to shocks. That flexibility is valuable when the UK's cycle differs from continental Europe.

The cost is that exchange-rate depreciation can transmit quickly into imported energy, food and manufactured goods. High public debt also increases the fiscal sensitivity to interest rates. Monetary and fiscal policy are therefore linked through the sovereign balance sheet even though the Bank is operationally independent.

## Housing is both a social constraint and a productivity constraint

High housing costs in London, the south-east and several other productive cities reduce real disposable income and make labour mobility more difficult. Planning restrictions, local opposition, infrastructure constraints and construction capacity all influence supply.

Housing affects productivity through matching. A high-productivity cluster cannot expand efficiently if workers cannot live within commuting distance. Firms then pay higher wages to compensate for housing costs, workers accept longer commutes, or investment moves elsewhere. The result can be lower agglomeration gains and weaker diffusion of opportunity.

The government's target of substantially increasing homebuilding and its planning reforms therefore matter beyond the construction sector. The relevant indicators are completed homes, affordability, planning times, transport access and whether new supply appears in the places with strongest labour demand.

## Devolution creates multiple economic models inside one sovereign state

Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland differ in industrial structure, demography, energy resources and fiscal arrangements. England itself contains large differences between London and the south-east, former industrial regions, university cities and rural areas. A UK average therefore hides distinct regional mechanisms.

Scotland combines oil and gas, offshore wind, finance, universities and a devolved government with significant policy authority. Wales has manufacturing and energy assets but lower productivity and income than the UK average. Northern Ireland has a unique post-Brexit trading position because of arrangements designed to avoid a hard border on the island of Ireland. English city-regions increasingly use devolved transport and development powers.

The policy challenge is not to make every region identical. It is to improve connectivity, skills, capital access and institutional capacity so that specialised regional economies can scale. Central grants without local execution capacity can produce weak returns; devolution without adequate fiscal capacity can shift responsibility without resources.

## The population is still growing, but migration is no longer the same labour-supply engine it was in 2022–2024

The ONS estimated the UK population at 69.5 million in mid-2025, up 0.3% from a year earlier. England accounted for most of the increase; Wales recorded a small decline, while Scotland and Northern Ireland changed little.

Long-term net migration fell sharply to 171,000 in the year ending December 2025, down from an updated 331,000 a year earlier and far below the 2023 peak. Non-EU work-related immigration declined substantially, while net migration of EU nationals remained negative.

This shift matters because the OBR already expects labour-supply growth to slow as migration falls and the population ages. Lower migration can reduce pressure on housing and public services in some places, but it can also tighten staffing in health, care, hospitality, construction, universities and high-skill industries. The economic effect depends on composition, participation, productivity and infrastructure rather than the headline number alone.

## The energy transition starts from strong offshore and low-carbon assets but still depends on networks and firm capacity

Great Britain obtained 73.3% of electricity generation from low-carbon technologies in 2025 according to the government's Clean Power metrics, while clean sources met 64.4% of electricity demand. These measures cover Great Britain rather than the whole United Kingdom and should not be treated as identical to a UK-wide energy balance.

The UK has exceptional offshore-wind resources, mature offshore engineering, nuclear experience, interconnectors and a long history in oil and gas. The government's strategy aims to combine offshore wind, nuclear, storage, grids, carbon capture and other technologies while using Great British Energy and private capital to expand investment.

As in Germany and Poland, capacity alone is not enough. Transmission, distribution, planning, storage and balancing determine delivered costs. Offshore projects can be delayed by grid queues even when seabed leases and capital are available. Nuclear projects offer firm low-carbon output but carry long construction and financing horizons. North Sea oil and gas can contribute security during the transition while mature fields decline structurally.

## Industrial policy has returned, focused on sectors where the UK already has capabilities

The Modern Industrial Strategy identifies eight growth-driving sectors: advanced manufacturing, clean energy industries, creative industries, defence, digital and technologies, financial services, life sciences, and professional and business services. The logic is selective rather than economy-wide protection: concentrate policy where existing capabilities, research and export networks make scale more plausible.

Advanced manufacturing policy targets aerospace, automotive, batteries, space, advanced materials and related industries. The clean-energy plan seeks to mobilise much larger private investment. Life sciences combines universities, the NHS, pharmaceuticals and biotechnology. Digital policy includes AI, semiconductors, cybersecurity, quantum and engineering biology.

The risk is familiar to industrial policy: government can misidentify future winners, create subsidy dependence or shift investment toward politically salient projects with weak returns. The counterargument is that planning, energy, procurement, skills and research coordination are already public-policy variables, so a purely passive state does not create a neutral market.

The test should be measurable additionality: business investment, productivity, exports, domestic supply-chain depth, private co-investment and whether supported firms remain competitive after initial policy support.

## Science is a major asset, but commercialisation and scale-up remain the bottleneck

The UK has globally strong universities, biomedical research, AI research, quantum programmes, aerospace engineering and creative industries. It attracts international students and researchers and has deep venture-capital networks relative to most of Europe.

The long-standing problem is that promising firms often scale elsewhere or are acquired before becoming large domestic corporations. Reasons include the size and risk appetite of late-stage capital, procurement, domestic customer scale, planning, energy costs and incentives for institutional investors.

A stronger scale-up ecosystem would not require preventing foreign acquisition. It would require making the UK sufficiently attractive that firms can choose to remain, list, build facilities and hire at scale domestically. The productivity payoff comes when research creates repeatable commercial systems, not when it produces publications alone.

## Defence is becoming a larger industrial and fiscal programme

The 2025 Strategic Defence Review set a path toward defence spending of 2.5% of GDP by 2027 and an ambition of 3% in the next Parliament when fiscal and economic conditions allow. The review also frames defence as an industrial policy channel.

The UK possesses important defence assets: nuclear submarines, aerospace, missiles, electronics, cyber capabilities, intelligence relationships and a permanent seat on the UN Security Council. It is also a nuclear-armed NATO state with unusually close security cooperation with the United States and major commitments to European security.

Higher spending can expand production and R&D, but procurement performance is crucial. Large programmes with delay and cost overruns can consume fiscal space without producing timely capability. A defence-industrial strategy succeeds when orders create operational systems, resilient inventories, trained personnel and exportable or dual-use capabilities.

## The UK's European and global roles reinforce one another more than they substitute for one another

The UK left the EU but not Europe. NATO, bilateral security ties, sanctions policy, intelligence cooperation, energy interconnection, scientific collaboration and trade keep the country embedded in European systems. The September 2026 Marginal Thinking Europe–Russia–Ukraine report already treats the UK as a central actor in the continent's security order.

At the same time, London remains globally connected through finance, the Commonwealth, the United States, the Indo-Pacific, multinational corporations and global services exports. This network gives the UK more external options than many European economies.

The constraint is scale. The EU and United States are both much larger economic blocs. Regulatory autonomy does not make the UK equivalent in bargaining power to either. Strategy therefore depends on coalitions, standards influence, specialised capabilities and market access rather than pure autonomy.

## Regional inequality is a capital-allocation problem, not only an income-transfer problem

Differences in productivity across British regions are persistent. London and parts of the south-east benefit from dense labour markets, finance, universities, transport and corporate headquarters. Other regions have strong firms and universities but thinner capital markets, weaker transport or smaller clusters.

Transfers can equalise disposable income but do not automatically build productive capacity. Long-run convergence requires transport links, housing, technical education, research commercialisation, local institutional capacity and private investment. The success of devolution should therefore be measured by capital formation and productivity rather than by the amount of authority transferred.

A national industrial strategy can help if it aligns sector policy with real regional assets: aerospace in existing clusters, offshore wind around ports, life sciences around research hospitals and universities, advanced manufacturing where supplier networks already exist. Dispersing projects purely for geographic symbolism risks reducing returns.

## Baseline scenarios

### Scenario 1: productivity-led reacceleration

Planning reform, housing construction, grids, industrial investment and scale-up finance raise business investment and output per hour. Services remain strong while advanced manufacturing and clean energy deepen regional productive capacity. Fiscal ratios stabilise because nominal revenue and productivity grow faster than debt-service pressure.

### Scenario 2: services resilience with weak domestic capital formation

London, professional services, universities and technology remain globally competitive, but housing, infrastructure and planning constraints keep domestic investment weak. GDP grows modestly, regional inequality persists and fiscal consolidation remains difficult because productivity does not accelerate.

### Scenario 3: energy and defence investment crowd in industry

Offshore wind, grids, nuclear, defence procurement and advanced manufacturing create durable supply chains outside the south-east. The UK becomes more resilient in energy and security while exporting specialised systems. The risk is cost overruns and shortages of engineers and construction capacity.

### Scenario 4: repeated inflation-fiscal shocks compress policy space

Energy shocks, weak productivity and high gilt yields keep inflation and debt-service costs elevated. Fiscal rules force repeated tax or spending adjustments, reducing policy stability. Private investment remains cautious and public services absorb a growing share of the budget.

## What would strengthen the structural assessment

- sustained output-per-hour growth clearly above the post-2008 trend;
- higher business investment as a share of GDP;
- faster housing completions in high-productivity labour markets;
- lower planning and grid-connection times;
- continued services-export growth alongside stronger advanced-manufacturing exports;
- measurable reduction in UK–EU administrative trade costs;
- private capital mobilised behind industrial-strategy projects without permanent subsidy dependence;
- clean-power growth accompanied by lower delivered industrial electricity costs;
- defence procurement that creates usable capacity on schedule;
- more scale-up companies remaining and investing in the UK;
- regional productivity growth outside London and the south-east;
- a credible medium-term decline in borrowing and stabilisation of debt interest.

## What would weaken the assessment

- productivity remaining near the 2019 level for several more years;
- high gilt yields and debt interest forcing repeated fiscal retrenchment;
- business investment remaining weak despite industrial-policy support;
- renewed goods-trade frictions with the EU without offsetting gains elsewhere;
- persistent housing shortages in the most productive cities;
- clean-energy projects constrained by grids, planning or financing;
- defence commitments rising faster than procurement capacity;
- lower migration producing acute labour shortages without automation or participation gains;
- regional investment announcements failing to produce durable private clusters;
- policy reversals that shorten the investment horizon of firms.

## Evidence limitations

ONS Labour Force Survey data have been subject to quality concerns and methodological work, so labour and productivity measures should be interpreted with the published caveats. The latest population estimate is for mid-2025 even though this dossier is dated October 2026. Long-term migration estimates for the year ending December 2025 remain provisional official statistics in development.

The March 2026 OBR forecast predates the July 2026 change of prime minister. It is used as the inherited fiscal baseline rather than as a statement of every current-government policy. Future Budgets can materially alter the borrowing path.

Great Britain's Clean Power metrics exclude Northern Ireland and are not identical to whole-UK electricity statistics. Government investment announcements are not treated as completed capital expenditure. Industrial-strategy ambitions are policy objectives; the report separates them from realised investment and productivity.

Trade data are revised and can be distorted by volatile precious-metals flows; the ONS underlying trade measures are preferred where available.
