# Slavery and economic power: a global history with Brazil at the centre of the Atlantic system

**Edition:** 24 October 2026 · **Information cutoff:** 24 October 2026

Slavery is a family of coercive labour and property regimes, not one uniform institution found unchanged across all regions and eras. Ancient Mediterranean enslavement, systems of household and military bondage in parts of Africa and Asia, Indigenous captivity in the Americas, Atlantic plantation slavery and modern forced labour involve different laws, labour incentives and routes to liberation. Their common feature is the denial of effective freedom to withdraw labour or control one's life. Comparing them requires care: twentieth-century international definitions should not be mechanically projected onto every earlier hierarchy. [1][7][8]

Brazil became the largest single receiving territory within the Atlantic trafficking network and maintained legal slavery until **13 May 1888**. The economic relevance reaches beyond plantations: ships, insurance, credit, ports, state revenue, land values and commercial accumulation benefited from or were structured around coerced labour. Equally essential is the history of resistance by enslaved Africans, their descendants and Indigenous peoples. Abolition was neither a benevolent gift from one ruler nor an automatic effect of industrialization. [1][2][3][5]

## Definitions and the historiographic cost of an undifferentiated term

A person can be held as chattel property, bound to an estate, subjected to debt bondage, trafficked for compulsory labour or coerced into marriage. These experiences intersect, but formal law, alienability, ability to form families and paths to manumission differ. A medieval peasant who owed customary services and retained enforceable rights is not automatically identical to a transatlantic chattel slave legally saleable as property. The comparison should identify the powers of the master or authority and what rights the captive can exercise. [2][7]

The ILO's present operational concept combines forced labour and forced marriage into an aggregate labelled 'modern slavery'. It does not mean the legal institution of hereditary chattel slavery remains recognized in the same way by present states. Some forced labour is imposed by governments; other cases involve private employers, criminal exploitation or abusive recruitment. Legal categories can hide real coercion, just as broad rhetoric can exaggerate comparability across epochs. [7][8]

An economic history should build a matrix by legal status, recruitment mechanism, coercive sanctions, owner claims, ability to move and earnings rights. It should then investigate who captured surplus and who enforced the system. Without this discipline, apparent comparisons of 'slave prices' and 'modern slavery prevalence' become numerically meaningless.

## Before the Atlantic: slavery in ancient and medieval worlds

Slavery existed long before European Atlantic expansion. Ancient states employed coerced workers in households, agriculture, mines and military production; forms of enslavement also existed across Islamic, Asian and African polities, differing in status and religious law. Warfare, debt and criminal punishment could be routes into captivity. It would be inaccurate to posit a single continental civilization as the inventor of slavery or to portray all precolonial African societies as sharing one labour institution.

In a stylized economic model, the captor acquires labour by force and seeks an income stream less subsistence, supervision and repression costs. Coercion may raise gross surplus for the owner while discouraging skill accumulation and creating enormous moral and social harm. Where capture expands through warfare it also destroys populations and productive capacity. These costs may fall outside the owner's accounts, so private profitability does not establish social efficiency.

The Atlantic trade later connected distinct coercive systems on an unprecedented oceanic scale. Studying early institutions provides context, not exculpation for merchants and planters who voluntarily participated in later racialized enslavement. Detailed comparison requires local primary scholarship rather than one universal curve for 'world slavery'.

## Atlantic shipping, epidemiological mortality and counting rules

SlaveVoyages estimates **12,521,354 embarkations** from African regions between **1501 and 1866**, with published matrices by embarkation region and time period. The estimates are reconstructions based on voyages, missing records and modelling; they do not count all forms of African enslavement worldwide. A person's embarkation is not the same as disembarkation, and neither is a census stock. Mortality, diversion and documentation determine the differences. [1]

The database reports approximately 64,137 embarkations in 1501–1550, 2,560,629 in 1701–1750 and 3,933,989 in 1751–1800. These are estimated people during different fifty-year intervals, not annual rates or direct measures of money earned by slavers. Counting a century together with a shorter period, or adding Brazilian arrivals to the aggregate embarkations, creates double counting. The transatlantic economy expanded over centuries before nineteenth-century interdiction. [1]

Transport itself required ships, provisioning, finance, insurance, markets and coastal intermediaries. Fatality and violence are core features of the institution, not external accidents removed from economic analysis. Financing merchants could spread voyage risk, while captive people bore extreme involuntary mortality and rights deprivation. The correct financial comparison includes losses suffered by captives and African societies, which the private ledger does not represent.

## Why Brazilian plantations became major recipients

Portuguese imperial commerce connected Brazilian sugar plantations and later gold and coffee production to Atlantic enslaved labour. Plantations demanded labour at scale, organized around coerced work groups and export commodities whose buyers were overseas. The Brazilian territory became the single largest destination in the Atlantic network over the full period. This historical fact does not imply every region, century or commodity relied on enslaved labour in identical proportions. [1][3][5]

Industrial and financial beneficiaries extended beyond planters. European merchants provided credit and textiles for trade; insurers and carriers earned shipping income; local intermediaries purchased people and crops; governments collected customs and defended the legal institution. The productive system was also a political coalition linking landed assets to access to office and monetary policy. A narrow account that places the entire system 'inside the farm gate' misses accumulation in ports and cities.

The contradiction was distributional: export earnings and productive assets could expand while a large labour force lacked wages, property, autonomy and schooling. Consumption markets remained socially unequal; capital was concentrated in land and human property instead of broad-based human development. Measuring the system's private export profits cannot justify coercion or establish the counterfactual growth path under free labour.

## Indigenous enslavement and frontier territorial expansion

The story is incomplete if restricted to Africans transported across the Atlantic. Colonial expansion in Brazil included enslavement and other forms of compulsory Indigenous labour, forced displacement, violent frontier expeditions and religious/administrative control. Legal regimes changed by place and period, and Indigenous communities were not passive: they resisted, fled, negotiated and maintained networks. The Atlantic shipping dataset cannot measure this internal coercion, so a single imported-person total must not be presented as the total population harmed by slavery in Brazil.

Territorial expansion linked labour capture and land seizure. Settlers often pursued agricultural access and coerced workers together; epidemics, war and movement disrupted Indigenous demographic and economic systems. In some places labour was organized under nominal forms of protection or tutelage that masked lack of free choice. The economic value of settlement land to colonizers was not the same as social value to displaced people.

A rigorous future microhistory would trace specific communities, legal orders, church archives, frontier routes and surviving Indigenous testimony. Generalization across the entire precolonial and colonial population would erase diverse social structures. An analysis centered on Atlantic capitalism must still account for the internal colonial frontier and its continuous distributional effects.

## The financing of human property and the allocation of risk

Under legalized chattel slavery, people were treated as assets by owners and could be sold, inherited or pledged within relevant legal arrangements. That created a financial mechanism: lenders could value captive people as collateral and slaveholders could expand production before export receipts arrived. But the price of an enslaved person reflects coercively appropriated expected labour and legal enforcement, not a legitimate measure of that person's worth. Abolition destroyed the owner's legal claim, not a natural economic entitlement. [2][5]

A plantation operating with debt faces multiple exposures: harvest failure, commodity prices, freight, mortality and the ability to maintain coercion. Owners attempted to transfer some financial risk to creditors, merchants, captives and society. A collapse in slave prices might threaten lender collateral yet represent approaching liberation for those whom the law wrongly treated as property. The same event has sharply different welfare interpretations.

Public-policy debates about compensation therefore ask whose losses are recognized. Abolition can cause an owner's asset write-off, but compensating owners while leaving freed people without land or education privileges former beneficiaries of coercion. Financial-accounting loss and moral/social gain cannot be added or netted without specifying whose welfare is measured.

## Resistance, quilombos, abolitionism and the agency of enslaved people

Resistance ranged from work refusal and flight to legal petitions, negotiated manumission, insurgency and the formation of quilombos. Such actions imposed costs on the slave system and created alternative communities; they also revealed aspirations and autonomous political organization. A narrative in which elite lawmakers 'gave freedom' to a passive population deletes indispensable historical causation. The National Library's abolition dossier records the growth of resistance and organized pressure before 1888. [5][11]

The abolitionist movement joined enslaved and free Black actors, journalists, lawyers, religious participants, segments of the urban public and politicians. Different factions disagreed over compensation, speed and citizenship. International antislavery pressure altered enforcement incentives, but local mobilization determined much of the final political feasibility. History should not collapse these groups into one ideology.

From a labour-economics perspective, flight and collective action changed the expected costs of repression and the bargaining conditions of owners. Yet reducing emancipatory struggle solely to an economic shock would erase human dignity and collective rights. Agency belongs in both causal and moral analysis, as a direct force reshaping the institution.

```flow
Violence and capture → transatlantic transport, private credit and maritime insurance → coerced production → export revenue and concentrated ownership
Resistance, abolitionist mobilization and enforcement pressure → reduction in slave trading → domestic political conflict → abolition law of 1888
Legal emancipation without broad asset and education access → unequal bargaining and persistent deprivation → present need for rights enforcement and opportunity
```

## 1831, 1850, 1871, 1885 and 1888: law versus enforcement

The legal chronology rejects the fiction that a single 1888 signature ended every coercive practice. The **1831** law declared illegal arrivals free but enforcement was deeply deficient; the **1850** Eusébio de Queirós law reinforced action against oceanic trafficking; the **1871** Free Womb legislation altered the status of children; the **1885** elderly emancipation law had limited immediate reach; and **Law 3,353 of 13 May 1888** formally extinguished slavery. The Arquivo Nacional documents the sequence and why laws often failed to protect people in practice. [2][3][4][5][9]

British maritime pressure mattered, but it was only one component of a larger shift in Brazilian power relations, slave prices, internal trade and abolitionist organization. The persistence of unlawful arrivals after 1831 shows the difference between a rule and compliance. Creditors and planters adjusted behaviour over decades, not overnight.

1888 altered formal legal status but did not itself distribute land, guaranteed wages, schools or protections against racial discrimination. The absence of material incorporation shapes a long-run historical interpretation without proving a single numerical 'cost of slavery' to modern GDP. Causal quantification requires local, intergenerational comparison and explicit assumptions.

## The 1872 census and why stocks cannot be added to voyage flows

The **1872 Brazilian census**, as summarized by IBGE, counted approximately **1.5 million enslaved people** among roughly ten million residents. This is a population stock at one date, not the count of all enslaved Africans shipped to Brazil over three centuries. Survivors of earlier voyages, children born locally, emancipated people, deaths and migration all affect the stock. Combining the 1872 total with SlaveVoyages embarkations as additive numbers would double count or mix incomparable populations. [1][6]

Census classifications and underreporting also need scrutiny. Enumerators operated under unequal legal and social structures; race, nativity, religious identification and legal status were not necessarily measured consistently. A demographic history must confront death rates, fertility, regional displacement and the economic implications of the domestic slave trade after maritime repression.

A careful economic denominator matters. The fact that slavery's share of total population changed does not show that labour coercion stopped being important in particular coffee-producing regions. Likewise an aggregate export total does not identify conditions for a specific household. The correct empirical method links population, location, occupation and legal status with appropriate dates.

## After abolition: land, schooling, labour contracts and persistent inequality

Formal liberty did not automatically create the assets needed for independent bargaining. Freed households frequently faced scarce land access, exclusionary hiring and education opportunities, unstable housing and continuing violence. Regions differed in immigrant labour recruitment, coffee investment, smallholder possibilities and urban wage demand. Simple stories that slavery ended and modern labour began the next day ignore these transitional costs.

Economic mobility depends on ownership, literacy, credit, networks and protection under courts. Without access to these, a person formally free may still face extremely weak wage bargaining or abusive debt relations. That does not make every later exploitative employment relation identical to historical chattel slavery; legal freedom is real but may be materially constrained.

A serious intergenerational analysis would link emancipation records, land titles, occupations, school access and race across specific municipalities, comparing treated and appropriate comparison populations. National averages alone cannot reveal the entire causal chain. The long-run concern is not to assign collective guilt by ancestry but to identify institutional paths through which historical expropriation affects present opportunity.

## Contemporary forced labour and what the 49.6m figure means

The ILO/Walk Free/IOM **2022 report** estimated **49.6 million people in modern slavery in 2021**: **27.6 million in forced labour** and **22 million in forced marriage**. The figures are survey- and case-data-based estimates, not a direct register of every victim, and the two categories are defined for international monitoring. They should not be interpreted as the number living under legally identical nineteenth-century Brazilian slave codes. [7][8]

Present mechanisms include deceptive recruitment, debt bondage, document confiscation, threats, forced commercial sexual exploitation and state-imposed work. Migration status and weak labour enforcement can increase vulnerability. Transnational supply chains create opacity where subcontractors and brokers separate brands from production sites. The relevant causal variables are worker mobility, employer concentration, the threat of punishment, grievance access and legal protection—not nationality or the mere existence of commercial contracts.

Abolition of formal ownership does not abolish every form of coercion; nevertheless describing all low-paid work as 'slavery' would obscure severe differences in freedom and enforcement. Prevention requires targeted inspection, credible remedies, cross-border recruitment safeguards, accessible rights and victim-centered data—not just promises that corporate purchasing codes eliminate abuses.

## Synthesis: coercion, capitalist accumulation and obligations of evidence

Slavery contributed materially to the production and movement of commodities in the Atlantic world, while inflicting profound human, demographic and institutional injury. It operated through state enforcement, private credit and trade networks; resistance and abolitionism contested those mechanisms until formal legal change became possible. Explaining the economy does not reduce suffering to accounting, and acknowledging pre-Atlantic bondage does not diminish the responsibility of later Atlantic participants. [1][2][5]

There are three research tasks that should not be collapsed. A documentary history reconstructs routes, laws, owners, contracts and resistance. A causal economic history asks how forced labour affected output, land, schooling and financial accumulation against credible alternatives. A present labour-rights study measures coercion under contemporary conventions. Each has its own units and uncertainty.

The forward question is institutional: can states enforce real exit rights, protect migrants and informal workers, expand access to land and education where exclusion persists, and hold supply chains accountable? Any precise cost-of-slavery GDP counterfactual or quantitative reparations assessment requires a separate model and assumptions. This report avoids disguising moral stakes as an unverified equation.
