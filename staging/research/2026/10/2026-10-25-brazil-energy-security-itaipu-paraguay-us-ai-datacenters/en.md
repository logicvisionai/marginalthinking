# Brazilian energy security, Itaipu and the new competition for power: Paraguay, the United States, data centers and strategic infrastructure

Brazilian energy security cannot be measured solely by the number of power stations or the renewable share of electricity generation. A secure system must supply reliable power when and where it is needed, transport it across resilient networks, finance critical maintenance, protect international agreements and manage the competing claims of ordinary consumers and industrial enterprises. The global expansion of artificial intelligence adds another dimension: dependable electricity and grid interconnection now influence where computational infrastructure will be built and who will capture its economic value.

Itaipu lies at the center of this intersection. Brazil and Paraguay signed the binational treaty on 26 April 1973, under military governments, and generation began in May 1984. The installation has twenty generating units with **14 gigawatts of installed capacity**, a power measure distinct from electricity generated over time. Its production reached **72,879,287 megawatt-hours in 2025**, or roughly **72.88 terawatt-hours**, 8.63% above the previous year. The binational company separately recorded **25,768 GWh delivered to Paraguay's ANDE**. Ownership and electricity entitlements are governed by the bilateral legal framework, not by an unrestricted choice of one national government or individual electricity buyer. [1][2][3]

A consequential development took place during **21–23 October 2026**. American technology company **Crusoe** and Paraguayan infrastructure developer **Araico** announced a partnership to deploy high-performance AI computing near an Itaipu substation. The initial deployment is planned for mid-2027, with a route toward **106 MW of IT capacity** and potential long-term expansion to approximately **280 MW**. Paraguay then became the twenty-sixth signatory of the American-led **Pax Silica Declaration**, linking hydroelectric power, critical minerals and artificial intelligence to a broader economic-security agenda. These announcements are documented; they are not evidence that Brazil's legal share of Itaipu has been secretly transferred to American corporations. [4][5][6]

The economically meaningful question is who controls the price, destination and productive uses of electricity and what obligations will apply to new energy-intensive customers. If 280 MW of IT equipment ran constantly for a year, that equipment alone would consume approximately **2.4528 TWh**, excluding cooling and electrical overhead. That figure is a conditional calculation, not a measured project load or approved electricity contract. Its significance is that a single digital project could become a substantial new consumer in a market shaped by international treaty rights and rapidly evolving computing demand.

## 1. Electricity security as a physical and institutional system

A country may produce abundant electricity over twelve months and still experience local blackouts because transmission equipment has failed. It can maintain large installed generating capacity yet fail to meet an evening peak if its available generation cannot be dispatched at the necessary moment. Electricity security involves resource adequacy, network reliability, flexibility, affordable costs, spare equipment and the capability to restore service after disruption.

Brazil's portfolio includes hydropower, wind, solar, thermoelectric plants and other resources with different operating characteristics. Reservoirs can provide flexibility within water and environmental constraints. Wind and solar depend on weather and time. Thermal resources can supply dispatchable generation but require fuel and financing. Batteries can support short-duration balancing without necessarily replacing large water reservoirs during prolonged drought.

Institutional coordination is essential. The National Electric System Operator coordinates Brazil's interconnected system; EPE undertakes long-term planning; ANEEL regulates critical parts of the market; public and private utilities own physical assets. Itaipu adds an international layer in which national authorities must respect a shared legal entity and negotiated entitlements.

## 2. The difference between Brazil's electricity mix and total energy mix

An electricity mix describes sources used to supply electricity. The broader energy balance includes petroleum, gas, biofuels and fuels consumed directly by factories and vehicles. Brazil may generate most of its electricity from renewable sources while remaining dependent on fossil fuels in transportation and industrial processes. Confusing the categories creates inflated claims of national energy independence.

EPE's **2026 National Energy Balance**, covering 2025, reports **86.6% renewable electricity in internal supply**, with **52.2% hydraulic participation**. Internal electricity supply reached **783.3 TWh**, including net imports of **7.4 TWh**, while final consumption was **667.8 TWh**. These are distinct accounting concepts, affected by system losses and other uses. The difference is not a reserve of electricity that can simply be allocated to new data centers. [7]

Brazil enjoys substantial low-carbon electricity advantages, but resource abundance does not guarantee cheap deliverable firm capacity at a specific site. The time profile of demand, transmission congestion and contingency requirements influence the costs of connection and reliable supply.

## 3. Formation of Brazil's electricity industry

Early Brazilian electrification developed around local cities, industrial customers, private concession operators and nearby hydroelectric sites. Growing urban populations and manufacturing forced the expansion of generation and the construction of lines connecting previously separated systems. Energy increasingly became an economic infrastructure question rather than a narrowly municipal utility service.

Twentieth-century public institutions and regional electricity companies sought to accelerate investment. Eletrobras and its subsidiaries played major roles in planning and engineering high-voltage grids and major hydroelectric works. Electricity became essential to steelmaking, transport, urbanization and the spread of consumer appliances.

Financing and technical equipment were not always available domestically. Foreign technology and capital could facilitate necessary investment while creating exposures to exchange rates and lender conditions. These dependencies were not proof that domestic actors lacked decision-making power; they were constraints that influenced the cost and timing of their choices.

## 4. The Paraná basin and geographical interdependence

The Paraná River belongs to a basin shared by Brazil, Paraguay and Argentina. Hydropower therefore intersects with navigation, downstream water use, flood management and cross-border law. A project affecting river levels and flows can impose costs or restrictions on another country even when the latter is not a shareholder in the operating company.

Itaipu's location provides access to large Brazilian power markets and is especially significant to the much smaller Paraguayan electricity system. Its creation followed earlier boundary and river-use disagreements involving the former Guaíra Falls. The engineering project required settling difficult questions of territory, legal rights and interstate cooperation.

A dam of this size is a political agreement expressed in infrastructure. Water, equipment and transmission lines cannot easily be separated from the treaties governing them. Unilateral attempts to alter important aspects of the system can carry legal, diplomatic and operational consequences.

## 5. The War of the Triple Alliance and Paraguayan national memory

The War of the Triple Alliance from 1864 to 1870 profoundly affected Paraguay's population, economy and political relations with Brazil and Argentina. Its legacy is part of the country's sense of vulnerability in relations with larger neighbors. This history does not dictate every modern negotiating position, but helps explain why control over Itaipu revenues and electricity commercialization remains politically sensitive.

Paraguay contributed to the legal and territorial basis of the shared enterprise and has a legitimate interest in using its allocation to support domestic industry. Brazil supported much of the financing and infrastructure needed to build the dam and absorb power that Paraguay initially could not use. Both facts matter to a complete historical accounting.

The structural asymmetry is considerable. Brazil possesses a larger domestic market, broader industrial capacity and more financing alternatives. Paraguay values the opportunity to convert a renewable resource into local productive activity. Sustainable cooperation requires recognizing that their optimal short-run choices will not always coincide.

## 6. The 1960s: military regimes and state-led development

Itaipu was conceived as Brazil was governed by a military dictatorship and Paraguay by Alfredo Stroessner's authoritarian regime. Large public works served industrial growth, territorial integration and the prestige of governing elites. Restrictions on democratic competition and public participation limited how environmental costs, displacement and distributive questions were debated at the outset.

Infrastructure built under dictatorship can produce genuine long-term economic benefits, but its institutional origins still matter. Engineering accomplishments do not erase deficiencies in consultation, civil liberties or the transparency of original decisions. Political history needs to describe both the productive outcomes and the conditions under which affected citizens could participate.

The broader Cold War is relevant because the United States had considerable influence in the region. Yet general American influence is not proof that Washington personally wrote, imposed or controlled the 1973 treaty. A claim of specific covert interference would require diplomatic records or other evidence particular to Itaipu.

## 7. The 1973 treaty and shared sovereignty over generation

The treaty was signed on **26 April 1973** and entered into force after ratification instruments were exchanged on **13 August 1973**. It created a binational entity with equal rights and obligations for the contracting states, supplemented by annexes governing administration, technical facilities and financial services. It is a specialized international regime, not a Brazilian license issued to Paraguay. [8][9]

The arrangement recognizes an equal entitlement to power and sets conditions for the allocation of energy not used by one party. Electricity commercialization is undertaken through national entities and contracts. The physical flow of energy can differ from the juridical fifty-fifty entitlement because actual demand, frequency conversion, network design and deliveries vary.

Consequently, a statement that Paraguay receives fifty percent of Itaipu is a statement about treaty rights rather than a claim that exactly half of annual output physically serves Paraguayan consumers. The distinction is central to Paraguay's growing interest in local demand that could reduce the surplus historically made available to Brazil.

## 8. Annexes A, B and C: how the binational arrangement works

Annex A concerns the company's institutional organization. Annex B describes its engineering facilities. **Annex C** establishes financial arrangements and the provision of electricity services. Its cost-based framework includes contracted capacity, expenses, royalties, compensation for ceded electricity and other components. A rate charged for capacity is not the same as a wholesale energy price. [8][10]

The traditional **Unit Cost of Electricity Service**, expressed in US dollars per kilowatt-month, cannot be directly compared with a data center power purchase agreement denominated in dollars per megawatt-hour. A proper conversion requires assumptions about capacity, energy output, utilization, contract duration and additional charges.

Annex C provided for review after fifty years from the treaty's entry into force, considering debt amortization and the relative volumes of contracted capacity. That milestone was reached in 2023. It did not establish a predetermined new tariff or permit one country to dictate revised conditions without an agreement.

## 9. The engineering system: twenty turbines and different frequencies

Itaipu's twenty **700 MW generating units** provide **14,000 MW of nominal installed capacity**. Portions of the generation system were designed for Paraguay's **50 Hz** network and others for Brazil's **60 Hz** network. Conversion and specialized high-voltage transmission allow the systems to exchange the power required by their different operating standards.

Installed capacity is not annual energy production. The theoretical maximum of 14 GW sustained for all **8,760 hours** in a conventional year is **122.64 TWh**, while actual 2025 generation was **72.88 TWh**. The ratio is approximately **59.4%**, a calculated annual capacity factor that should not be mistaken for mechanical availability or reliability. [2]

Even when adequate water and generating units exist, a converter or transmission failure can disrupt deliveries to one side of the system. Reliability therefore requires maintenance, redundant routes, replacement equipment, sound protective relays and coordinated operational planning across borders.

## 10. Guaíra Falls and the costs of large-scale development

Itaipu's reservoir transformed the landscape and submerged the former Guaíra or Sete Quedas falls, a site of major scenic and ecological importance. People were displaced and ecosystems altered as the governments prioritized the construction of a major electricity source. Some losses cannot be fully represented in financial accounts.

The project also created productive capacity that supported industrial activity and urban life far beyond the river valley. Royalties and local investments offered mechanisms for compensating affected territories, without making every historical impact reversible.

New data centers and transmission lines raise smaller but related distributional questions. Their economic benefits may be realized in global corporate revenue while water, land and equipment construction affect local residents. Cost-benefit assessment must identify where burdens fall rather than treating national development claims as substitutes for consultation.

## 11. Argentina and the 1979 Tripartite Agreement

Argentina became an essential diplomatic actor because it is downstream on the Paraná River. During the 1970s, Buenos Aires was concerned that the Brazil–Paraguay project would constrain Argentina's own water use and hydroelectric plans. Academic historical research describes how technical and environmental arguments became intertwined with geopolitical competition. [11]

The **Tripartite Agreement of 19 October 1979** among Brazil, Paraguay and Argentina established a framework for coordinating hydraulic uses and relevant levels and operational conditions in the common river system. Itaipu's institutional history records the accord as an important element in resolving the controversy surrounding the plant. [12]

This episode is more informative for energy security than a simplistic story of one dam dominating its neighbors. Shared basins create legitimate competing interests, and negotiated mechanisms can reduce disputes and support long-lived infrastructure.

## 12. The 1973 oil shock and a broader industrial strategy

The treaty's signing coincided with the first oil shock, which greatly increased the importance of alternative energy sources. Brazil expanded investment in hydropower, domestic petroleum development, ethanol and transport infrastructure while trying to manage import dependence and rapid industrial growth. [13]

Large infrastructure projects required long-term finance. Interest rates, currency changes and delays could materially increase obligations. Latin American countries accumulated significant external borrowing through the 1970s and encountered severe debt pressures in the following decade. [14]

This history demonstrates that energy independence cannot be pursued through construction volume alone. Secure electricity must remain financially sustainable, otherwise debt burdens and tariff distortions may constrain the maintenance and investment necessary for future security.

## 13. Itaipu's debt and the dispute over financial fairness

An influential Columbia Center on Sustainable Investment study described the project's debt financing, including a substantial Eletrobras loan in 1975 and smaller international equipment-related facilities. Currency adjustments, interest and the long amortization profile influenced the cost of electricity and debates over the division of benefits. [15]

Paraguayan perspectives emphasize the unequal economic advantages of the initial consumption and financing arrangement. Brazilian arguments highlight the construction risks borne by larger financial institutions and the provision of a market for electricity not originally demanded in Paraguay. The two narratives often refer to different kinds of cost and benefit.

Paying down construction debt before and around the 2023 review milestone changed the financial context. It did not eliminate operating expenses, maintenance, environmental obligations, reinvestment or prospective transmission upgrades. The phrase “the debt is paid” cannot rationally be interpreted as “the power is free.”

## 14. Democratization and the changing legitimacy of the treaty

Both Brazil and Paraguay eventually emerged from military rule, opening more space for parliaments, civil society and the press to debate the bilateral arrangement. Claims about unequal revenue distribution and industrial opportunities gained greater public visibility. The binational framework survived, but its political justification had to respond to changed democratic expectations.

Paraguay's increasing electricity demand gradually reduced the previous imbalance between its treaty entitlement and domestic consumption. The opportunity cost of ceding surplus became more important as electricity-intensive businesses explored local investment. Brazil diversified its generation through wind, solar and other resources, although Itaipu remained crucial to certain regions and the wider grid.

A treaty signed for a very different industrial era may require adjustment, but renegotiation also brings risks. Stability depends on keeping operational coordination intact while addressing legitimate claims from both governments.

## 15. Lula and Fernando Lugo: the renegotiation of 2009–2011

In 2009, presidents **Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva** and **Fernando Lugo** reached an understanding about compensation for Paraguayan electricity not consumed domestically. Brazil's Congress approved the necessary measures in 2011, tripling a widely cited annual amount from about **US$120 million to US$360 million** under the arrangements discussed at that time. Both legislative records and Itaipu's official history document the decision. [16][17]

For Paraguay, the increase was an assertion of economic rights and a means of securing a greater share of benefits. For Brazil, it reflected an assessment that regional partnership and diplomatic stability were worth financial concessions. The distribution of resulting costs to final consumers was a significant part of the political debate.

Historical dollar amounts must be interpreted by year, legal basis and compensation category. They cannot be compared with a contemporary AI data center's electricity costs as though all represented the same type of payment. The broader strategic question concerns whether public and private energy revenues help build lasting production and welfare.

## 16. The 2023 fifty-year review of Annex C

The treaty reached its fiftieth anniversary of entry into force in August 2023, triggering the scheduled review of Annex C. Itaipu's official statements make clear that the governments, rather than the plant's technical staff alone, bear responsibility for political negotiation. The company provides operational and financial advice. [9]

The economic environment has changed materially since 1973. Brazil's system is more diverse, Paraguay's domestic market has expanded, the original debt profile has substantially run its course and digital technologies are creating new categories of demand. Negotiations cover compensation, royalties, service costs and future commercialization rules.

The treaty review should not be presented as an automatic unrestricted international auction of all Paraguayan energy. It remains subject to bilateral legal instruments, electricity-system requirements and the agreed distribution of rights.

## 17. The 2024–2026 tariff and competing national priorities

In 2024, Brazil and Paraguay agreed on a service-cost reference of **US$19.28 per kilowatt-month** for **2024, 2025 and 2026**. Paraguayan authorities emphasized royalties, compensation and social investment resources. Brazilian authorities emphasized maintaining affordable electricity and developing a longer-term framework that could change commercialization from 2027 onward. [18][19]

Paraguay has a legitimate interest in extracting greater economic value from its electricity, especially when domestic firms can now use more of it. Brazil has a legitimate interest in cost-effective power for households and industry and in preserving dependable supplies. These priorities are not automatically compatible.

The treaty's unit cost is not a quoted rate for Crusoe's proposed computing campus. Calculating its actual cost would require the separate Paraguayan retail or contracted tariffs, transmission charges, loss factors, taxes, firm-capacity commitments and any incentives negotiated with the ANDE.

## 18. The March 2026 negotiations and unresolved contractual terms

On **19 March 2026**, foreign ministers **Mauro Vieira** and **Rubén Ramírez Lezcano** met in Asunción and reported progress on Annex C. Paraguay's foreign ministry explicitly stated that it was premature to announce the future tariff before the negotiators completed their agreement. [20][21]

Political statements about lower prices or market opening cannot substitute for the eventual binding text. Assertions that a particular energy price will apply in 2027 must therefore distinguish a proposal, an interim rate and an approved final arrangement.

Prospective market reforms also interact with transmission rights, existing contracts and the technical roles of ANDE, ENBPar and grid operators. Additional flexibility will not erase engineering constraints or the need for predictable binational coordination.

## 19. The 2025 Brazilian espionage controversy

In April **2025**, Paraguay recalled its ambassador and suspended Itaipu-related talks following Brazil's acknowledgment that Brazilian intelligence had conducted surveillance against Paraguayan officials between **June 2022 and March 2023**. Lula's administration said the operation had been stopped after the government learned of it. The reported objectives and methods became subjects of political and investigative scrutiny. [22]

This is a specific episode of documented interference by **Brazilian actors against Paraguay**, rather than an established US operation. It demonstrates that the value of energy-negotiation information can motivate actions that severely damage confidence between governments.

The episode does not prove that the 2026 American–Paraguayan computing agreement involved cyber espionage or treaty violations. Those are separate claims requiring their own evidence. The historical precedent is relevant to institutional safeguards and diplomacy, not an invitation to presume wrongdoing in unrelated transactions.

## 20. American influence: from historical context to documented 2026 involvement

The United States exercised substantial political and economic influence in Latin America during the twentieth century, including during the Cold War. Finance, corporate technology and regional diplomacy affected national development strategies. Its general role is relevant background to the history of Brazil and Paraguay.

Still, structural influence is not equivalent to proof that Washington drafted Itaipu's tariff provisions or ordered a specific energy decision in 1973. Such an allegation requires records about the particular transaction. The treaty itself is a bilateral instrument between Brazil and Paraguay.

The 2026 evidence is more concrete: a senior American diplomatic visit, a publicly announced computing partnership and Paraguay's entry into a US-backed strategic-technology declaration. Influence can be examined through investment, contracts and institutional commitments without inventing covert activities.

## 21. Christopher Landau's visit and investment diplomacy

US Deputy Secretary of State **Christopher Landau** visited Paraguay during **19–21 October 2026**, accompanied by announcements concerning digital infrastructure, minerals and other economic initiatives. Paraguay's public news service reported the Crusoe–Araico project, along with discussions of uranium investment and the initiation of talks about civilian nuclear cooperation. [23]

The official description portrayed the American relationship as an alternative to financing attributed to geopolitical competitors. This is evidence of strategic framing by the parties, not proof that the offered financing is automatically advantageous. Actual economic benefits depend on interest rates, procurement, local suppliers, taxes and enforceable obligations.

Brazil was not a named partner in the private announcement. That fact warrants scrutiny of treaty compliance and the allocation of electricity, but does not by itself establish infringement of Brazil's share. Paraguay's national authority to develop domestic businesses and customers remains relevant.

## 22. The industrial meaning of the Crusoe–Araico project

Crusoe announced on **22 October 2026** its strategic partnership with Araico for AI computing infrastructure powered by Itaipu. It described a location near an electrical substation, services to US and Latin American customers and an expected initial operating phase in mid-2027. [4]

The company distinguished an expansion path toward **106 MW of IT power** from a longer-term option of approximately **280 MW**. These figures should never be presented as an installed or metered load as of the announcement date. IT power also differs from the site's total power requirement, which includes cooling, conversion and supporting equipment.

The location may provide employment, construction and engineering contracts in Paraguay, while the cloud operator may hold high-value software, customer relationships and processing technology abroad. The distribution of value is determined by corporate agreements, tax structures and the extent of local participation, not the nominal size of the electrical connection alone.

## 23. Pax Silica as a new form of energy geopolitics

On **23 October 2026**, Paraguay became the **twenty-sixth signatory** to the **Pax Silica Declaration** at the US Department of State. Paraguay's foreign ministry described a shared commitment to leverage **critical minerals and hydroelectric electricity** for advanced AI infrastructure in the Americas. [5]

The agreement's policy language links electricity, semiconductors, digital hardware and advanced computing to national economic security. It suggests a strategic shift: renewable electricity from smaller states can support international AI infrastructure, making energy contracts part of technology diplomacy.

The signature does not change ownership of Itaipu or create a military alliance against Brazil. To assess its consequences, observers must examine subsequent financing, investment screening, procurement and digital-security commitments. A declaration establishes political direction; execution remains a separate evidentiary question.

## 24. Quantifying the hypothetical 280 MW electricity load

Power is measured in megawatts; annual energy is power multiplied by time. **280 MW** operated continuously for **8,760 hours** would consume **2,452,800 MWh**, or **2.4528 TWh**. A **106 MW** block operated under the same assumption would consume **0.92856 TWh**. These figures represent conditional arithmetic, not observed demand or a confirmed energy purchase agreement. [4]

A data center's **Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE)** compares whole-facility electricity with IT electricity. At a hypothetical PUE of **1.20**, a 280 MW IT load would correspond to **336 MW** of whole-facility demand and approximately **2.94336 TWh** per year if sustained continuously. Actual load depends on installed hardware, utilization, efficiency and cooling conditions.

Relative to Itaipu's actual 2025 generation of approximately **72.88 TWh**, the servers-only case equals **3.37%**, and the hypothetical whole-campus case **4.04%**. This comparison establishes an order of magnitude, not a claim that the investment will reduce Brazilian entitlement by that fraction.

## 25. When hydroelectric exports become imported digital services

Low-cost electricity can attract manufacturers or data centers. The benefits retained by the host country depend on employment quality, tax receipts, procurement, knowledge transfer and the location where resulting goods or services are sold. AI facilities may involve tremendous capital expenditures while requiring relatively few permanent employees per megawatt.

Paraguay could benefit through local construction, electrical engineering, logistics, technical training and domestic software providers. Alternatively, it could become primarily a location where imported computing equipment uses subsidized power to sell services billed elsewhere. Similar choices confront Brazil under its domestic data-center policies.

The relevant opportunity cost is the value of electricity under realistic alternative uses: what other industries or consumers could purchase it, what network investments are required and who receives the resulting gains? “Digital sovereignty” cannot be demonstrated merely by locating foreign-owned processors near a hydropower station.
