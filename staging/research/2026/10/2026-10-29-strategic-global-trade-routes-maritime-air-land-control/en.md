# The world's strategic trade routes: who controls maritime, air and land corridors, what they carry and why they face growing pressure

**29 October 2026 · Marginal Thinking · Global System Power · Historical and geoeconomic investigation**

Global power is visible in the movement of ships, aircraft, trains and trucks. A country may produce oil, food, semiconductors or medicines while depending on foreign ports, locks, insurers, navigation services, airspace, roads or digital platforms to convert that output into export revenue. International trade does not flow across a homogeneous surface. It is concentrated in corridors, narrow passages, terminals and institutional interfaces whose disruptions can reach many distant markets simultaneously.

UNCTAD estimates that maritime transport carries **over 80% of international merchandise trade by volume**. Vessel rerouting raised the industry's **ton-miles by 5.9% in 2024**, nearly three times the 2.2% expansion of seaborne cargo volume. The distinction matters: the global system required more voyages, fuel, working capital and effective fleet capacity to move comparatively little additional physical cargo. Airfreight uses a different business model: less weight, much higher value per kilogram, expensive capacity and a premium for delivery speed. Roads and railways remain indispensable for continental supply chains and access to seaports, even though they cannot replace oceans for most long-haul bulk commodities. [1][2][12]

“Who controls a trade route?” has several separate answers: **coastal states** exercising jurisdiction; **public and private operators** of canals, ports and railways; **shipping lines** deciding capacity and schedules; **insurers and financiers** setting economic conditions; **governments and armed groups** capable of disrupting access; and **commodity buyers** determining cargo destinations. Owning a terminal in a foreign port does not automatically confer sovereignty over the waterway. Deploying naval vessels does not change international rights of transit. A container handling concession does not grant an operator control over all cargo crossing the host country.

A sound comparison must distinguish **metric tonnes, trade value, container TEUs, ship deadweight tonnage, tonne-miles, oil barrels per day, air cargo tonne-kilometres and shipping-connectivity index points**. These are incompatible denominators. One passage may dominate in petroleum while another is essential for container services. A small airfreight route may be vital for semiconductors. A road carrying relatively little international trade can nevertheless be essential to domestic food distribution.

## 1. The global map is a network of unequal dependencies

A trade corridor consists of nodes and links. Nodes include seaports, terminals, railway stations, airports, inland distribution centers and customs crossings. Links include oceans, straits, canals, roads, tracks and communication networks. Corridor performance depends on its least capable link and the operational compatibility of its nodes. An efficient port can remain commercially constrained if the railway or customs service behind it is congested.

Strategic importance is not identical to absolute traffic volume. It rises when **substitutability is low**, a passage concentrates an essential input or nearby alternatives are insufficient. Hormuz is particularly important for petroleum and gas; Malacca connects the industrial Asian system to the Indian Ocean; Suez and Panama reduce travel distances; the English Channel and Rhine–Danube axis serve closely integrated manufacturers. Air hubs handle goods whose economic value can justify expensive transportation.

For disruption risk, the more useful variable is how much **critical cargo cannot be rerouted within the commercially necessary time**, multiplied by financing costs and production losses. A tanker may accept thousands of additional nautical miles while a refrigerated medical shipment or irreplaceable industrial component cannot wait for a slower service.

## 2. Ancient Mediterranean, Nile and Bronze Age trading networks

Trade geography predates industrial globalization. Eastern Mediterranean, Egyptian, Phoenician and Mesopotamian networks connected copper, tin, timber, grain, textiles and metals. Bronze production relied on inputs located in different regions, creating dependence on caravan routes and coastal shipping. Their paths changed as rulers, taxes, warfare and merchant security changed.

The Mediterranean linked agricultural basins, commercial cities and administrative centers. Possessing a port was not enough without reliable inland roads, warehouses, standards, protection and credit. Roman roads and harbors strengthened regional integration, but the empire financed maintenance and imposed security through coercive political power.

The lasting lesson is that merchants rarely choose routes solely by geographic distance. They select corridors where **security, infrastructure and legal costs** compensate for physical exposure. The same principle governs today's choice between Suez, the Cape of Good Hope and Eurasian freight rail.

## 3. Indian Ocean monsoons and pre-European networks

Long before European imperial expansion, Arab, Indian, Persian, East African and Southeast Asian mariners used seasonal monsoon winds to connect the Indian Ocean. Pepper, cloves, textiles, ceramics, horses, metals and valuable stones circulated through multiple commercial communities rather than a single capital. Ports near the Gulf, East African coast, subcontinent and Malay straits served as intermediaries.

Weather shaped voyage timing, warehousing and trade credit. Merchants developed knowledge of local prices, law and sailing seasons. Malacca became a major interface between the Indian Ocean and East Asian seas. Its present importance derives from physical geography and accumulated commercial infrastructure over many centuries.

European maritime imperialism changed these networks but did not invent them. Military fortresses, monopolies and colonial fees were superimposed on existing Asian and African routes. An account that makes Europeans the sole originators of Asian integration misrepresents the earlier trading system.

## 4. The Silk Roads were never a single highway

The term Silk Road describes changing combinations of inland and maritime connections among China, Central Asia, Persia, India and Mediterranean markets. Silk and porcelain are famous commodities, but horses, metals, pigments, food, technologies, religions and information also traveled. Routes shifted with dynastic stability, oasis control, taxation, disease and warfare.

Periods of political integration, including Mongol expansion, supported long-distance exchange, while conflicts elsewhere moved traffic toward other passages. The useful historical analogy is not one road that can simply be recreated in 2026. It is **a system of overlapping corridors** requiring cooperation among many authorities.

China's contemporary Belt and Road infrastructure initiatives draw on Silk Road imagery, but they are modern financing, investment and trade strategies. The image cannot establish uninterrupted territorial control extending from ancient China to modern European ports. Individual contracts, assets and national authorities must be examined.

## 5. Portugal, the Cape of Good Hope and colonial commodity flows

Vasco da Gama's voyage to India at the end of the fifteenth century demonstrated direct sea links between Europe and Asia around the Cape of Good Hope. Access to spices without relying on the same Mediterranean intermediaries was central to the commercial motivation. The Portuguese Crown combined oceanic navigation with naval force and coastal trading posts.

The Cape remains an economically meaningful alternative when the Red Sea or Suez route is insecure. Yet geographical substitution has financial consequences: a longer route does not instantly create more ships, crews, bunker supply or loading capacity at the necessary terminals.

Atlantic colonial commerce moved sugar, tobacco, metals, enslaved people and later industrial manufactures. Its wealth was deeply unequal and relied on systematic violence against enslaved and colonized populations. Any history of trade routes must consider human costs alongside innovations in navigation and commercial finance.

## 6. Steam power, railways and industrial synchronization

Steamships lowered reliance on wind, improved scheduling and made coal supply stations strategic assets. Railways connected inland producers to seaports, and telegraphs accelerated trade and price information. Factories and expanding urban populations required predictable shipments of energy and raw materials.

Industrial supply chains became less tolerant of delay because plants relied on recurring deliveries. A reliable weekly service may be more economically valuable than one unusually fast sailing. Standardized containers, larger ports and precision scheduling later amplified the need for coordination between different modes.

Infrastructure brought wider markets and lower costs for many products while also strengthening colonial control, external debt and concentration of operating profits. Technical productivity and political distribution cannot be evaluated as though they were the same question.

## 7. Suez in 1869: infrastructure changes the geography of empire

The **Suez Canal opened in 1869**, connecting the Mediterranean and Red Seas and shortening many Europe–Asia voyages. Construction involved French and Egyptian interests, coerced labor in important phases, public debt and growing European influence over Egypt. A new commercial shortcut transformed the strategic status of Egyptian territory.

Britain's 1882 occupation and the canal's role in imperial connectivity showed how civilian infrastructure could become a military and diplomatic objective. Gamal Abdel Nasser's **1956 nationalization** triggered the Suez Crisis involving Egypt, Britain, France and Israel, and exposed the limits of European colonial power during the Cold War.

Closure following the 1967 war pushed ships around southern Africa for years and encouraged economies of large tankers. Reopening in 1975 illustrated the long-term commercial impact of losing the shortest route even when an alternative ocean passage exists.

## 8. Panama: construction, sovereignty and the dispute over concessions

The **Panama Canal began operating in 1914** after a construction project involving locks, excavations, disease control and extensive geopolitical intervention. Its history is tied to Panama's separation from Colombia and substantial twentieth-century US control of the Canal Zone. The **1977 Torrijos–Carter Treaties** established a transition culminating in Panama's full administrative responsibility on **31 December 1999**.

The **Panama Canal Authority (ACP)** now manages the passage as a Panamanian institution. Foreign investment in nearby container terminals is not ownership of the canal or a transfer of Panamanian sovereignty. Canal neutrality has specific treaty foundations, which require interpretation according to their texts rather than political slogans.

The enlargement completed in 2016 enabled Neopanamax ships and changed some US and Asian trade patterns. Freshwater availability nevertheless makes drought an enduring threat to operations. In **fiscal 2026, the ACP reported 13,951 transits**, compared with **13,404 in fiscal 2025**, and **521 million PC/UMS tons**, compared with 489.2 million. PC/UMS is a specific canal tonnage measure, not the physical mass of cargo shipped. [3]

## 9. The container revolution and power of shipping lines

Modern containerization gained momentum with commercial deployments in the United States during 1956 and subsequent standardization. The system reduced handling time, theft and losses and allowed a single unit to move from truck to rail to ship. It enabled more extensive international production networks with scheduled intermediate-goods deliveries.

A **TEU** represents one twenty-foot equivalent unit of container capacity. It is neither one metric tonne nor proof that a particular container is loaded. Vessel capacity, cargo mass, international trade value and number of port calls are distinct concepts.

Containerization strengthened companies that control schedules, vessel slots and routing. UNCTAD recorded MSC at approximately **20% of global container-fleet TEU capacity in April 2025** and analyzed the Gemini Cooperation, Premier Alliance and Ocean Alliance arrangements. That percentage is not twenty percent of every maritime cargo category, nor does an alliance gain legal sovereignty over an international strait. [4]

## 10. Law of the sea and the limits of absolute control

The **1982 United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea**, in force since 1994, established a framework for territorial seas, exclusive economic zones, the high seas and passage through straits used for international navigation. Coastal jurisdiction and internationally protected navigation can coexist under different legal regimes. A coast guard's operational strength does not create private ownership over a global passage. [5]

**Transit passage** through relevant international straits and **innocent passage** in other circumstances are not interchangeable legal categories. Coastal states can exercise applicable environmental and security powers, but proximity to a waterway does not imply unrestricted authority to prohibit every commercial voyage. Specific disputes and treaty provisions require specific legal analysis.

Law alone is not enough for commercial operations. A merchant ship may enjoy a navigational right yet face unaffordable insurance, missile threats or lack of port services. **Legal permission to navigate** and **economic viability of sailing** are distinct conditions.

## 11. How to measure strategic trade-route importance

A route's importance has several dimensions. Cargo weight matters for grains and ores. Cargo value and urgency matter for chips, instruments and pharmaceuticals. Distance matters when closure forces thousands of additional miles. Substitutability matters when buyers lack alternative suppliers or infrastructure.

Contractual and institutional risk also matter: customs processing, sanctions, tariffs, arbitration and schedule reliability. Energy and food dependence create security externalities beyond freight charges. A huge harbor with alternatives may be less critical for some products than a smaller passage with no viable bypass.

UNCTAD measures seaborne trade and maritime connectivity; EIA estimates petroleum movements; IATA reports cargo tonne-kilometres; the World Bank studies transport cost and logistics; canal authorities publish their own metrics. Their datasets complement one another but do not produce a credible single numeric ranking of who “controls global trade.”

## 12. Strait of Hormuz: energy at the edge of the Gulf

Hormuz connects the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman and Indian Ocean between Iran and the Arabian Peninsula. It is a crucial exit for regional petroleum and gas, with Asian economies among the leading destinations. The corridor is exposed to physical disruption, insurance repricing, military escalation and shifts in the ability to ship.

EIA estimated approximately **20.9 million barrels of oil and petroleum liquids per day through Hormuz in the first half of 2025**, plus **11.4 billion cubic feet per day of LNG**. About 89% of crude and condensate traffic went to Asia; China, India, Japan and South Korea were particularly important destinations. US import dependence on that exact route was smaller than the exposure of major Asian importers. [6]

These figures must not be carried forward as 2026 observations. EIA's subsequent quarterly estimates recorded **14.9 million barrels/day in 2026 Q1** and **4.9 million barrels/day in Q2** under regional energy disruptions. They describe estimated actual flows in those windows, not a permanent change in the physical carrying capacity of the strait. [7]

## 13. Oil pipelines only partly bypass Hormuz

Saudi Arabia operates pipelines connecting eastern oil-producing areas to the Red Sea, and the United Arab Emirates uses infrastructure toward Fujairah outside the narrow Gulf exit. EIA estimated roughly **4.7 million barrels/day** of combined bypass capability for major Saudi and UAE facilities under a specified disruption scenario. The figure is a conditional capacity estimate, not a guarantee of fully available incremental flows every day. [6]

Rerouting oil requires pipeline capacity, operational compatibility, terminals and tankers at alternate loading locations. A ship sailing from a Red Sea outlet may encounter a different bottleneck near Bab el-Mandeb. A bypass of one chokepoint can therefore introduce reliance on another.

Refineries are also configured for particular crude characteristics. Replacing a missing barrel of one quality with a barrel of another can require technical and contractual adjustment. Aggregate global oil supply may look adequate while some facilities face a deficit of a suitable input.

## 14. Malacca and Singapore: the factory system's ocean connector

The Strait of Malacca between the Malay Peninsula and Sumatra connects the Indian Ocean to the Singapore and South China Sea routes. It links Gulf, Indian and African energy exports with industrial centers in China, Japan, Korea and Southeast Asia. Its cargoes include oil, LNG, containers, chemicals and numerous manufacturing inputs.

EIA estimated **23.2 million barrels/day of petroleum liquids through Malacca in the first half of 2025**, above the corresponding Hormuz figure. In **2026 Q2** the estimate was **16.6 million barrels/day**, compared with **23.8 million in 2025 Q2**, reflecting changes in global energy routes. Neither oil figure measures all container traffic. [6][7]

Indonesia, Malaysia and Singapore exercise distinct coastal and security responsibilities. Shipping companies schedule the vessels; Singapore's transshipment and maritime-finance services provide commercial influence, not sovereignty over neighboring coasts. Alternatives through Sunda and Lombok involve distance, depth, infrastructure and economic constraints.

## 15. The South China Sea and contested maritime claims

The South China Sea links Malacca with Chinese, Vietnamese, Philippine and other East Asian ports. It is indispensable for container services, imported fuels, industrial components and raw materials. Disputes over islands and maritime zones involve China and multiple Southeast Asian states under conflicting legal and political positions.

Claims to islands and resources are not equivalent to a lawful power to terminate international navigation across an entire sea. The law of the sea and merchant decisions remain relevant. Naval and coast-guard presence can change commercial risk perceptions without changing the text of international law.

Severe disruption would hit economies deeply integrated into intra-Asian supply chains. Some cargo could be redirected, but alternative passages involve more time, fuel, port capacity and schedules. Electronics plants with low inventories face different consequences than coal producers able to tolerate longer shipments.

## 16. Taiwan, Bashi Channel and western-Pacific industrial risk

The waters around Taiwan and the **Bashi Channel** between Taiwan and northern Philippines matter for commercial navigation, energy deliveries and connections to the open Pacific. A crisis could affect container and tanker routes and have far-reaching consequences for semiconductor production centered on Taiwan.

**Shipping risk** and **factory disruption** are different mechanisms. Ships might reroute while interruptions of electricity, precision equipment or factory operations cause chip shortages that no maritime detour can repair. Strategic importance combines geography, manufacturing concentration and the time needed to replace production capacity.

A military scenario should not be described as an event that has already occurred. Its supporting signals would include official exclusion zones, navigational warnings, insurance withdrawals, merchant rerouting and verified industrial shutdowns.

## 17. Suez and Bab el-Mandeb: two gates to one corridor

The Egyptian state and its **Suez Canal Authority** govern canal operations and transit revenue. **Bab el-Mandeb**, between Yemen and the Horn of Africa, connects the Red Sea to the Gulf of Aden under other coastal jurisdictions. A Europe–Indian Ocean voyage depends on both segments: the value of the Egyptian shortcut falls if the southern sea entrance becomes commercially unsafe.

Attacks on merchant vessels by Houthi forces from late 2023 contributed to large-scale ship rerouting. UNCTAD reported **Suez tonnage in May 2025 remained approximately 70% below 2023 levels**. The canal authority reported **12,758 vessels crossing in 2025**, compared with **13,213 in 2024**. The two sources use different periods and measurements and cannot be combined into one global cargo percentage. [1][8]

The Cape diversion increases distance and can change effective fleet supply and port economies. An operationally open canal can still see limited traffic when insurers and shipowners avoid Bab el-Mandeb. Physical availability, commercial schedules and risk appetite therefore have to be measured separately.

## 18. The Cape of Good Hope and the cost of an alternative

Routing ships around southern Africa avoids Suez and Bab el-Mandeb and can make sense for security, ship-size or customer reasons. The Cape is not a canal controlled by one administration; it is an oceanic route. South Africa governs its ports and coastal jurisdiction, not all Atlantic–Indian Ocean commerce.

EIA estimated **9.1 million barrels/day** of oil and petroleum liquids around the Cape in **the first half of 2025**, and **9.4 million barrels/day** in **2026 Q2**. These are petroleum-route measures that may overlap with other chokepoint statistics; they are not a separate share of total global cargo to add mechanically to Hormuz or Malacca. [6][7]

Longer voyages tie up ships for more days per delivered cargo. If one round trip requires more time, the same fleet completes fewer cycles annually. Freight rates may rise even if the physical tonnage shipped remains stable, which is why ton-miles and lead times are essential to understand rerouting.

## 19. Gibraltar, the English Channel and Euro-Atlantic logistics

The **Strait of Gibraltar** links the Mediterranean and Atlantic between Spain and Morocco, with Gibraltar's distinct British territorial position nearby. It serves container, energy, grain and industrial flows between maritime systems. The **English Channel and Dover Strait** connect dense trading regions across Britain and continental Europe.

Freight ferries, coastal vessels, the Channel Tunnel and connected roads and railways support food, automobiles, parts and consumer products. Brexit-related customs and sanitary procedures changed documentation and border costs for many shipments. A border processing delay can be economically significant even when water navigation is safe.

Control is spread across maritime authorities, customs, port firms, ferry operators and national legal rules. A commercially open passage need not be frictionless for perishable goods. The relevant risks combine weather, congestion, regulatory procedures and service capacity.

## 20. Bosporus, Dardanelles and Black Sea access

The Turkish Straits connect the Black Sea, Sea of Marmara and Mediterranean. Türkiye has central jurisdiction, and the **1936 Montreux Convention** defines special conditions for merchant shipping and naval vessels. Rules applicable to warships should not be inaccurately imposed on ordinary peacetime merchant movements.

Oil, refined products, grains and manufactured goods from Black Sea states depend on the route. Russia's war against Ukraine affected port access, insurance, export facilities and grain availability. The consequences extend to food-importing states far from the conflict.

EIA estimated petroleum-liquids flows of **3.7 million barrels/day in 2025 Q2** and **4.1 million in 2026 Q2**. These are petroleum statistics and do not count grain exports or total vessels. The strategic importance also comes from the limited substitutes for a maritime connection to the Black Sea. [7]

## 21. Danish Straits and the Baltic economy

The Danish Straits provide access between the Baltic Sea and the North Sea, serving energy, manufactured goods and northern European ports. Denmark, Sweden and neighboring states exercise territorial and operational responsibilities under international rules. Ice, draft requirements, navigational procedures and security conditions shape transit costs.

EIA recorded **4.9 million barrels/day** of oil liquids passing the Danish Straits in the first half of 2025 and **4.7 million in 2026 Q2**. The comparatively smaller change contrasts with major shifts through Hormuz but still matters for the Baltic energy supply chain. [6][7]

This region also contains energy and communications installations. Damage to a submarine cable is not synonymous with closing the passage to ships. Investigations must distinguish accidents, technical defects, deliberate interference and the restoration costs for each asset.

## 22. Ports, transshipment and the power to choose network calls

Shanghai, Ningbo-Zhoushan, Singapore, Busan, Qingdao, Rotterdam, Antwerp-Bruges, Jebel Ali, Los Angeles and Long Beach perform different roles: industrial origin, transshipment, global services, inland market access or consumer distribution. Their strategic relevance depends on the number and quality of links to other ports and inland routes.

UNCTAD's **third-quarter 2026 port liner shipping connectivity index** reported **Shanghai 2,439**, **Ningbo-Zhoushan 2,110**, **Singapore 1,883**, **Busan 1,655** and **Qingdao 1,422**. These are **index points, not TEUs, metric tonnes or revenue**. The concentration of highly connected ports in East Asia reflects the region's industrial and logistics infrastructure. [9]

Port control involves multiple levels. Governments exercise jurisdiction and set rules. Public or private operators manage terminals under contracts. Customs authorities clear merchandise, and shipping lines decide schedules. Deepwater access and fast cranes matter, but integration with domestic rail, roads and distribution centers is equally crucial.

## 23. Shipowners, flags, insurers and lenders shape the network

One ship may be owned in one country, registered under another flag, chartered by a third party and insured in another financial center. Registration does not reliably identify the final economic owner. UNCTAD describes significant vessel-owning capacity associated with Greece, China and Japan, alongside major open registries such as Liberia, Panama and the Marshall Islands. [1]

Insurance premiums may rise with war risk; banks finance vessels and cargo inventories; governments control sanctions, export licenses and payment systems. International institutions enforce shipping safety and environmental standards. These instruments distribute commercial power, but none individually equals sovereignty over a narrow sea passage.

IMO recorded **171 cases of piracy and armed robbery, attempted or completed, during 2025**, a 17% increase on 2024, including **122 reported incidents around the Straits of Malacca and Singapore**. Raw incident counts are not per-vessel probabilities: that calculation would require a comparable denominator of voyages and definitions of exposure. [10]

## 24. Product categories explain why strategic weights differ

Crude oil and refined products are important to Hormuz, Malacca, Suez, Cape routes and Turkish and Danish straits. LNG travels from Gulf and other producers in specialized ships and requires liquefaction, regasification and contract capacity. Grains and fertilizers move mainly as bulk cargoes, often with freight costs affecting food production and consumer prices.

Containers carry machinery, electronics, automotive parts, textiles, processed foods and many kinds of industrial inputs. Air cargo specializes in valuable or urgent items: semiconductors, medicines, medical equipment, precision instruments and time-sensitive spare parts. Land networks connect factories, farms and consumers and often operate at high delivery frequency.

Food-security risks depend on how freight affects final prices, how much import dependence exists for fertilizers and how long stocks will last. A commodity may have alternative physical suppliers, but renegotiating contracts, charters and inventories can take weeks or months.

## 25. Asia–Europe, trans-Pacific and transatlantic shipping

Asia–Europe container services traditionally connect East Asia through Malacca, the Indian Ocean, Bab el-Mandeb and Suez, with a Cape alternative. Trans-Pacific services link East Asian industrial hubs to North American ports and, depending on the journey, to eastern destinations via Panama or intermodal rail. North Atlantic routes join European and American manufacturing and consumer markets.

Actual costs are determined by terminal capacity, carrier schedules, inland rail, trucking and port tariffs rather than nautical distance alone. A nearby substitute port will not eliminate delays if its connecting freight trains or warehouses are already full.

Commercial planning must balance **least-cost normal operations** and **redundancy under disruption**. Multiple carriers, safety stocks and alternative gateways cost money. Resilience is an investment that must exist before a crisis; it cannot be assumed to appear without capacity constraints when disruption begins.

## 26. The 2026 oil shock requires consistent time windows

EIA quarterly petroleum-liquids estimates show dramatic changes between **2025 Q2 and 2026 Q2**: Hormuz **21.0 to 4.9 million barrels/day**; Malacca **23.8 to 16.6**; Suez and SUMED **5.1 to 5.8**; Bab el-Mandeb **4.5 to 8.1**; Panama **2.5 to 3.2**; Cape of Good Hope **9.3 to 9.4**. The figures must be read with production changes, alternative routes and overlapping traversals in mind. [7]

Hormuz flows fell by approximately **76.7%** and Malacca by **30.3%** when comparing their respective Q2 observations. The calculation concerns estimated oil liquids transported, not a share of world merchandise, the number of containers or continuous physical closure of the waterway.

## 27. How war, congestion and climate affect final prices

A transport disruption may raise costs in three stages. The ship or aircraft consumes more fuel and time; financing increases as goods spend more days in transit; industrial production can halt if a necessary component fails to arrive. The last mechanism may impose losses far above the original freight charge.

Pass-through is uneven. Businesses with abundant inventories may absorb weeks of delay. Just-in-time manufacturing or strict cold-chain operations may suffer disruption much sooner. Insurance, freight adjustment clauses and contract terms determine the initial allocation of cost, which may change in later negotiations.

A substitute route must be evaluated by effective capacity rather than by its mere existence on a map. Railway freight cannot necessarily move the same millions of tonnes of bulk ore at the same unit cost as an ocean carrier. Air cargo can rescue small urgent shipments, not replace the total capacity of oceangoing bulk fleets.

## 28. Air cargo and the economics of high-value products

Air transport does not compete with bulk carriers to move tens of millions of tonnes at low cost. It offers time, reliability and reduced inventory for valuable, urgent or perishable goods. Pharmaceuticals, semiconductors, aerospace components, medical instruments, computing equipment and some fresh products can justify much higher transport cost per kilogram.

IATA reported that global air cargo demand grew **3.4% in 2025** measured in **cargo tonne-kilometres (CTK)**, with capacity in available tonne-kilometres up 3.7%. The measure incorporates both mass and distance, not simply tonnes or trade value. Cargo also travels in passenger aircraft bellies, so canceled passenger flights can reduce effective freight capacity. [11]

Air logistics depends on fuel prices, landing slots, pilots, air traffic controls, sanctions and overflight risk. A longer route adds fuel, crew hours and may reduce cargo payload. Detours also depend on legal overflight permissions and access to suitable airports.

## 29. Major airfreight axes: Asia–North America, Asia–Europe and intra-Asia

IATA's 2025 distribution of cargo tonne-kilometres put **Asia–North America at 23.4% of the global market**, **Europe–Asia at 21.5%**, **Europe–North America at 13.5%**, **within Asia at 7.4%**, and **Middle East–Asia at 7.4%**. These are CTK market shares, not countries' export values or airport tonnage. [11]

In August 2026, year-on-year demand rose **13.2% on Asia–North America**, **3.1% on Europe–Asia**, and **6.1% within Asia**. At the same time, IATA recorded contractions of **12.1% on Europe–Middle East** and **11.0% on Middle East–Asia** amid regional disruption. An August reading is not an observation for all of October. ## 30. Who controls air routes? States, airlines and traffic services

The **1944 Chicago Convention** establishes that every state has **complete and exclusive sovereignty over the airspace above its territory**. There is no equivalent of unrestricted high-seas navigation through another state's territorial airspace. Overflight agreements, air traffic management and national restrictions determine lawful operations. [13]

A state may close airspace for safety under relevant international obligations, and an airline may avoid a zone even without a formal prohibition. Aviation regulators, air traffic controllers and meteorological services affect commercial access. An airport may serve as a hub for a foreign airline without transferring territorial sovereignty.

Export controls add another layer. An aircraft capable of carrying advanced computing equipment may lack legal permission to transport certain chips to a destination. Control of air cargo therefore includes goods classification, technology authorization and payment as well as the legal right to land.
