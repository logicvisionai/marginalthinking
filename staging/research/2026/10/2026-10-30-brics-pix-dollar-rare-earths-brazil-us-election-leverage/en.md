# BRICS, Pix and rare earths: Brazil between monetary autonomy, US–China competition and the political crisis of 2026

**30 October 2026 · Marginal Thinking · Global System & Power / Political Economy & Markets · Institutional and geoeconomic research**

The 2026 dispute between Brazil and the United States is not one conflict with one cause. Washington initiated and concluded a trade investigation explicitly questioning Brazil's treatment of electronic payments and Pix; it also negotiated tariffs and access to critical-mineral supply chains as US competition with China intensified. Meanwhile, BRICS governments examined cross-border payment interoperability and trade in national currencies—an agenda materially narrower than the single currency once floated by President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva. In October, Lula's reelection and Senator Flávio Bolsonaro's departure to the United States added a diplomatic and political crisis to trade negotiations that began before the runoff. [1][2][3][10][15][19][20][21][24]

Documented US commercial pressure and American admission of a defeated Brazilian presidential contender are important facts. A further claim—that Washington arranged his exit in return for Brazilian concessions over rare earths, Pix or BRICS—requires a different evidentiary standard. No reviewed primary negotiating record or independent account establishes an explicit exchange of those kinds. The episode should be investigated through sequencing and bargaining mechanisms, **not treated as a proven secret minerals-for-protection transaction**. [21][23][24][25][26]

This assessment reconstructs four connected systems—**currency, payment infrastructure, mineral supply-chain control and political bargaining**—and asks how each affects American influence, Brazilian interests and the heterogeneous BRICS coalition. Information cutoff is **30 October 2026, Brasília time**. Numbers retain their source dates; scenarios are conditional and never retrospective observations.

## 1. From Bretton Woods to BRICS: international payments were political before Lula's proposal

The present international monetary system emerged from political bargains and accumulated network advantages. The 1944 Bretton Woods design centered international parities on the dollar and established multilateral institutions. Washington ended official dollar convertibility into gold in 1971, but dollar centrality endured. Deep securities markets, dollar funding, correspondent banking, trade invoicing, clearing infrastructure and a demand for liquid financial assets reinforced one another. A dominant currency is not maintained by decree alone: it benefits from network effects and complex institutions that cannot be reproduced by an announcement.

The post-Cold War incorporation of China, India and other economies into world production increased the economic scale of countries that did not necessarily shape the financial rules governing their commerce. During and after the 2008 crisis, Federal Reserve dollar liquidity arrangements with partner central banks illustrated the connection between the funding currency and crisis-response capacity. US monetary tightening can raise foreign debt costs, alter exchange rates and constrain imports where contracts and finance depend on dollars.

Brazilian concerns over external-currency dependence long predate the present presidency and are not exclusive to BRICS. Regional payment compensation and transactions in national currencies have existed in various forms. But sanctions, geopolitics and BRICS enlargement changed the scale of the question. The interest in practical alternatives is real; it does not establish that a comprehensive replacement is ready. [29][30]

## 2. BRICS as a forum, not a unified monetary sovereign

BRIC began as a financial research category in 2001; its members formed a political dialogue in the 2000s, held their first leaders' summit in 2009 and were joined by South Africa in 2011. The New Development Bank and Contingent Reserve Arrangement arose from agreements reached in 2014. Subsequent enlargement brought energy exporters and importers, large manufacturing systems, agricultural powers and countries subject to different sanctions and financial restrictions into a more complicated coalition.

BRICS is not a customs union, a single market, a currency union or a military alliance. China and India have significant independent—and sometimes rival—strategic interests. Brazil, Russia and Gulf members operate different fiscal, exchange-rate and foreign-policy regimes. Several depend heavily on Western capital markets while others actively seek sanction-resistant alternatives. Adding up national GDP or population does not reveal capacity to issue a collective currency.

Its measurable importance lies in coordination over development finance, international institutions, trade procedures and payment innovations. Such a forum can have strategic value without creating money. Successful cooperation depends on compatible rules, bank balance sheets, exchange liquidity, enforceable contracts and domestic consent, not merely a shared description of a multipolar world.

## 3. Lula's monetary idea: 2023 advocacy, 2025 clarification, 2026 position

In 2023, Lula publicly advocated a reference currency for commerce among BRICS countries and at times compared the desired autonomy with the euro experience. His formulations varied between a common trade unit and a more expansive common-currency aspiration that would avoid replacing national monies. The Johannesburg discussion included exploration of a reference mechanism. These were political aspirations and technical proposals, not the creation of a common central bank or currency. [2][32]

In 2025, Brazilian BRICS sherpa Maurício Lyrio said a common currency was not on the bloc's contemporary agenda. In February 2026 Lula himself said, while discussing trade with India, that no BRICS single-currency proposal was under discussion; the emphasis was practical settlement in national currencies. A 2023 suggestion and a 2026 institutional decision are different events. [3][4]

The **12 September 2026 New Delhi Declaration** offers the clearest contemporary benchmark. Paragraph 90 welcomes technical work on payment-message interoperability, cross-border transfers and trade settlement in members' domestic currencies, recognizing national preferences. It does not announce a common currency's name, issuer, convertibility regime, reserve backing or implementation date. A 30 October BRICS+ research report likewise described payment interoperability as more plausible than a near-term currency union. The report is independent analysis, not a leaders' decision. [1][28]

## 4. Four monetary instruments that must not be conflated

| Mechanism | What actually changes | Required infrastructure | Status in October 2026 |
|---|---|---|---|
| National-currency invoicing | Currency in which trade is priced and settled | Banks, FX markets, compliance | Available in selected bilateral transactions |
| Payment-system interoperability | How cross-border messages and final transfers connect | Central banks, payment providers, legal settlement | A BRICS technical objective, not one verified universal network |
| Trade unit of account | A benchmark for denominating claims, potentially a basket | Conversion formula, hedging and governance | Historical idea; no BRICS unit in force |
| Full common currency | Shared money replacing national currency in broad uses | Common monetary authority, liquidity and fiscal framework | Not agreed within BRICS |

Central bank digital currency is a separate technological design, not a fifth synonym for a common currency. Digitizing claims in Brazilian reais does not eliminate the real–rupee conversion or exchange risk. A faster communications channel also does not change the currency in which a debt is legally due.

These distinctions expose different strategic mechanisms. Routing messages outside SWIFT does not remove dollar dependence if financing, currency hedges and reserves remain dollar-based. Accepting local-currency settlement does not mean transferring monetary authority to China. Each architecture determines which firm, bank or public authority bears liquidity and exchange-rate risk.

## 5. Dollar liquidity: the measurable obstacle to replacement

According to the Bank for International Settlements, the US dollar featured on **89.2% of foreign exchange trades** in April 2025. The euro appeared on **28.9%**, the Chinese renminbi on **8.5%**. Because each currency trade has two sides, these are not parts of a distribution totaling 100%. Average global FX turnover was approximately **US$9.6 trillion a day**. [5][6]

```chart

US dollar | 89.2
Euro | 28.9
Chinese renminbi | 8.5
```

Even two non-US trading firms may exchange currencies through a liquid dollar pair because hedges and reliable prices are easier to obtain there. Credibly replacing that vehicle function requires deep alternative FX markets, enforceable financial contracts, abundant safe assets, predictable capital access and institutions able to provide emergency liquidity. No political declaration can immediately manufacture such depth.

Local-currency settlement can still improve efficiency on high-volume bilateral corridors and reduce unnecessary conversion steps. But illiquid currency pairs can carry larger spreads, restrictions on repatriation or credit risk when trading flows become unbalanced. Any quantitative claim of savings should compare the full cost—including hedging, working capital and settlement risk—not just headline payment fees.

## 6. Sanctions, correspondent banking and financial coercion

Financial sanctions depend on jurisdiction, participating banks, assets, payment networks and the legal or technological control of critical nodes. SWIFT supplies financial messaging; it is not the dollar, the holder of every bank deposit or the ultimate settlement mechanism. Restricting messages, freezing reserve assets and preventing dollar clearing are different interventions and have different effects.

Financial restrictions on Russia after 2022 strengthened interest in alternate payment routes, while increasing secondary-sanctions exposure for participating intermediaries. Brazil and India can rationally seek lower costs without endorsing all sanction-avoidance objectives of other BRICS states. The coalition's strategic diversity makes a single compliance regime difficult.

For Washington, market access and confidence in dollar institutions are important economic interests. Nevertheless, a successful domestic payment arrangement abroad is not automatically a material challenge to the dollar's international role. Relevance depends on actual cross-border adoption, international borrowing, reserve portfolios and alternative funding markets.

## 7. Development finance: what the NDB can and cannot accomplish

The New Development Bank finances infrastructure and development projects. Lending in local currency can reduce currency mismatch for investments earning domestic revenue. A project collecting fees in reais but repaying a dollar loan faces higher local debt service after depreciation; local-currency financing redistributes that exposure. But the lender still requires capital, credible risk management and access to investors. [29][30]

The Contingent Reserve Arrangement is a separate balance-of-payments safety-net mechanism, not a shared treasury or interchangeable commercial bank. New Delhi's declaration discussed resilience improvements and technical work; it did not announce a replacement for global multilateral liquidity arrangements. [1]

These initiatives face political-economy problems within BRICS: who contributes funds, who can draw on them, how losses are absorbed and what conditions are imposed. A country's commitment to the Global South does not settle credit and governance conflicts among member governments.

## 8. Pix: public digital infrastructure, not an international currency

Brazil's central bank launched **Pix on 16 November 2020** as a fast-payment arrangement for transfers in Brazilian reais. It combines common rules, institutional participation, address identifiers and final settlement in the domestic instant-payment system. Retail users can transfer money continuously, with rapid availability of funds. The system changed payment convenience and the competitive environment in Brazil. [7][8]

**Pix is neither cryptocurrency nor Drex nor a BRICS currency.** By itself it does not provide international FX, cross-border bank compliance or foreign-currency finality. Extending a Pix-like interface across borders would require exchange providers, identity verification, anti-money-laundering controls, operational agreements and liquidity in other currencies.

The public infrastructure model matters because uniform technical rules can increase interoperability across banks and payment firms. It can reduce frictions for small merchants and households. But its economic effect must be separated by payment use case: a transfer between relatives does not displace exactly the same products as card-based retail payments, while cards can bundle credit, installments, rewards and distinct dispute processes.

## 9. Pix in numbers: high domestic scale, not a measure of dollar displacement

For **September 2026**, Brazil's central bank reported more than **150 million individual users** of Pix during the month, roughly **7.8 billion transactions** and over **R$3.8 trillion** in transferred value. The peak day reached about **318.1 million transactions** in September. These are domestic activity measures denominated in reais, not central bank revenues, an increase in GDP or evidence that international payments shifted from dollars. [7]

| Measure | Observed value | Correct interpretation |
|---|---:|---|
| Individuals using Pix | More than 150 million | Users in September 2026 |
| Monthly transactions | About 7.8 billion | Number of transfers in September 2026 |
| Monthly transferred value | More than R$3.8 trillion | Nominal amount moved, not domestic output |
| Single-day record | Approximately 318.1 million | Transactions on 4 September 2026 |
| Pix share of global dollar settlement | Not determined by these data | Requires a separate cross-border dataset |

High transaction counts establish the system's importance, not the volume of card-network revenue displaced. Much of the measured activity may consist of payments or transfers that would never have been processed by US-branded card networks. A reliable estimate of lost fees requires transaction-level substitution, average rates, merchant mix and counterfactual behavior.

## 10. USTR's Section 301 complaint: documented US objection to Pix

The United States Trade Representative opened its Brazil Section 301 investigation on **15 July 2025**, covering digital commerce and electronic payment services alongside tariffs, intellectual property, corruption-related measures, ethanol and deforestation. On **1 June 2026** USTR determined that selected practices restricted or burdened US commerce and explicitly questioned Brazil's preferential treatment of a domestic payment system. On **15 July 2026**, the US announced a **25% tariff on specified Brazilian products**. The measure was not an across-the-board 25% charge on everything Brazil sells, nor was Pix its sole stated reason. [9][10][11][12]

USTR's contention is that government policies favor a national payment champion at the expense of foreign firms and unfairly influence competing services. It is a litigating government's position, not a neutral international finding establishing the alleged economic harms. A proper legal assessment would test actual participation rules, service visibility, fees, equal access and enforcement against foreign and domestic operators. [10][12][13]

The Brazilian government disputes the US allegations. Its foreign and industry ministries said in August that US justifications mischaracterize Brazil's practices and called for an agreement respecting Brazilian sovereignty and international trade rules. The evidence thus establishes a real policy conflict between views of public payment infrastructure and competition; it does not vindicate either side's complete causal interpretation. [14]

## 11. Commercial interests of card networks and payment providers

Card schemes, digital wallets, merchant acquirers and bank transfers overlap only partly. A very low-cost instant transfer can displace transactions traditionally served by some fee-charging intermediaries. However, cards also provide consumer credit, installment arrangements, chargeback processes, benefits and global acceptance. The substitution rate depends on the product and customer segment.

Whether Brazil discriminates against foreign suppliers cannot be answered simply by noting that Pix is public infrastructure. The legal issue concerns access and the symmetry of regulatory requirements. A government can legitimately standardize a national interoperable rail and can also design rules that impose undue disadvantages. Those possibilities require institutional and economic evidence, not slogans.

American diplomatic attention to Pix can align with private firms' interest in market share; alignment does not automatically establish improper influence. To demonstrate a coordinated corporate campaign, researchers would need submissions, meetings, regulatory analyses and verifiable financial impacts. The USTR record proves a US trade complaint, not a US plan to abolish Pix or a comprehensive threat to dollar hegemony.

## 12. Pix, SWIFT, CIPS and cross-border rails operate at different levels

```flow
Domestic Pix in reais → real-time transfer and settlement in reais → Brazilian payment efficiency
Brazil–India trade → contract denomination → currency liquidity provider → international messaging and settlement → rupee receipt
BRICS Payment Task Force → standards and interoperability study → potential lower costs without a common currency
```

A payment has at least six separate technical and institutional dimensions: customer authentication, messaging, denomination, conversion, clearing, final settlement and treatment of defaults or disputes. Pix solves a subset of these problems for domestic transactions in reais. SWIFT provides communications; renminbi cross-border infrastructure and other regional networks have different participation and currency rules. Treating every rail as a substitute for the others obscures actual dependencies.

Interoperability between domestic fast-payment systems would require AML screening, identity assurance, data formats, liquidity arrangements, exchange-rate quotes, dispute resolution and operational resilience. A technical bridge could make transfers faster and cheaper while creating new concentration in the bridge provider. Cybersecurity, privacy and governance rights are economic properties of the system, not optional details. [1][29]

Sovereignty also has a civil-liberties dimension. Reduced dependence on external providers can increase state autonomy, while excessive domestic surveillance might reduce individual control over financial information. An institutional assessment should examine efficiency together with privacy, oversight, fraud and remedies.

## 13. The liquidity problem hidden inside promises of instantaneous international payments

Consider an illustrative Brazilian importer paying an Indian supplier. The importer wishes to spend reais; the supplier wants rupees. A direct corridor can avoid routing both legs through dollars, but an intermediary must quote the real–rupee rate, provide currency inventory, finance timing differences and absorb or hedge price movements. Persistent trade imbalances may leave institutions holding currency they cannot easily spend or hedge.

Multilateral netting can reduce gross transfers when money flows in both directions. It cannot erase settlement risk, liquidity shortages or restrictions on currency conversion. Payment-versus-payment mechanisms can reduce principal risk but require coordinated legal finality. Speed of messaging is not unlimited convertibility.

Brazil could benefit from corridors that match actual trade flows, provided contracts do not quietly shift exchange-rate losses to the central bank or public financial institutions. Any credible project must specify who bears currency risk and whether its claimed savings exceed supervision and liquidity costs.

## 14. The Brazil–China–US triangle: commercial interests rather than a two-camp model

China purchases large volumes of Brazilian agricultural and mineral products and supplies manufacturing inputs and equipment. The US is an important market and source of investment, technology, services and financial instruments. Increasing options with one partner does not require ending commerce with the other. The challenge is to manage incompatible technical rules, financing terms, strategic export controls and industrial incentives. [19][26][27]

Brazil's Mercosur membership creates a separate layer of commitments. A BRICS payment project is not itself a preferential tariff agreement; a minerals investment with US firms may involve environmental, industrial and ownership questions without altering BRICS institutions. These instruments interact, but their legal effects are not interchangeable.

US policy can combine commercial outreach with pressure on selected practices. Brazilian policy can welcome American capital for minerals while keeping major Chinese trade relationships. Actual bargaining outcomes depend on contractual rights, processing capabilities, market alternatives and state institutions, not just diplomatic rhetoric.

## 15. Rare earths: the difference between resources and industrial control

The US Geological Survey's 2026 mineral statistics estimated rare-earth reserves at **44 million metric tons of REO equivalent in China**, **21 million in Brazil**, **6.3 million in Australia**, **3.8 million in Russia** and **1.9 million in the US**. Estimated **2025 mine production** was **270,000 metric tons for China**, **2,000 for Brazil** and **51,000 for the US**. Reserves and annual production represent separate phenomena; they are plotted separately. Figures are subject to methodology, estimation and revision. [16]

Brazil's central contrast is substantial geological endowment and relatively small current extraction. A country's reserve estimate does not establish an immediately deliverable supply of qualified magnets. Ore grade, light versus heavy rare-earth composition, metallurgical recovery, environmental permissions, roads, energy, reagents, financing and reliable buyers determine commercial output. After mixed concentrates or carbonates are produced, additional separation, refining, metalmaking and magnet manufacturing remain necessary.

The term rare earth covers materials with different strategic uses. Neodymium and praseodymium are important in magnets; terbium and dysprosium can support performance in high-temperature applications. The value of a deposit depends on recoverable composition, not simply aggregate REO tonnage.

## 16. China's downstream processing concentration

The IEA identifies concentrated refining and processing capacity as a major risk to minerals security. Export controls on selected heavy rare earths introduced by China in 2025 disrupted downstream firms dependent on relatively small quantities of specialized inputs. An IEA stress case quantified large amounts of downstream production exposed to full implementation of additional restrictions. This is **exposure under a scenario**, not an observed amount of industrial output already lost. [17][31]

Processing dominance can persist even when ore is mined elsewhere. Industrial know-how, trained chemical engineers, qualified capacity and long-term purchase commitments are difficult to duplicate. New projects outside China need to secure both feedstock and reliable buyers. If only extraction is diversified, dependence can migrate to a different location in the value chain rather than disappear.

For Brazil, signing an ore or mixed-carbonate sales agreement may generate jobs and exports while leaving high-margin processing overseas. Mandating instant domestic vertical integration, on the other hand, may destroy project viability where capabilities and scale do not exist. The relevant evidence concerns feasible investments, contracts, costs and technology transfer.

## 17. An actual US financial commitment: the Pela Ema project

The US Development Finance Corporation released documentation for a proposed **US$465 million financing** of SVRE's Pela Ema rare-earth project in Goiás, involving production expansion, operating costs and other specified uses. Estimated total project costs were **US$628 million**. Its expected product includes mixed rare-earth carbonate, which still requires downstream processing. [18]

This is direct evidence of American strategic and commercial interest. It is not proof that Washington owns every Brazilian mineral deposit, or that funding conveyed automatic control over national sovereignty. Lending agreements, shareholdings, purchase contracts, operating licenses and ownership of subsurface resources establish different rights.

A complete review must examine credit conditions, security, covenants, offtake commitments, environmental obligations, tax effects, beneficial ownership and possibilities for local separation facilities. International supply diversification can coexist with contractual asymmetries that deserve public scrutiny.

Brazil's Superior Electoral Court reported Lula's reelection on **25 October 2026** with **64,256,232 valid votes (51.79%)** against **59,826,041 (48.21%)** for Senator Flávio Bolsonaro. These official results are facts, not hypothetical outcomes. Pre-election Reuters reporting described how the incoming administration's approach to Washington could alter expectations over tariffs, critical minerals and strategic autonomy. Such reporting describes possible policy consequences, not a demonstrated capacity to control an electoral result. [19][20]

Flávio's political proximity to American conservatives and Lula's emphasis on multilateral institutions affect diplomatic expectations. Tariffs, technology restrictions and conditional financing offer observable channels through which a powerful state can change another country's choices. A much stronger allegation—that an external government orchestrated an election outcome or made mineral concessions a condition of recognizing a candidate—requires explicit evidence of those actions. Political affinity alone is insufficient.

## 19. Trade talks began before the October election crisis

Brazil's foreign and industry ministries documented high-level talks with USTR on **31 August 2026** on US tariffs and bilateral commerce. A further ministerial meeting in Milwaukee on **30 September** outlined a working group for a potential agreement involving tariff and non-tariff matters. These talks preceded the **25 October** presidential runoff and Flávio's subsequent departure. [14][15]

The chronology limits a simple claim that the later departure caused the beginning of trade bargaining. The election may still change the composition and bargaining confidence of the incoming administration; additional political incidents can shift the pace and tone of negotiations. But the underlying tariff and minerals agendas have an independently documented pre-election history.

USTR investigation July 2025 → Section 301 determination June 2026 → specified tariffs July 2026 → bilateral working group September 2026

## 20. What is known about Flávio Bolsonaro's departure

Reuters reporting on **27 and 28 October** described Senator Flávio Bolsonaro leaving Brazil after the electoral defeat and not complying with an order to surrender his passports amid an investigation in which he denies wrongdoing. Accounts reported travel through Argentina and onward toward the US. On **29 October**, a New York Times report republished by Folha cited American authorities who said Flávio and his family entered the US with temporary **humanitarian parole**. Reuters reported on **30 October** that the Trump administration had no current plan to endorse his unsupported claim to be Brazil's president-elect. [21][22][23][24]

Humanitarian parole is not identical to formal asylum, permanent residence or diplomatic recognition. His assertion that he won the election did not carry proof overriding the official count. His characterization of the investigation as persecution must be presented as his claim; allegations under investigation do not establish criminal guilt. [20][22][24]

Other Reuters reporting described conflicting accounts from Brazilian and Argentine officials concerning Argentina's role in his route out of Brazil. The episode may strain relations between neighbors, but those accounts alone do not establish a multinational operation to condition Brazilian mineral exports. [25]

## 21. Political leverage can exist without a proven quid pro quo

Providing temporary admission to an opposition figure can affect diplomacy, domestic political narratives and the receiving state's influence over the visitor's circumstances. Washington can choose the visibility and legal terms of the admission, while Brazilian institutions can make their own judicial and diplomatic decisions. This creates potential leverage in a general political sense.

Several explanations remain possible: ideological sympathy, declared humanitarian grounds, domestic US political incentives, desire to maintain channels with Brazilian opposition or concern about relations with Lula. Alternatively, the White House may judge that formal backing for an electoral challenge would impair the trade relationship with a legally reelected Brazilian government. Reuters' 30 October account documents a posture of caution at that point, though policies can change. [23][24]

To establish a specific minerals-for-protection trade, investigators would need negotiating correspondence linking the visitor's status to mineral terms, formal demands, a documented concession schedule, or corroborated direct testimony. **None was established by the central records reviewed for this assessment.** The possibility deserves continued scrutiny, but not publication as settled causation.

## 22. What a real minerals agreement could exchange

Potential bilateral terms include mining credit, purchase guarantees, geological cooperation, technology transfer, local processing capacity, permitting, environmental standards and access to strategic inputs for defence and industrial customers. The distributional consequences vary. Brazil could remain a supplier of intermediate material and import advanced magnets, or negotiate investment in domestic separation and qualified component manufacture.

National sovereignty consists of several concrete rights: mineral licensing, taxation, corporate governance, disposition of output, environmental oversight, access to information and enforceability of contracts. Foreign financing does not inherently mean sovereignty is lost; nor does domestic ownership guarantee independence when downstream technology and buyers remain concentrated abroad.

Project-level evidence should establish where profits, jobs, intellectual property and shutdown risks reside. Documents and actual investment matter more than labels such as surrender or liberation of natural resources.

## 23. Domestic industrial trade-offs and environmental constraints

Rare-earth industrialization needs competitive energy, skilled specialists, chemical processing, environmental approval, equipment and dependable demand. Extraction and separation can involve significant land, wastewater and waste-management risks. The policy challenge is to achieve credible supply without transferring hidden environmental or financial costs to communities.

One perspective favors industrial policy and domestic value addition to avoid prolonged export of raw material. Another emphasizes that inflexible local-processing mandates may deter capital and prevent viable mining. Neither determines the right answer for every deposit. Proper counterfactuals consider costs, state subsidies, technological capability, price volatility and obligations to workers and surrounding communities.

An exclusive Brazil–US framing would miss possible investments and markets from Europe, Asia and Brazilian businesses. Diversification can improve negotiating options, provided contracts and public commitments do not assume facilities or capabilities that have not been built.

## 24. China as customer, competitor and potential processing partner

China's purchase of Brazilian commodities supports export revenue and can finance new investment, but concentration of demand creates vulnerability to policy and price shocks. Dependence must be measured by product and stage: soybean destinations, machinery suppliers, fertilizer inputs and mineral processing plants differ fundamentally. [27]

A Chinese off-take contract might support output while leaving processing abroad. An American contract might create a different dependency. Brazil's industrial objective need not be a categorical choice between the countries; it can seek qualified suppliers, intellectual-property development and exposure limits across several commercial partners.

India, European economies and private companies introduce further options and constraints. The global industrial economy does not operate as two perfectly closed camps. Regulatory compatibility, sanctions and critical equipment access nevertheless create situations in which options conflict.

## 25. Mercosur and the regional diplomatic implications

The Brazil–Argentina relationship matters to regional industry and political management. Divergent reports over Flávio's travel through Argentina may create an additional diplomatic dispute without necessarily altering Mercosur trade rules. Mercosur is an incomplete customs union; it has no authority to determine a Brazilian senator's US immigration status or reverse a domestic electoral count. [25]

Investments in Brazilian minerals can occur without changing the common external tariff. General import concessions to outside countries, by contrast, can have regional legal implications. Separating minerals contracts, tariff policy and immigration prevents the analytical error of treating every bilateral controversy as a single legal bargain.

Other members could face second-order logistics and industrial effects of Brazil's external decisions. Such consequences require actual data on regional shipping, electricity and investment, not simply political proximity.

## 26. An evidence matrix for claims of US pressure

| Evidence | Supported observation | Unsupported leap |
|---|---|---|
| USTR investigation and tariff | Formal US trade dispute involving Pix | All tariffs exist solely to defeat a BRICS currency |
| DFC project documentation | US financing interest in Brazilian rare earths | Automatic transfer of mineral sovereignty |
| September bilateral working group | Negotiation began before the election | October flight created the talks from nothing |
| 25 October TSE result | Official Lula reelection | Flávio recognized as alternative president |
| Reports of US humanitarian parole | US admission of Flávio and family | Proven secret exchange for Pix or minerals |
| September BRICS declaration | Local-currency settlement and interoperability talks | Adopted BRICS single currency or global Pix replacement |

A causal investigation needs **mechanism, sequence and conditionality**. Mechanism identifies who can impose tariffs, direct financing, grant migration status or shape access to technology. Sequence establishes when decisions were made. Conditionality requires documentation linking a benefit or penalty to a specific concession. Temporal coincidence alone does not establish a bargain.

Competing explanations must be tested. Pre-existing mineral talks weaken the notion that the flight originated mineral interest, even if future bargaining could be influenced by the episode. A later trade accord without an explicit migration reference would not logically rule out informal pressure, but it would still leave the stronger allegation unproven absent independent evidence.

## 27. Four conditional futures through 2030

**Scenario A — pragmatic trade negotiation.** Brazil and the US reach sector-specific understandings on tariffs, competition, investment and minerals without fundamentally changing Pix or Brazil's BRICS participation. Tests: published commitments, continued diplomatic meetings and domestic payment-system continuity.

**Scenario B — gradual financial interoperability.** BRICS members enable selected cross-border local-currency corridors while dollar liquidity remains important for international finance. Tests: verifiable transaction shares, actual costs and operating settlement mechanisms—not announcements alone.

**Scenario C — industrial fragmentation and cross-pressure.** US–China technology and mineral restrictions disrupt firms dependent on several markets at once. Brazilian contracts and industrial policy adjust. Tests: concrete export controls, equipment interruptions, changed investment locations and observable price effects.

**Scenario D — prolonged bilateral political crisis.** Flávio's circumstances deepen diplomatic tension and slow commercial talks. Tests: recorded interruptions in negotiations, diplomatic demarches, judicial decisions and explicit documentary links between political and commercial demands.

## 28. What meaningful autonomy would require

Full commercial self-sufficiency is not a realistic goal in a specialized international economy. A more practical objective is **to reduce irreplaceable dependencies and improve reliable alternatives**. In payments, that means multiple corridors, deep FX and interoperable standards; in critical minerals, extraction through qualified refining and magnet manufacture; in diplomacy, diverse trade and investment relationships; in finance, balanced exposures and robust funding alternatives.

Autonomy is not isolation. Domestic production of every input may be prohibitively costly. Credible negotiating capacity comes from the ability to continue operating when one supplier fails or one government applies restrictions. Mineral reserves without processing, local-currency invoices without liquidity and diplomacy without implementation cannot deliver more than limited formal independence.

Brazilian institutions must coordinate central banking, commercial negotiations, industrial policy, environmental oversight and public accountability. Long-horizon investment should remain workable across changes of government. Transparent contractual obligations and reliable legal institutions contribute to national bargaining power.

| Financing and off-take terms | DFC and company contracts | Control over output and risk |
| US official legal stance on Flávio | Immigration and diplomatic instruments | Distinguishes admission, asylum and recognition |
| Cross-conditionality between migration and commerce | Verified correspondence and corroborated testimony | Could substantiate a specific coercive bargain |

Indicators prevent rhetoric from being mistaken for economic transformation. A meeting can signal priorities without completing an investment. A memorandum may precede construction by years. A political crisis can dominate public debate without changing a single commercial contract.

## 30. Conclusion: the contest over the infrastructure of autonomy

There is measurable competition over payment standards, mineral supply chains, export markets and political legitimacy. BRICS provides a forum to discuss financing and national-currency payment mechanisms, not an adopted common currency. Pix has become a large-scale Brazilian public payment infrastructure explicitly contested in US trade policy, but it is not an international dollar substitute. Brazil's rare-earth reserves provide potential strategic value only insofar as geology is converted into industrially usable materials and capabilities. [1][7][10][16][17]

Lula's reelection and Flávio's flight added a real diplomatic controversy to US–Brazil relations that already involved tariffs, trade consultations and minerals. Journalism documents US admission of the former candidate, but the examined record **does not demonstrate a direct bargain conditioning that admission on mineral concessions, a changed Pix regime or abandoning BRICS monetary initiatives**. Maintaining that distinction is necessary for a defensible geopolitical investigation. [14][15][20][23][24]

Brazil's choices over the coming years concern institution-building more than symbolic alignment: competitive and nondiscriminatory payments, safer international interconnection, diversified industrial financing, mineral processing capacity and diplomacy resilient to political disagreement. Monetary power, technology, contracts, environmental governance and democratic institutions are parts of the same political economy without being reducible to a single conspiracy.

## Method, evidence classes and limits

**Observed:** formal USTR actions, Brazilian and BRICS government documents, Central Bank statistics, TSE results, USGS data and the DFC financing documentation. **Attributed claims:** US allegations of payment discrimination, Brazilian responses, positions expressed by Lula, Flávio and officials. **Conditional inferences:** possible economic effects of local-currency settlement, alternative mineral contracting and political leverage. **Scenarios:** explicitly hypothetical, without unsupported probabilities.

Journalistic reporting on Flávio's international movement relies on attributed sources; this dossier does not claim to reproduce complete US immigration records. USGS figures estimate reserves and 2025 mine production, not a 30 October 2026 physical census. BIS currency turnover shares count either side of FX trades and therefore cannot be added to 100%. Pix figures measure domestic activity, not direct losses for American card networks. DFC materials document financing terms without entailing control over Brazilian deposits generally. The proposed causal link between admission of Flávio and concessions in payments or minerals remains **unproven**.
