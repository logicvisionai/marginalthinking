# Global Macro — U.S. manufacturing holds expansion while Europe’s labour market softens at the margin

The first October releases reinforce a global regime of uneven resilience rather than synchronized acceleration. The U.S. ISM Manufacturing PMI registered 54.5 in September, only 0.1 point below August and the ninth consecutive month of manufacturing expansion. In the euro area, August unemployment held at 6.4% month on month but rose from 6.3% a year earlier, while the number of unemployed increased by 267,000 over the year. U.S. energy trade adds a separate supply-side signal: propane exports averaged about 2.0 million barrels per day in the first half of 2026, 11% above the same period of 2025.

Taken together, the releases do not describe a single global cycle. U.S. manufacturing remains expansionary, European labour conditions are broadly stable but slightly softer than a year ago, and U.S. hydrocarbon export capacity continues to redirect energy flows toward Asian demand.

**Information cutoff: 1 October 2026, 14:00 BRT.**

## U.S. manufacturing remains above the expansion threshold

The Institute for Supply Management reported a September Manufacturing PMI of 54.5, compared with 54.6 in August. Manufacturing activity therefore expanded for a ninth consecutive month. The small decline in the headline index is less important than the persistence of a reading comfortably above 50.

```chart
type: line
title: U.S. ISM Manufacturing PMI
unit: index
August 2026 | 54.6
September 2026 | 54.5
```

The September reading suggests continuity rather than a new acceleration. August had already shown broad expansion across the five subindexes that directly enter the PMI. A nearly unchanged headline in September means the relevant question shifts from whether manufacturing has returned to expansion toward how durable that expansion is under restrictive financing conditions and elevated input costs.

## Euro-area unemployment is stable monthly but slightly weaker annually

Eurostat reported a seasonally adjusted euro-area unemployment rate of 6.4% in August, unchanged from July and up from 6.3% in August 2025. The EU rate was 6.1%, also unchanged from July and up from 6.0% a year earlier.

Eurostat estimated 11.357 million unemployed people in the euro area and 13.600 million in the EU. Compared with July, unemployment increased by 26,000 in the euro area; compared with August 2025, it increased by 267,000.

The labour data do not indicate a sharp deterioration. They do, however, limit the case for describing European labour conditions as continuously tightening. Stable monthly rates coexist with a modest year-on-year increase in unemployment.

## U.S. propane exports show how energy abundance is being transmitted through trade

The U.S. Energy Information Administration reported that propane exports averaged 2.0 million barrels per day in the first half of 2026, 11% above the same period in 2025. Exports reached a record 2.1 million barrels per day in April. The EIA attributes the increase to higher U.S. propane production and changing trade flows, particularly stronger shipments to India and other South Asian markets.

```flow
U.S. propane production -> export availability -> higher South Asian shipments -> changing regional energy flows
```

This is structurally different from the cyclical information in PMI and unemployment data. Manufacturing surveys and labour statistics describe current demand and production conditions; propane exports show how physical supply capacity and trade infrastructure alter the geography of energy flows.

## The common signal is dispersion

The October 1 releases extend the pattern visible at the end of September: national and sectoral cycles are not converging neatly. U.S. manufacturing remains in expansion, euro-area unemployment is stable but marginally softer on a year-on-year basis, and U.S. energy exports are expanding through a trade channel increasingly connected to South Asia.

For macro analysis, that dispersion matters because monetary policy, industrial activity and commodity transmission can move in different directions at the same time. A manufacturing expansion does not by itself imply accelerating aggregate demand; stable unemployment does not eliminate gradual labour-market softening; and stronger energy exports can reflect supply abundance and trade reallocation rather than stronger domestic consumption.

The next tests are whether U.S. manufacturing orders and employment preserve the expansion, whether European unemployment remains close to current levels, and whether high U.S. energy exports continue as Asian demand and global shipping patterns adjust.
