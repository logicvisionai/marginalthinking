# Germany: industrial conversion, fiscal rearmament and the reconstruction of Europe's manufacturing centre

Germany in 2026 is not well described either as a deindustrialising economy in terminal decline or as the unchanged export machine of the 2000s and 2010s. It remains Europe's largest economy, one of the world's densest manufacturing systems, the central balance sheet of the euro area's industrial core and a political system with unusually broad institutional capacity. At the same time, several assumptions that supported the previous growth model have weakened at once: Russian pipeline gas is no longer the low-cost anchor it once was, Chinese demand is less reliable for German capital goods and vehicles, the domestic working-age population is ageing, transport and digital infrastructure require large replacement investment, and defence has become a much larger fiscal and industrial commitment.

The decisive question is therefore one of conversion. Germany has capital, engineering knowledge, firms, research institutions, household savings, fiscal capacity and access to the European single market. The issue is whether those assets can be converted into grid capacity, transport reliability, defence production, software-intensive industry, housing, skilled labour and private investment quickly enough to prevent the old cost advantages from disappearing faster than new sources of productivity emerge.

The cyclical data do not settle that structural question. Destatis' detailed release for the second quarter of 2026 put real GDP growth at 0.3% quarter on quarter and 1.0% year on year, with exports again an important contributor. Yet employment was 0.5% below a year earlier and private consumption was nearly flat in real terms. The Bundesbank expected only slight third-quarter growth, partly because low Rhine water levels disrupted industrial logistics. Germany is recovering, but the recovery is occurring inside a deeper reallocation of energy, capital, labour and security spending.

## Federalism distributes power across institutions rather than concentrating it in one centre

The Federal Republic combines a strong federal executive with constitutionally powerful Länder, an elected Bundestag, a Bundesrat representing state governments, an independent judiciary and a dense layer of municipalities and corporatist institutions. Friedrich Merz has served as Federal Chancellor since May 2025, but the mechanics of German state capacity extend far beyond the chancellery. Tax sharing, education, policing, infrastructure approval, energy siting and implementation often require coordination across levels of government.

This architecture can slow projects, but it also distributes legitimacy and implementation capacity. Large reforms frequently require not only a parliamentary majority but accommodation among Länder, municipalities, regulators, firms, unions and courts. The result is a state that is strong in rule-bound administration yet can be slow when policy requires rapid cross-jurisdiction execution.

```flow
Federal government + Bundestag
  -> national budget, defence, tax, industrial and EU policy
Bundesrat + Länder
  -> implementation, education, policing, planning and co-legislation
Municipalities + regulators + courts
  -> permits, local infrastructure, enforcement and judicial review
Firms + unions + works councils
  -> wage formation, training, investment and restructuring
European Union + euro area
  -> trade, competition, fiscal and monetary framework
```

The important distinction is between decision capacity and execution capacity. Germany can mobilize very large financial resources once political agreement is reached; converting those resources into railways, grids, housing, defence equipment or digital public services remains an implementation problem.

## The industrial model is still large, but its old cost structure has changed

Germany remains Europe's largest economy and one of the world's deepest manufacturing systems. Its productive strengths include vehicles, machinery, chemicals, electrical equipment, pharmaceuticals, industrial automation, precision engineering and a broad network of medium-sized specialist firms. The euro area is Germany's most important partner region; Bundesbank data also show the United States as the largest single-country source of receipts and China as the largest source of expenditure in Germany's international current account.

The model historically combined high-value exports with imported intermediate goods and energy. That creates leverage when global trade expands, but exposure when energy, shipping, geopolitical relations or foreign industrial demand change. The automotive sector faces a simultaneous transition in propulsion technology, software, Chinese competition and capital intensity. Chemicals and metals remain highly sensitive to energy and logistics costs.

Germany's July 2026 current-account surplus was €21.2 billion, with a €21.1 billion goods surplus. A large external surplus remains evidence of tradable-sector strength, but it does not mean every industrial region or firm is competitive. Aggregate export capacity can coexist with declining employment in energy-intensive or legacy manufacturing.

| Structural layer | Durable strength | Main constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Machinery and industrial equipment | Dense supplier networks and engineering specialization | Weak investment cycle and external demand dependence |
| Automotive | Scale, brands and production know-how | EV/software transition and Chinese competition |
| Chemicals and materials | Integrated industrial clusters | Energy and logistics costs |
| Mittelstand | Specialized export niches and apprenticeship links | Succession, labour scarcity and digital adoption |
| EU single market | Large home market without internal tariffs | Common regulation and exposure to EU-wide demand |
| Euro | Eliminates intra-euro currency risk | Monetary policy is not calibrated to Germany alone |

## Growth has returned, but the recovery is still more export-led than household-led

Destatis reported that second-quarter 2026 GDP increased by 0.3% from the first quarter and 1.0% from a year earlier. Manufacturing gross value added rose 0.9% quarter on quarter, while construction was nearly flat. Private consumption increased only 0.1% from a year earlier, compared with 3.0% growth in government consumption.

This composition matters. Export and public-demand support can restart aggregate GDP without immediately restoring a strong private investment and household-consumption cycle. Germany's longer-run challenge is to convert public infrastructure spending and industrial restructuring into higher private capital formation and productivity.

The Bundesbank's September assessment reinforced that distinction. It expected only slight third-quarter growth, citing temporary factors including low Rhine water levels that disrupted transport and raised costs for chemicals, metals and refined petroleum. A highly industrial economy remains sensitive to physical infrastructure in ways that a purely service-based macro narrative can miss.

## Employment is weakening before demographic pressure has disappeared

Approximately 45.41 million people resident in Germany were employed in August 2026. Destatis reported a 0.5% decline from a year earlier and a seasonally adjusted fall of 29,000 from July. Employment had already been declining on average during the first seven months of the year.

This is occurring while the demographic structure points toward a smaller domestic workforce over time. Germany's population was about 83.5 million in 2026, but Eurostat-based projections published by Destatis place the population around 74.7 million in 2100 under the referenced baseline. The fertility rate was 1.36 in 2024.

The immediate labour problem is therefore not simply unemployment. It is matching: industrial restructuring can release workers in one region or occupation while ageing creates shortages in health, skilled trades, engineering and public services elsewhere. Immigration can expand labour supply, but its economic effect depends on housing, language acquisition, credential recognition, education and local administrative capacity.

## Fiscal policy shifted from scarcity rules toward targeted balance-sheet expansion

Germany's constitutional debt brake shaped fiscal policy after the global financial crisis. In 2025, however, constitutional changes excluded certain defence and security expenditure above a threshold from the debt-brake calculation and enabled a €500 billion Special Fund for Infrastructure and Climate Neutrality. The fund is designed to operate over twelve years, with resources for federal infrastructure, Länder and municipalities, and the Climate and Transformation Fund.

By 2026, the scale was material. The Federal Ministry of Finance reported €128.7 billion in planned investment expenditure across the core federal budget, Climate and Transformation Fund and infrastructure special fund. This is a structural shift: the state is attempting to use its balance sheet to repair infrastructure and raise potential growth while maintaining a rules-based fiscal framework.

The first-half 2026 general-government deficit was €71.3 billion, equal to 3.1% of GDP on a provisional basis. A half-year deficit ratio is not a full-year outcome, but it illustrates the cost of the investment and security transition.

The core risk is not simply higher debt. Borrowing can raise future productive capacity if it finances rail, grids, schools, digital networks or defence systems that would otherwise become bottlenecks. The risk is that procurement, planning and execution lag the financing, leaving a larger public balance sheet without a commensurate rise in potential output.

## The energy transition has changed the generation mix faster than the industrial cost problem

Germany exited nuclear generation in 2023 and continues to reduce coal use while rapidly expanding renewables. In 2025, Bundesnetzagentur data show renewables generated 257.5 TWh, or 58.8% of total electricity generation. Solar output reached 74.1 TWh, onshore wind 106.5 TWh and offshore wind 26.1 TWh. Natural gas generated 60.6 TWh, lignite 67.2 TWh and hard coal 28.2 TWh.

Installed renewable capacity approached 210 GW by the end of 2025, including about 117 GW of solar and 68.1 GW of onshore wind. The transformation is therefore no longer marginal.

The bottleneck has shifted from adding capacity to integrating it. Variable renewable generation requires transmission expansion, storage, flexible demand, dispatchable backup and faster permitting. Wholesale prices can be negative during periods of surplus and still high during scarcity. In 2025, the average day-ahead price was €89.32/MWh and negative prices occurred during 573 hours.

For industry, the relevant variable is not the annual renewable share alone. It is the delivered cost and reliability of electricity and gas at the time production is needed. The energy transition succeeds economically only if low-carbon capacity becomes a stable industrial input rather than simply a higher installed-capacity statistic.

## Infrastructure execution is the bridge between fiscal capacity and productivity

Germany's investment shift matters only if financial authorization becomes usable physical and digital capacity. The 2026 federal budget placed more than €34 billion into traditional transport investment, while the infrastructure special fund also supports rail, roads, bridges, broadband, hospitals, schools and other public assets. These categories address bottlenecks that accumulate over long periods and therefore cannot be reversed by a single budget year.

Transport is especially important for an economy organized around industrial clusters and cross-border supply chains. Delayed rail freight, bridge restrictions, congested roads or low river levels can interrupt production even when aggregate capital spending is high. Digital infrastructure has a similar role: broadband, public digital services and data infrastructure affect the transaction cost of firms and the administrative cost of investment.

The productivity test is therefore physical. Higher appropriations should eventually be visible in shorter transport times, higher network reliability, more construction completion, faster permits and lower coordination costs. If these indicators do not improve, fiscal expansion risks raising demand without repairing the supply constraints it was designed to remove.

## Defence rearmament is becoming an industrial policy channel as well as a security policy

Germany has sharply expanded defence expenditure since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine. The 2026 federal budget raised the regular defence allocation to €82.7 billion, while an additional €25.5 billion was planned from the Bundeswehr special fund. Different official accounting frameworks produce different totals for NATO and constitutional purposes, so these figures should not be mechanically combined with broader security spending categories.

The economic mechanism is clear. Higher procurement can support domestic and European producers of vehicles, electronics, munitions, aerospace systems, sensors and software. It can also compete for engineers, metals, electronics and fiscal resources with civilian infrastructure and industrial projects.

Germany therefore faces an allocation problem rather than a simple spending target. Defence expansion creates durable capacity only when procurement contracts translate into production lines, inventories, trained personnel and interoperable systems. Fiscal authorization is the beginning of the process, not the end.

## The euro amplifies Germany's continental scale while limiting national monetary adjustment

Germany gains substantially from the euro. Its firms operate inside a large currency area without exchange-rate risk against major European customers and suppliers, and the financial system benefits from a common monetary architecture. At the same time, interest rates are set by the ECB for the euro area as a whole.

That matters because Germany's domestic cycle can differ from those of faster-growing or more inflationary members. National adjustment therefore falls more heavily on wages, fiscal policy, bank credit, investment and competitiveness. Germany's traditionally large external surplus is one mechanism through which domestic saving has been intermediated abroad when investment at home was relatively weak.

The 2025–2026 fiscal shift is partly an attempt to alter that pattern by raising domestic investment without abandoning the monetary union's common framework.

## Political stability does not remove distributive conflict

Germany's political system is consensus-heavy but not conflict-free. Industrial restructuring, migration, energy prices, pensions, housing, taxation and defence spending distribute costs unevenly across regions, generations and sectors. Federalism and coalition government force those conflicts into negotiated legislation and implementation.

That can produce slower decisions than in centralized systems. It can also make policy more durable once agreement is reached. The relevant analytical question is not whether consensus is efficient in the abstract. It is whether the time required for consensus is compatible with the speed of industrial, security and demographic change.


## The old German growth model was a specific historical bargain, not a permanent national characteristic

Post-war West Germany built a social-market economy around competitive manufacturing, coordinated wage bargaining, vocational training, bank-industry relationships and an export orientation that became progressively more European. Reunification then expanded the fiscal burden and the domestic market while integrating eastern Germany into a common legal, monetary and infrastructure system. European monetary union removed exchange-rate risk against Germany's largest regional customers, while the enlargement of the European Union created a dense Central European production network in which components, intermediate goods and final assembly could move across borders.

After the early-2000s labour-market and corporate restructuring, German firms entered the decade before the pandemic with three external conditions that reinforced one another. Chinese industrialisation generated demand for machinery, vehicles and chemicals; Russian pipeline gas supplied an important part of the energy system at costs compatible with energy-intensive industry; and the euro area provided a large home market without recurrent intra-European currency crises. High household and corporate saving could coexist with large current-account surpluses because foreign demand absorbed German production.

None of those mechanisms has vanished completely, but all are less dependable. China is simultaneously a customer, supplier and industrial competitor. Energy supply has been diversified at higher infrastructure and transition cost. European security expenditure has moved from a relatively contained budget item toward a major industrial programme. The model now requires more domestic investment precisely when ageing, permitting delays and construction capacity constrain execution.

This historical framing matters because Germany does not need to replace "exports" with "domestic demand" in a simple binary sense. It needs a different composition of exports, a more resilient energy and logistics base, higher domestic capital formation and productivity growth strong enough to finance ageing and security without eroding competitiveness.

## China and the United States expose two different vulnerabilities in the external model

Germany's 2025 trade data show why external adjustment is difficult. China again became Germany's largest trading partner by total goods turnover at €251.8 billion, ahead of the United States at €240.5 billion. But the composition was asymmetric: German imports from China reached €170.6 billion while exports to China fell to €81.3 billion, producing an €89.3 billion German goods deficit with China. The United States, by contrast, remained Germany's most important single export destination.

That means the two relationships transmit different risks. With China, Germany faces supplier dependence in selected intermediate and manufactured goods while also confronting weaker Chinese demand and stronger Chinese competition in sectors such as electric vehicles, batteries, industrial equipment and clean-energy technologies. With the United States, German firms retain a large high-income customer and investment market, but trade-policy changes and sectoral tariffs can hit export-heavy industries quickly.

The adjustment is therefore not reducible to "decoupling". Germany would bear large costs from abrupt separation from either economy. The more realistic strategy is diversification: greater sourcing flexibility, more production inside end markets, stronger European demand, tighter screening of security-sensitive dependencies and continued trade where substitution would be economically costly without improving resilience.

| External relationship | German advantage | German exposure | Structural response |
|---|---|---|---|
| European Union | integrated market, common standards, dense supply chains | weak continental growth transmits quickly | deepen investment and capital-market integration |
| United States | high-value export and investment market | tariff and policy volatility | local production plus export diversification |
| China | scale, supplier depth and large industrial market | import concentration and rising direct competition | selective de-risking, alternative suppliers and technology upgrading |
| Central Europe | efficient manufacturing network | wage convergence and regional security risk | move network toward higher value added and resilience |

## Automotive restructuring is a test of whether engineering depth can become software-intensive industrial leadership

The automotive sector illustrates the broader conversion problem. Germany still has global brands, engineering capability, supplier density, capital and a large installed production base. Those are real assets. But electric propulsion reduces the importance of some legacy mechanical competencies while increasing the importance of batteries, power electronics, software, semiconductors, data architecture and rapid product cycles.

The competitive pressure is therefore not simply that Chinese vehicles may be cheaper. The deeper issue is whether German producers and suppliers can shorten development cycles, secure battery and semiconductor supply, build software competence and preserve margins while financing parallel legacy and electric platforms. A successful transition would keep high-value design, engineering and manufacturing in Germany even if the component mix changes. A weak transition could leave the country with expensive legacy capacity and a shrinking share of the fastest-growing segments.

The United States provides a second stress channel. Destatis reported that German exports of motor vehicles, trailers and semi-trailers to the United States fell 17.8% in 2025; in the first quarter of 2026 vehicle exports to the US were again markedly weaker. This does not by itself prove a permanent loss of competitiveness, but it shows how concentrated sectoral exposure can turn changes in external demand or trade policy into domestic production risk.

## The Mittelstand is an institutional asset, but succession, digitalisation and financing are becoming harder constraints

Germany's medium-sized specialist firms are often treated as a cultural label. Economically, their importance comes from accumulated process knowledge, long supplier relationships, apprenticeship systems, export niches and a willingness to invest in equipment whose return depends on many years of production. This creates capabilities that are difficult to reproduce quickly elsewhere.

The same structure can also slow adjustment. Smaller industrial firms have fewer resources than global groups to absorb energy-price shocks, build AI and software teams, comply with multiple regulatory systems, relocate production or finance expensive decarbonisation. Family ownership can support long investment horizons but creates succession problems when demographic change reduces the pool of owners and skilled managers.

Policy therefore faces a trade-off. Subsidising every incumbent business would preserve low-productivity capacity and weaken competitive selection. Allowing infrastructure, energy and financing bottlenecks to destroy otherwise productive firms would waste accumulated knowledge. The economically relevant distinction is between protecting companies from competition and removing system-level constraints that make investment unnecessarily expensive.

## Coordinated labour institutions can absorb restructuring, but they cannot manufacture workers that do not exist

Works councils, collective bargaining, sectoral agreements and co-determination give German workers institutional influence over restructuring. Combined with vocational education and short-time work instruments, these arrangements can reduce the social cost of adjustment and preserve firm-specific skills during temporary downturns. They also encourage negotiated rather than purely unilateral changes in working conditions.

Their effectiveness is greatest when the problem is cyclical. Demographic contraction is different. Destatis' 16th coordinated population projection shows that, with net migration of 250,000 people per year, the population aged 20 to 66 would still decline by about 4.0 million over the projection horizon; without net immigration, labour-force potential would fall by roughly 6.2 million by 2035 alone. Offsetting that loss through migration would require net annual immigration of about 570,000 people aged 20 to 66 between 2025 and 2035.

Those numbers do not imply a single migration target because labour-force participation, retirement ages, working hours, automation and productivity can change. They do show that demographic arithmetic is too large to be solved by one policy instrument. Germany must combine immigration, faster recognition of qualifications, childcare, older-worker participation, vocational training, housing in high-demand regions and capital investment that raises output per worker.

## Migration is economically necessary in many sectors but integration capacity is itself scarce infrastructure

At the end of 2025 Germany had about 83.5 million residents, 110,000 fewer than a year earlier. Net immigration of 235,000 was insufficient to offset a natural decrease of 352,000 because deaths exceeded births. The immediate policy problem is therefore not simply whether immigration is high or low, but whether the country can convert migration into durable labour-market participation.

Language training, credential recognition, schools, housing, municipal administration and transport determine how quickly newcomers can move into productive employment. These capacities are geographically uneven. A city with labour demand but no affordable housing cannot absorb workers efficiently; a municipality with housing but weak transport may remain disconnected from the jobs that need labour.

This creates a feedback loop between demographic policy and infrastructure policy. Faster immigration without local capacity can intensify housing scarcity and political conflict. Restrictive immigration without a productivity surge can deepen shortages in health care, construction, skilled trades and engineering. The binding constraint is not a single national headcount; it is the matching system between people, skills, housing and firms.

## Regional inequality is less about a simple east-west divide than about industrial geography and fiscal capacity

Reunification reduced many of the institutional and infrastructure gaps between eastern and western Germany, but productivity, headquarters concentration, wealth and demographic structure remain uneven. At the same time, prosperous western industrial regions can be more exposed to automotive or chemical restructuring than some service-oriented cities, while parts of the east have attracted semiconductor, battery and renewable-energy investment.

Municipal fiscal capacity also matters. Germany delegates significant implementation responsibilities to Länder and municipalities, yet the places that need infrastructure most are not always those with the strongest tax base or administrative staff. Federal investment can therefore fail to produce equal outcomes when planning capacity is scarce locally.

The transition should be evaluated at cluster level as well as national level. A country can maintain an export surplus while a specific automotive corridor loses suppliers, or add semiconductor investment while housing scarcity prevents labour mobility. National averages conceal the spatial mechanisms through which structural change becomes politically salient.

## The debt-brake settlement has moved Germany from financial scarcity toward an execution test

The 2025 constitutional changes and the €500 billion Special Fund for Infrastructure and Climate Neutrality altered the fiscal regime. In 2026 the federal budget, Climate and Transformation Fund and infrastructure special fund together provide around €128.7 billion for investment-related expenditure. The special fund has a twelve-year horizon, reducing the risk that multi-year projects depend on a single annual budget.

This does not abolish fiscal constraints. Germany still operates under national constitutional rules, European fiscal rules and political bargaining over recurrent spending. The change is narrower but important: selected infrastructure and security investment can now be financed at a scale that was difficult under the previous interpretation of the debt brake.

The constraint therefore moves downstream. Planning law, procurement, engineering capacity, local co-financing, construction labour and administrative digitalisation become more important than the headline borrowing authority. If project delivery remains slow, additional fiscal space can raise demand without raising supply capacity at the same pace. If execution improves, the same borrowing can increase potential output and reduce maintenance liabilities that would otherwise accumulate.

## The energy transition is a system-integration project rather than a contest between individual technologies

Renewables produced 257.5 TWh in 2025, 58.8% of German electricity generation. That scale means the core technical problem is increasingly temporal and geographic. Solar output is concentrated in daylight hours and seasons; wind output depends on weather and location; industrial load is continuous; transmission capacity is not always where new generation is located.

Germany therefore needs complementary assets: north-south transmission, distribution-grid reinforcement, storage, flexible gas or other dispatchable capacity during the transition, demand response, cross-border interconnection and market rules that value flexibility. The fact that day-ahead prices were negative for 573 hours in 2025 while industry still faced high delivered energy costs shows why annual average generation shares are insufficient measures.

The industrial question is reliability at competitive total cost. Chemicals, metals, glass and other energy-intensive activities cannot base investment decisions on low prices that occur only during surplus hours. A successful transition would turn abundant renewable generation into predictable power contracts, grid access and lower system costs. A poorly coordinated transition could combine high renewable capacity with curtailment, congestion and expensive backup.

## Physical logistics remain part of Germany's macroeconomics

The Rhine episode in 2026 is a reminder that industrial output depends on rivers, railways, bridges and roads as much as on financial conditions. Low water levels raised transport costs and impaired shipments for chemicals, metals, coke and refined petroleum. Similar vulnerabilities arise when major bridges require restrictions or when rail congestion reduces freight reliability.

This is one reason infrastructure investment has a higher potential return in Germany than a generic "stimulus" label suggests. Repairing a bridge or increasing rail capacity can reduce recurrent logistics costs for entire industrial clusters. Digital public administration can also shorten project lead times if it reduces duplicated paperwork and improves coordination across levels of government.

The correct metric is not the amount appropriated. It is whether travel times, freight reliability, grid-connection queues, broadband coverage, permit duration and maintenance backlogs improve. Those indicators connect public capital directly to private productivity.

## Defence spending changes the composition of German industry as well as the public budget

Germany's 2026 fiscal plan sharply increases defence and security resources. The February 2026 federal budget report put defence and security expenditure at roughly €100.9 billion under the relevant budget definition, while separate NATO and special-fund accounting produces different totals. This accounting complexity makes it important not to add figures mechanically.

The structural effect comes through procurement. Long-term orders can justify investment in munitions, vehicles, sensors, electronics, aerospace, cyber capability and maintenance. They can also support dual-use technologies and supplier networks. But defence production competes with civilian industry for engineers, machine tools, electronics and metals, particularly when labour supply is already tightening.

The productivity consequence therefore depends on capacity creation. Imports may be operationally necessary and can improve military capability quickly, but they produce a different domestic industrial effect from contracts that lead firms to build new production lines in Germany or elsewhere in Europe. Defence spending should be evaluated as both a security input and a capital-allocation programme.

## Germany's financial strength is large, but savings do not automatically become domestic productive capital

Germany's external surpluses and household saving have often been interpreted as evidence of national financial strength. That is broadly correct, but the macroeconomic mechanism matters. When domestic investment is weak relative to saving, capital is intermediated abroad and the current account remains in surplus. This can coexist with deteriorating domestic infrastructure.

The euro reinforces this pattern in two ways. It removes exchange-rate risk with Germany's largest European trading partners, supporting cross-border production, while monetary policy is set for the currency union as a whole rather than Germany's cycle alone. Adjustment to country-specific weakness must therefore come through wages, fiscal policy, bank lending, investment and productivity rather than an independent national exchange rate or policy rate.

The current fiscal shift can be understood partly as an attempt to reconnect German saving and public borrowing capacity with domestic fixed capital. Whether private investment follows will be one of the most important indicators of success. Public spending that crowds in factories, housing, grid connections and research has a different long-run effect from spending that mainly raises construction prices.

## Research, engineering and advanced industry give Germany options that a simple decline narrative misses

Germany retains strong universities, Fraunhofer institutes, corporate R&D, machine-tool expertise, chemicals knowledge and industrial automation capabilities. These assets create plausible paths into power electronics, industrial AI, robotics, defence technology, advanced materials, semiconductors and low-carbon production. They also allow established industries to upgrade rather than disappear.

The constraint is commercialization speed. Research capability does not automatically produce globally scaled digital platforms or rapid adoption by medium-sized firms. Fragmented data systems, slow procurement and risk aversion can reduce the return on scientific capacity. High-tech policy should therefore be judged by private adoption, productivity and exportable products rather than announced programmes or research budgets alone.

Germany's comparative advantage is likely to remain strongest where software is embedded in complex physical systems: industrial equipment, vehicles, energy systems, medical technology, aerospace, automation and defence. Losing manufacturing depth would therefore also weaken part of the country's innovation system, because engineering learning is tied to production.

## A contradiction ledger is more useful than a single verdict on German decline

| Proposition | Evidence supporting it | Counterevidence or limitation | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germany is deindustrialising | weak energy-intensive output, employment losses, auto transition | manufacturing depth, exports and specialized firms remain large | sectoral restructuring is real; generalized collapse is not established |
| Fiscal policy has become expansionary | €500bn special fund and much higher 2026 investment envelope | planning and construction capacity constrain delivery | high confidence on financing shift; medium on productivity effect |
| Renewables solve the energy-cost problem | 58.8% of 2025 generation was renewable | grids, storage, backup and delivered industrial prices remain constraints | transition advanced, integration incomplete |
| Demography is a binding constraint | population ageing and projected labour-force decline | migration, participation and automation can offset part of the loss | high |
| China dependence is falling | weaker German exports to China force diversification | Chinese imports and supply-chain exposure remain large | relationship is changing rather than disappearing |
| Rearmament supports industry | large procurement can sustain production lines and R&D | imported systems and labour crowding reduce domestic multiplier | outcome depends on procurement composition |
| Strong institutions guarantee execution | rule of law and federal capacity are durable assets | multi-level permitting and local capacity can slow projects | institutional quality is high; speed is uneven |


## What would change the structural baseline

The baseline would improve if public investment raises construction and infrastructure delivery rather than remaining mainly budget authorization; if electricity grids and storage grow fast enough to turn renewable abundance into lower industrial costs; if private capital spending follows the fiscal impulse; and if labour-force participation, immigration integration and productivity offset ageing.

It would weaken if employment declines broaden across manufacturing and services, if infrastructure execution remains slow despite larger budgets, if automotive and chemicals lose external market share without replacement sectors scaling, or if defence and social spending crowd out growth-enhancing capital formation.

The contrary evidence matters. Germany is not an economy without assets: it retains exceptional industrial density, a skilled workforce, deep savings, strong institutions, a large current-account capacity and access to the EU single market. But those strengths do not guarantee that the old model can continue unchanged. The 2026 regime is best understood as a conversion problem: financial and institutional resources are being redirected toward a new energy, security and infrastructure configuration whose productivity effects are not yet fully visible.
