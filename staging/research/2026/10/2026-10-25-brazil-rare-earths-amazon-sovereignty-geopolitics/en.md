# Rare earths, the Amazon and Brazilian sovereignty: five centuries of natural resources, private power and geopolitical competition

Brazil's rare-earth dispute starts long before a mine is opened or a container of concentrate leaves a port. It begins with the state's ability to understand its geology, enforce mineral rights, regulate industrial contracts, protect territorial rights and select which stages of a sophisticated manufacturing chain can realistically develop at home. This is not merely a competition for deposits. It is a conflict over technology, financing, the ownership of operating companies, the allocation of environmental risks and the limits of strategic autonomy under competition between the United States, China and Europe.

The United States Geological Survey's *Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026* estimates Brazilian rare-earth reserves at approximately **21 million metric tons** of rare-earth-oxide equivalent, compared with **44 million in China**, **6.3 million in Australia** and **1.9 million in the United States**. It reports Brazilian mine production of **2,000 tons** during 2025, against **270,000 tons in China**. Brazil's National Mining Agency, ANM, separately reported production of **326 tons of rare-earth-oxide concentrate**, exports of **$14.9 million** of concentrate and imports worth **$30.6 million** of processed oxides and magnets in 2025. These data use different reporting categories and should not be casually substituted for one another. [1][2]

The central contrast is unmistakable: geological endowment has not yet been converted into industrial dominance. The US Congressional Research Service reports Chinese shares close to **60% of global extraction, 90% of processing and separation and 94% of rare-earth magnet manufacturing**. This means that a country controlling a mine can remain dependent on another country for the equipment and intermediate materials that allow its industries to function. The strategic bottleneck can reside in chemicals, know-how and customer certification rather than underground tonnage. [3]

The evidence also demands restraint. Foreign governments and companies have documented economic interests in Brazilian deposits, including financing and exclusive output agreements. Illegal mining generates genuine armed threats in Amazonian border regions. Yet these facts do not establish the existence of an American, French or European invasion plan to seize Brazilian rare earths. Strategic assessment becomes unreliable when documented commercial leverage and hypothetical warfare are treated as interchangeable.

## 1. Seventeen elements, many markets and different forms of value

Rare-earth elements usually refer to the fifteen lanthanides along with scandium and yttrium. Their chemical similarity makes separation difficult, but their industrial uses vary considerably. Neodymium and praseodymium support high-performance permanent magnets. Dysprosium and terbium improve selected magnets' resistance to high temperatures. Cerium and lanthanum have applications in catalysts, glass and numerous other processes. These materials do not share a single market price or identical strategic value.

The term rare is partly historical: some elements are more abundant in the Earth's crust than popular terminology suggests. Economically useful deposits are rarer because concentrations must be recoverable under specific conditions. A project can contain extensive mineralized rock and still fail because grades are insufficient, impurities are costly to remove or environmental obligations outweigh expected revenue. The technical task is to assess mineralogy, recovery and product composition, not simply to multiply estimated rock mass by a headline oxide price.

The economic chain moves from geology through extraction, beneficiation, chemical separation, metalmaking, alloy production and magnet manufacturing. Each additional stage involves particular expertise, machinery and customer requirements. A mineral deposit does not automatically create a high-value factory. A country seeking strategic independence must evaluate which stages it can operate competitively and which dependencies it can manage through contracts and diversification.

## 2. Occurrences, resources, reserves and research permits are different things

A geological occurrence indicates that a mineral or relevant rock has been identified. A mineral resource normally requires technical estimates of continuity, grade and volume under defined reporting standards. A reserve is the portion judged economically extractable after considering relevant modifying factors. Exploration permits authorize investigations under law; they do not prove that a commercially viable orebody exists.

Brazil's numbers demonstrate how reporting systems matter. The USGS estimated 21 million tons of reserves for 2025, while the ANM's 2025 *Brazilian Mineral Summary* used approximately 11.4 million tons of proved and probable Brazilian reserves for its reference period covering 2024. Without reconciling methods and source vintages, the difference should not be described as a sudden new discovery of nearly ten million tons, still less as direct evidence of manipulation. [1][4]

The ANM reported 3,067 areas in mineral exploration, 78 areas whose final studies had been approved or whose exploitation requests were pending, and 75 SGB-identified locations of mineral potential in October 2026. Only four areas were classified as actively mined, all in Goiás. A 485.4% increase in exploration authorizations between 2021 and 2025 marks interest in future production, not an equivalent expansion of realized output. [2]

## 3. Where Brazil's rare-earth potential is actually located

Brazilian exploration covers several geological settings. Ionic clays in Goiás contain strategically important elements; Minas Gerais has mineralized alkaline complexes and pilot processing projects; older coastal sediments, granites and pegmatites occur in other regions. Minaçu, Goiás, hosts Serra Verde, the country's principal commercial rare-earth operation. Poços de Caldas and Araxá in Minas Gerais have distinct geological and industrial characteristics. The Brazilian Geological Survey, SGB, records additional prospects across the Southeast, Center-West and Amazon. [2][5]

Amazonian locations include Pitinga and Morro dos Seis Lagos in Amazonas, as well as prospects in Roraima, Rondônia and other northern states. These locations should not be presented as a continuous belt of ready-to-mine reserves. Logistics, mineral processing and environmental restrictions differ from site to site. An occurrence within a protected territory is not equivalent to an authorized mine supplying international customers.

This geography challenges the assumption that a rare-earth dispute must be an invasion of the rainforest. Much of the current commercial activity lies in Goiás and Minas Gerais. The Amazon's importance is genuine but involves different mineral deposits, extraordinary ecological assets, frontier policing, Indigenous rights and long distances from industrial infrastructure.

## 4. Colonial Brazil: extraction before modern mineral sovereignty

Portuguese colonization tied territorial control to Atlantic commerce, coercive labor relations and the search for products valuable in European markets. Brazilwood, sugar and gold emerged at different historical stages, each connected to land distribution, colonial taxes and the control of shipping routes. Colonial trade often transferred commercial influence to institutions outside local communities, even when production demanded significant labor and knowledge within Brazil.

Gold mining required transport, engineering and organized taxation, but also relied on slavery and unequal access to economic power. The Crown's fiscal claims demonstrate that public authority could extract substantial revenues without making the surrounding society equal or economically independent. Smuggling and competing private interests further complicated the relationship between formal rule and effective control.

The comparison with contemporary rare earths has limits. Brazil is no longer a colony and now possesses constitutional authority over mineral resources. Foreign investment under Brazilian law is not the same institution as colonial rule. The historical parallel concerns whether resource exports occur without sufficient accumulation of domestic productive capacity and whether the benefits of extraction reach the broader population.

## 5. Indigenous occupation and the myth of uninhabited frontiers

The territory occupied by the modern Brazilian state was home to numerous Indigenous peoples long before Portuguese arrival. Colonial expansion displaced communities, changed ecological relationships and introduced disease and coercive labor. The rhetoric of unoccupied land helped legitimate appropriation even where indigenous political and economic systems had existed for generations.

European colonial authorities used frontier settlements, missions, forts and maps to establish claims, but formal possession often exceeded actual administrative reach. This gap between legal maps and state presence continues to shape discussions of remote border regions, where illegal operators can exploit weak transport, justice and enforcement institutions.

The 1988 Constitution recognizes Indigenous peoples' original rights over traditionally occupied lands and imposes duties on the Brazilian state to protect those rights. Mining in such territories is subject to specific constitutional conditions, not merely a company's willingness to pay compensation. Defending Brazilian sovereignty must include defending communities from unauthorized extraction, not using foreign pressure as an excuse to remove their rights. [6]

## 6. Independence in 1822 and the persistence of external dependence

Political independence gave Brazil control over institutions, diplomacy and the formal direction of its economic affairs, but it did not remove reliance on foreign finance, shipping, machinery or export markets. The Empire continued operating within an Atlantic economy marked by slave labor, agricultural exports and unequal property relations.

This distinction between legal sovereignty and productive autonomy matters today. A government can own the mineral rights beneath its territory while depending on foreign companies for investment, chemical processing, industrial software or downstream buyers. Such economic dependence does not necessarily imply coercion. It becomes a vulnerability when alternatives are absent and an outside actor can change key terms without a credible domestic response.

The historical lesson is that national authority requires material capacities: engineering, administration, credit markets, transport and institutions. Removing foreign participants from a contract would not create those capacities automatically. Nor does permitting foreign investment guarantee that they will develop locally.

## 7. The 1850 Land Law and the origins of contemporary property disputes

Brazil's 1850 Land Law reorganized rules for access to unallocated public land and strengthened purchase as the principal route to lawful private acquisition. It operated within a slave society where poor and formerly enslaved people had limited opportunity to accumulate property. Its legacy is relevant to later land concentration and the distribution of bargaining power over rural territory.

A contemporary farmer may own surface land, receive agricultural income or negotiate access with a miner. That does not confer ownership of underlying deposits. Article 176 of Brazil's Constitution distinguishes mineral rights from ownership of the soil and assigns the deposits to the Union. Mining is carried out under legally authorized arrangements; surface owners possess rights defined separately, including participation in extraction proceeds under applicable law. [7]

Landowners may favor mining when it offers compensation, new roads or service contracts. They may oppose it if water contamination, heavy transport or restrictions on farming threaten existing income. This difference in incentives matters. The claim that Brazilian farmers act collectively to transfer national minerals to foreign states cannot be inferred from the mere existence of large rural properties.

## 8. Imperial geology, transport and mining finance

During the Brazilian Empire, geological knowledge and engineering institutions grew alongside economic demand for minerals and infrastructure. The selection of deposits to study was shaped by prices, access to waterways, industrial customers and financing. Many rare-earth elements had only recently been recognized by chemistry, and the magnet applications that dominate current strategic debates did not yet exist.

Railways, workshops and geological surveys could create productive knowledge while serving private export interests. Companies required finance and trained workers to turn deposits into reliable shipments. This created a tension between export-led income and the development of broader domestic manufacturing capacity.

The historical record cautions against imagining that declaring a mineral strategic is enough to build an industry. Mineral knowledge needs to be transformed into commercially reliable processes. Otherwise, an apparently valuable occurrence remains a geological possibility rather than a source of sustainable income.

## 9. Republican mining policy and industrial state formation

Twentieth-century governments strengthened mineral legislation, public infrastructure and industrial enterprises. The Vargas era and subsequent developmental programmes treated steel, energy and extraction as foundations of national productive capacity. Organizations and companies involved in geology, transport and basic industry sought to create systems that markets alone had not produced quickly.

Legal relations between land and subsoil evolved through different constitutions and codes. The current constitutional regime should not be projected backward unchanged to the colonial or imperial periods. Institutional history demonstrates that sovereignty and concession rights are products of political choices, which can be revised under law.

Mining expansion sometimes generated regional prosperity, jobs and exports, but also produced environmental liabilities and towns dependent on a single operator. Lessons from this experience support strong technical oversight and transparent public finances rather than abstract assumptions that state ownership or private ownership alone guarantees success.

## 10. The military regime and resource-led Amazon development

Brazil's 1964–1985 military governments emphasized territorial integration, strategic resources, energy and national security. Infrastructure and settlement programmes expanded roads and changed land use in the Amazon. State incentives helped bring companies and settlers into new areas, with major economic, social and ecological consequences.

Much of the territory was already occupied by Indigenous peoples and other communities. Infrastructure planning often failed to protect those rights, contributing to displacement, conflict and forest destruction. The record combines industrial expansion with repression, indebtedness and highly uneven distribution of costs and benefits.

The experience illustrates the limits of a purely military conception of sovereignty. Soldiers can protect borders, but a legitimate mineral economy also requires courts, geological surveys, independent environmental agencies and democratic oversight. Treating every environmental dispute as a national-security threat would repeat some of the institutional failures of earlier eras.

## 11. Calha Norte and the physical presence of government

The Calha Norte programme began in 1985 in an environment of concern about weak state presence along northern Amazonian borders. Its early logic combined territorial security, infrastructure and development. Research on its history emphasizes the relationship between military planning, national integration and the complexity of public services in remote areas. [8]

The Defence Ministry has maintained responsibilities associated with military units and frontier infrastructure. Public investment in civilian services has also featured in the broader history of the programme. A barracks or border outpost, however, cannot substitute for schools, hospitals, courts, safe transport and environmental monitoring.

Strategic capability is more than the number of troops stationed in an area. Communications, fuel, medical support, reconnaissance, mobility and coordination with police and other agencies determine practical performance. Weak infrastructure can leave communities exposed to illegal networks even when the state formally claims the territory. These vulnerabilities are more directly evidenced than a foreign military plan to occupy a rare-earth deposit.

## 12. SIVAM and the technology required to govern remote territory

The Amazon Surveillance System, SIVAM, and subsequent information and protection structures sought to improve monitoring of airspace and remote regions. Sensors, radar, mapping and communications can increase the state's awareness of unauthorized aircraft, illegal infrastructure and environmental changes. Yet information has value only when authorities can act on it lawfully and sustainably.

Detecting illegal activity does not automatically dismantle it. Operations require transport, investigations, skilled personnel, fuel and the ability to maintain legitimate control after a temporary intervention. Criminal enterprises can move routes and adapt to enforcement if alternative institutions remain weak.

The system also illustrates technological dependence. Purchasing advanced monitoring equipment abroad may quickly improve capabilities while leaving reliance on external maintenance, specialist components and software. The analogy with rare earths is structural: imported machinery can be indispensable initially, but the eventual level of autonomy depends on accumulated domestic technical expertise.

## 13. Enéas Carneiro and the language of national technological sovereignty

Enéas Carneiro, born in Acre in 1938, founded the nationalist PRONA party and ran for president in 1989, 1994 and 1998 before serving in the Chamber of Deputies. His politics mixed social conservatism, state-oriented nationalism, support for stronger armed forces and criticism of technological dependence. The Chamber's historical profile records his advocacy of a Brazilian nuclear bomb during presidential campaigns, a position in tension with Brazil's peaceful nuclear commitments and international nonproliferation obligations. [9][10]

In a parliamentary speech on 21 August 2003, Enéas argued that Brazil sold primary products cheaply while remaining dependent on higher-value production abroad. He invoked niobium and Amazonian energy resources as examples of what he saw as national underdevelopment and called for scientific and industrial capacity. The speech is a reliable primary source for his own views; its assertions about prices and technical possibilities still require independent scrutiny. [11]

His diagnosis bears a recognizable relation to the rare-earth question: resource ownership alone is insufficient if processing and engineering are controlled elsewhere. Yet nationalism does not replace unit-cost analysis. The price of ore cannot be compared meaningfully with the price of a complex finished product without accounting for chemical composition, capital costs, risks and the services embodied in it.

## 14. Why Enéas's defense arguments do not prove a need for war

Building domestic technology and defending borders are legitimate goals. They do not establish that nuclear weapons are economically necessary for processing minerals or legally consistent with Brazil's constitutional and treaty commitments. Defence policy should be designed around actual risks, strategic alternatives and the lawful institutions responsible for national security.

Warnings about foreign seizure of the Amazon have circulated for decades. Interest in its climate role, genetic resources or mineral deposits does not by itself demonstrate hostile military intent. Foreign states often seek contracts, financing relationships and stable access because these instruments are far less costly and politically disruptive than territorial conquest.

A sound defence assessment evaluates border crime, cyber vulnerabilities, equipment dependence and military contingencies separately. It maintains deterrence without presenting exceptional hypotheticals as observed realities. The greatest immediate security deficiencies may lie in poor monitoring and law enforcement rather than in the absence of an enormous offensive military programme.

## 15. The Constitution and the legal meaning of mineral sovereignty

Article 20 of the Constitution places mineral resources among the Union's assets. Article 176 distinguishes deposits from surface land and requires state authorization or concession for exploration and extraction. A concessionaire obtains ownership of the mineral product extracted under the applicable legal regime; assignment of mineral rights requires competent approval. Companies with foreign capital may operate through the legally permitted corporate structures without converting the deposit itself into foreign sovereign territory. [6][7]

Article 231 recognizes Indigenous territorial rights and prescribes distinct requirements for mineral activity in Indigenous areas, including congressional authorization and consultation with affected communities. These rights are not simply negotiable conveniences to be displaced by a commercial claim of strategic importance.

Several forms of authority coexist. The Union holds mineral title and controls the concession framework, companies own shares and contract rights, farmers own surface interests, and constitutionally protected communities hold territorial rights. A statement that a mine was “sold to foreigners” must identify the legal object transferred. Corporate stock, an offtake agreement, mining authorization and sovereignty over territory are not the same thing.

## 16. Farmers, surface interests and speculation around deposits

Rural landowners have different incentives depending on property size, water sources, farming activity and the location of mineral prospects. A landowner expecting service contracts and compensation may favor mining. A cattle producer or crop farmer facing reduced access to water may oppose it. Both can act within legitimate interests, and neither is necessarily representative of all Brazilian agriculture.

Exploration announcements can increase surrounding land prices even when no economically viable reserve has been established. Speculators may purchase land based on anticipated infrastructure or access agreements, while junior mining companies seek rights to investigate the subsurface. This creates information asymmetry between well-financed participants and smallholders who may lack legal or geological assistance.

Potential conflicts with agrarian settlements or unallocated public land require parcel-level evidence. The ANM's mining-rights registry should be compared with official rural cadasters, conservation areas and recognized community territories. Claims that named farmers have conspired with foreign individuals to transfer deposits require deeds, contracts and verifiable beneficial-ownership information, not inferred motives.

## 17. Kalunga territory and judicial protection of consultation rights

The Kalunga quilombola territory in northern Goiás demonstrates how new mineral exploration can intersect with older failures of land regularization. In January 2026, Brazil's Federal Public Prosecutor's Office reported a judgment requiring an integrated plan to complete titling of approximately 262,000 hectares. Only about 34,000 hectares had been definitively titled, and the lack of legal resolution had facilitated land disputes. [12]

The same institution reported in November 2025 that the mining agency had suspended certain exploration requests in the territory, complying in part with a judicial ruling concerning the lack of free, prior and informed consultation. The dispute involved rights under International Labour Organization Convention 169 and proceedings still subject to further review. [13]

These facts do not establish that every mining project in Goiás is illegal or that all exploration affects the same protected polygon. They show why cadastral overlays, project-specific consultation and transparent boundaries matter. Effective protection can reduce the likelihood of confrontation, support predictable investment and prevent irreversible harm while legal questions remain unresolved.

## 18. The Cerrado as a center of rare-earth investment

The Cerrado contains economically important agricultural land, river headwaters and geological environments favorable to ionic-clay rare-earth deposits. These activities interact through water use, road access, vegetation and land rights. Mining can create services and jobs while imposing environmental costs that extend beyond the concession boundary.

Ionic-clay extraction uses chemical routes different from conventional hard-rock mining. Some ions can be recovered from clays through leaching processes, but outcomes depend on soil chemistry, reagents, recovery efficiency and environmental controls. Neither low crushing requirements nor the label green technology guarantees an environmentally harmless process.

The Mining Observatory's September 2026 review identified overlaps between mining claims, settlements and vulnerable territories in Goiás. As an independent investigative source, its geographic conclusions should be checked against official coordinates, permit conditions and project documents. Its value is to raise testable concerns about water, consultation and cumulative effects rather than to replace administrative or technical evidence. [14]

## 19. Serra Verde and USA Rare Earth: a documented contractual turning point

On 11 May 2026, Brazil's competition authority CADE announced an administrative procedure involving Serra Verde Pesquisa e Mineração and USA Rare Earth. According to the agency, the firms had agreed to create a multinational rare-earth business spanning eight operations across Brazil, the United States, France and the United Kingdom. The proposed chain would cover mining, separation, metallization and magnet manufacturing. [15]

CADE also reported a **15-year supply contract** committing **100% of Serra Verde's Phase I output** to a special-purpose structure backed by US government agencies and private capital, with minimum prices for relevant magnetic rare earths. Such an arrangement may reduce financing risk and secure access for an American-aligned supply chain. It does not confer American territorial sovereignty over Goiás or automatically encompass every future project stage.

The authority's proceeding examines competition and notification requirements. Its commencement does not establish an antitrust violation. Nevertheless, it gives a concrete case for studying who controls output destinations, processing technology and bargaining power after an investment. Sovereignty can be diluted commercially without any change in national borders, especially when alternatives are limited and contracts are difficult to renegotiate.

## 20. American financing and the business logic of diversification

The United States seeks alternative rare-earth supplies because China dominates chemical processing and magnet manufacturing. American defense, aerospace, automotive and electronics firms need reliable access to magnet inputs. Public finance, development agencies and private investors can support mining and processing ventures abroad as part of a strategy to reduce concentrated supply-chain risk. [3]

Brazil's Mines and Energy Ministry referenced a proposed **$565 million US Development Finance Corporation financing structure** for Serra Verde in its 2026 national-strategy study. The reported financing is not necessarily equivalent to the entire amount of funds already disbursed or to ownership of all the company's assets. It is evidence of substantial US financial interest in an alternative industrial supply chain. [16]

The interests of Brazilian project sponsors can align with those of American lenders when financing turns otherwise uneconomic reserves into producing assets. Tensions emerge when exclusivity, pricing, location of refining or intellectual-property rights limit domestic industrial options. The most useful test concerns actual contract terms and retained technological capabilities, not the nationality of the financing institution alone.

## 21. Individual investors, beneficial ownership and the difference between contracts and crimes

Rare-earth ventures often use holding companies, investment funds, loans, offtake structures and subsidiary contracts. Shareholders own claims on corporate value, lenders hold repayment rights and buyers receive contractual claims on specified output. These interests are related but legally distinct. An executive appearing in a transaction announcement does not necessarily exercise ultimate control over a mine or personally own the deposit.

The Serra Verde–USA Rare Earth deal offers sufficient public evidence to evaluate its broad commercial design. Accusations that particular foreign individuals or Brazilian landowners engaged in bribery, fraud or illegal collusion would require further documentary or judicial proof. Even CADE's examination of the announced transaction does not itself establish misconduct. [15]

Effective research should identify ultimate beneficial owners, voting rights, related-party prices, collateral, guaranteed supply volumes and the fiscal location of returns. These facts show whether economic decisions can be influenced by entities outside Brazil and whether the country receives appropriate compensation. Speculative lists of powerful foreigners are an inadequate substitute for company records and regulatory filings.

## 22. France, the United Kingdom and where value is added

CADE described operations in France and the United Kingdom within the proposed rare-earth group. Such arrangements may distribute extraction, chemical separation, metallization and magnet manufacturing among jurisdictions with different industrial capabilities. They can connect Brazilian output to technologically sophisticated markets without necessarily bringing every profitable production stage to Brazil. [15]

European policy seeks to diversify critical-material supplies and reduce dependence on concentrated Asian refining. At the company level, Australian, Canadian, European and Brazilian investors pursue different projects in Goiás and Minas Gerais. In September 2026, Brazil's development bank approved **R$77.5 million** for a Viridis research and processing facility in Poços de Caldas, including pilot production of mixed rare-earth carbonate and work on metallurgical routes. Pilot output demonstrates a stage of technological learning, not yet a complete world-scale magnet supply chain. [17]

It is essential to distinguish national governments, European institutions and private businesses. French sovereignty over French Guiana is unrelated in law to a company's ownership of a European chemical processing plant. The strategic test is whether financing agreements create Brazilian skills, process knowledge, equipment and business opportunities or leave domestic firms restricted to supplying unrefined materials.

## 23. French Guiana and the real meaning of the Brazilian-French border

French Guiana is French territory and therefore part of France's presence in South America. Its border with Brazil's state of Amapá creates an unusually direct European–South American frontier through forests and rivers. That geography generates legitimate security, environmental and policing issues independent of rare-earth investment.

In January 2025, Brazil announced a bilateral agreement to improve cooperation against illegal gold mining. In March of the same year, the French Armed Forces Ministry described Operation JARARACA, in which Brazilian and French military personnel coordinated to dismantle illegal mining infrastructure near the frontier. These reports document joint operations with Brazilian participation, not an unauthorized French incursion to capture mineral deposits. [18][19]

The strategic risks in this area include cross-border money laundering, smuggling, mercury contamination, armed criminal groups and accidental incidents between enforcement teams. Effective sovereignty requires coordination, clear rules and command accountability. Asserting that French participation in a joint operation proves preparation for a future invasion would invert the evidentiary meaning of the reported activity.

## 24. Illegal gold mining and the specific reality of armed crime

Illegal gold extraction has created serious security risks in Amazonian areas. Profit can be generated from small, easily transferred quantities, and criminal networks may control fuel, transport, weapons, access routes and laundering channels. In January 2026, Interpol reported an operation involving Brazil, French Guiana, Guyana and Suriname, with 198 arrests related to cross-border illegal mining and associated offenses. [20]

Gold's market characteristics differ from rare-earth concentrates. It has very high value per unit of weight and can be sold through established trading channels. Rare-earth production usually depends on bulky materials, chemical operations and specialized industrial customers. These differences mean that illegal-mining patterns cannot simply be transposed from gold to rare earths, although corruption, illegal access and territorial intimidation could affect either sector.

The most visible armed conflict mechanism is thus criminal and localized rather than a confrontation between regular armies. Protecting communities requires financial investigations, lawful enforcement, environmental monitoring and alternative livelihoods alongside military or police mobility. Persistent state presence matters more than short-lived operations whose targets immediately reorganize across another river or border.

## 25. Seis Lagos and the option not to mine

Morro dos Seis Lagos, in northwestern Amazonas, has been studied by Brazil's Geological Survey as a significant mineralized formation. Its strategic relevance must be considered together with remote logistics, legal restrictions and territorial protections. A report identifying mineral potential does not establish an operating commercial reserve with environmental permission and signed purchase contracts. [5][21]

In remote locations, extracting and moving material may require expensive energy, roads, river transport and long supply chains. Physical works can create lasting impacts on communities and ecological systems. Where protected territory is involved, exploration and extraction are subject to the constitutional and statutory conditions governing those areas.

Preserving a deposit or postponing its exploitation is not equivalent to giving it to a foreign power. It can be an exercise of public sovereignty when knowledge, ecological conditions and legal constraints make immediate mining inappropriate. That choice requires enforcement strong enough to keep illegal operators from exploiting the area while the government evaluates future options.

## 26. Pitinga and other northern geological environments

Pitinga in Amazonas has an established history of tin extraction and associated mineralization. Other parts of the Amazon, including locations in Roraima and Rondônia, have received geological attention for materials such as niobium, phosphates and rare earths. The nature of these deposits differs from the ionic-clay projects in Goiás. [5][21]

By-product recovery may improve economic viability where existing operations already possess equipment and infrastructure. Recovering rare earths from another mining or processing flow could reduce some fixed costs, but would still require suitable chemistry, environmental compliance and reliable markets. Mineral-bearing tailings do not automatically become profitable inventories.

The Amazon must not be described as one homogeneous orebody. Mineral grades, settlement patterns, ecological sensitivities and transport routes vary dramatically. Policy should support scientific mapping while refusing to presume that every occurrence ought to become an active mine. Strategic use of resources includes carefully deciding which projects should remain undeveloped.
