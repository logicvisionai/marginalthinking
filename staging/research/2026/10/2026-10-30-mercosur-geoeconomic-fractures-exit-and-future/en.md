# Mercosur under pressure: history, strategic competition, political divergence and the economics of a possible exit

**30 October 2026 · Marginal Thinking · Political Economy & Markets · Long-form research**

Mercosur is not simply a tariff arrangement that can be judged by whether four governments agree on foreign policy. It was created as an institutional response to an earlier rivalry between Argentina and Brazil; it grew into a partial customs union and a manufacturing market; and it now operates in an international economy where China buys much of the region's commodity production, the United States uses bilateral trade and strategic-security instruments, and the European Union has begun to provisionally apply a negotiated trade opening. These pressures pull members in different directions without necessarily making withdrawal economically rational. [1][3][4][6][8]

The central tension in 2026 is **between shared market access and independent commercial diplomacy**. Argentina's agreement with the United States tests how far a member can move without changing a common external tariff. Uruguay seeks diversification beyond a bloc dominated by two large economies. Brazil simultaneously depends on China as a commodity buyer, values access to the United States and seeks to protect industrial capabilities. Paraguay balances trade interests with a diplomatic relationship with Taiwan; Bolivia is still incorporating the bloc's legal framework. No single ideological label explains those incentives. [5][6][13][14][16][17]

The question is not merely whether Mercosur survives. A customs union can survive as a legal organization while losing capacity to coordinate trade policy; conversely, it can permit narrow negotiated flexibility and become more valuable. The analysis below reconstructs the political origin, the distribution of benefits and costs, external leverage, the actual exit provisions, and five conditional futures. **Information cutoff: 30 October 2026 (São Paulo time).** Figures retain their own observation years; scenarios are not forecasts.

## 1. The political bargain that preceded the customs union

The 1991 Treaty of Asunción followed the democratic rapprochement initiated by Argentina and Brazil in the mid-1980s. The 1985 Iguaçu Declaration mattered because trade liberalization was built on a decision to replace strategic suspicion with mutual predictability. The bilateral relationship involved confidence-building in sensitive sectors, including nuclear affairs, as well as industrial cooperation. This chronology matters for withdrawal calculations: ending trade preferences would not abolish diplomatic relations, but it would weaken one of the institutions through which historical competitors manage friction. Paraguay and Uruguay joined because access to their larger neighbors and a voice in rule-making were valuable even when the large economies determined much of the region's demand. [1][3]

The original promise was ambitious: freer movement of goods, services and factors, a common external tariff, sectoral coordination and policy harmonization. An institution built to solve several problems at once can persist after failing to complete one objective. A firm exporting automobile parts, a worker with regional residence rights and a foreign ministry seeking coordinated bargaining may value different pieces of the organization. Judging success or failure from aggregate trade alone misses those separate dimensions.

## 2. A chronology of institutional consolidation and recurring shocks

| Period | Institutional or economic turning point | Consequence for cohesion |
|---|---|---|
| 1985–1990 | Argentina–Brazil democratic rapprochement and industrial cooperation | Reduced political rivalry before the tariff project |
| 1991 | Treaty of Asunción and trade-liberalization timetable | Common-market commitment |
| 1994–1996 | Ouro Preto protocol and institutional consolidation | Legal personality, consensus, common trade framework |
| 1998–2004 | Ushuaia democratic clause and Olivos dispute settlement | Political membership standards and adjudication |
| 1999–2002 | Brazilian exchange-rate adjustment and Argentine crisis | Exposed lack of macroeconomic convergence |
| 2005–2012 | Structural fund, Parliament and Venezuelan accession | Greater institutional and political breadth |
| 2016–2017 | Venezuelan suspension under different legal grounds | Membership rights shown to be conditional |
| 2024 | Bolivia becomes a State Party, with phased rule incorporation | Geographic enlargement without immediate uniformity |
| 2025–2026 | EU signature, US–Argentina agreement, EFTA and Singapore implementation | External liberalization and bilateral-flexibility disputes |

This is not an uninterrupted trajectory toward the European Union model. A common market was the stated destination; the actual institution remains intergovernmental, its customs union imperfect, and its implementation of regional decisions uneven. The chronology explains why a new ideological disagreement can be accommodated for years while an apparently narrow tariff dispute can reach the organization's core legal promise. [1][3][14][19]

## 3. How Mercosur makes decisions—and why smaller states retain leverage

The Common Market Council provides the political direction; the Common Market Group executes; the Trade Commission addresses commercial instruments. Under the Ouro Preto framework, major decisions are made by consensus, and many regional norms require national incorporation before practical effect. This protects a small member against simple voting domination by Brazil but also gives each government an opportunity to delay rules it regards as costly. The organization has legal personality, but it is not a supranational treasury, customs administration or parliament capable of overriding national executives in ordinary trade policy. [2][3]

Consensus therefore produces a dual effect. It can stabilize membership by making a small government heard; and it can paralyze negotiations when national objectives diverge. A proposal to give individual members greater negotiating freedom is neither merely a technical change nor necessarily an abandonment of integration. Its effect depends on whether exceptions are jointly defined, transparent and enforceable, or instead become a series of unilateral decisions that erase the external tariff.

## 4. Legal ambition versus actual commercial integration

Mercosur has liberalized most intra-bloc tariff lines, but vehicles, sugar and special customs zones follow particular arrangements. The bilateral Brazil–Argentina automotive regime continues to be operated through Economic Complementation Agreement 14. These exceptions matter because the region's most integrated industrial chains are not necessarily governed by the simplest free-trade rule. Investment has adapted to negotiated thresholds, rules of origin and sectoral calendars. A sudden institutional break can thus create costs even if either side later maintains zero headline tariffs. [18][19]

A customs union also differs from a free-trade area: members are supposed to coordinate treatment of imports from third countries. When one country unilaterally eliminates an external tariff, manufacturers elsewhere may fear transshipment or a shift in sourcing toward the most permissive external gateway. Origin checks, customs controls and anti-circumvention measures become more important, raising costs for compliant companies as well as potential opportunistic traders.

## 5. The scale of commerce: large external exposure and limited internal density

Brazilian foreign ministry data for 2023, excluding Bolivia, report Mercosur's trade with the world at **US$684.5 billion**, including **US$379 billion in exports**. Intra-bloc trade reached approximately **US$49 billion** in 2023, versus **US$4.5 billion** in 1991. The different measures must not be simply divided into an import or export share without consistent denominators and membership coverage. They show that integration expanded from its original base while global demand grew into the dominant commercial reference. [4]

The destination distribution of Mercosur exports in 2023 was **China 29%, EU 14%, United States 12%** according to that same ministry source. The remainder comprises many markets and cannot be assigned to one comparable geopolitical camp. These are *goods-export destination shares in 2023 for the ministry's defined bloc*, not 2026 forecasts or measures of foreign political control.

The exposure is asymmetric. China's purchases matter disproportionately for agricultural and mineral exporters; regional partners are particularly relevant to selected manufactured goods, vehicles and intermediates. A dollar of soybean sales and a dollar of automotive components can have different effects on domestic employment, research intensity and the survival of supplier networks. A policy that maximizes commodity export revenue does not automatically maximize industrial learning.

## 6. Brazil: scale, export diversification and the industrial dilemma

Brazil is the bloc's largest economy and its industrial anchor, but dominance in market size does not mean it can dictate regional rules. The IMF's 2026 assessment reports that China's share of Brazilian exports rose from roughly 3% in the early 1990s to nearly 30% in 2025; Mercosur's share fell from a late-1990s peak near 19% to about 8% in 2025. Those are Brazil-specific series, not the previous chart's bloc-wide 2023 shares. [15]

Brazil faces a two-sided tradeoff. Lower tariffs can reduce the price of capital goods and inputs and increase competition; rapid liberalization can also pressure domestic firms when taxation, transport, credit and exchange rates remain unfavorable. Conversely, retaining tariffs indefinitely can preserve inefficient production and make firms less productive. The policy question is which domestic capabilities can plausibly become competitive, what adjustment periods are justified, and who pays for them. This is why China's scale, US market access and the EU opening each pull Brazil toward different selective compromises rather than a single geopolitical alignment.

## 7. Argentina: stabilization strategy, industrial exposure and negotiating autonomy

Argentina has repeatedly combined a relatively integrated relationship with Brazilian manufacturing with currency and macroeconomic regimes very different from Brazil's. The peso's real value, import licensing, foreign-exchange availability and tax conditions have often moved cross-border profitability more than the formal Mercosur tariff. A government's desire to liberalize rapidly is therefore partly a domestic stabilization and productivity strategy, not only a diplomatic preference. Yet external market opening can affect automobile, machinery, textile and other producers before financing and infrastructure constraints adjust. [18][21]

The concrete 2026 test is the **US–Argentina Agreement on Reciprocal Trade and Investment**, signed on 5 February. Washington describes broader access for American goods, including motor vehicles and agricultural products. Brazilian officials subsequently reviewed potential inconsistencies with Mercosur rules, while Argentine officials argued that relevant concessions could fit existing exemptions. That is an unresolved legal-policy disagreement in the cited February reporting, not established proof that Argentina had left or formally denounced Mercosur. [6][7]

A useful distinction is between the agreement's announcement, signature, legal domestic approval, item-by-item implementation and consistency with Mercosur obligations. They are separate stages. Neither a presidential speech nor a bilateral document automatically dissolves regional treaty commitments.

## 8. Uruguay: the small-economy demand for options

Uruguay's rationale differs from Argentina's in scale and economic structure. Access to Brazil and Argentina is valuable, but its incentives to reach overseas meat, dairy, forestry, services and investment markets are strong. A common tariff can become politically controversial if two larger neighbors delay negotiations important to a small exporter. Uruguay's engagement with China predates the present ideological contrasts, and President Yamandú Orsi's February 2026 mission to Beijing aimed to deepen economic ties and attract investment. The visit is documented; a completed unilateral free-trade agreement with China is not established by that fact. [16]

Uruguay can therefore press for negotiating flexibility without preferring full treaty withdrawal. The actual alternatives range from bloc-level trade diplomacy to narrow waivers, bilateral sectoral accords consistent with regional law, or exit followed by an attempt to renegotiate access to its neighbors. The final option would require replacing market-access rights that firms currently take for granted.

## 9. Paraguay: geography, asymmetry and the Taiwan constraint

Paraguay depends heavily on logistics through neighboring territories and on regional electricity, agriculture and investment relationships. Its smaller market gives it less bargaining weight with global powers but a consensus vote within Mercosur. The Paraná–Paraguay waterway, roads and regional energy systems illustrate a practical layer of integration that tariffs cannot replicate. Since Paraguay has diplomatic relations with Taiwan, potential Mercosur–China talks also encounter an institutional obstacle unrelated to the rate of tariff reduction. Reporting in February 2026 highlighted both Brazil's exploratory openness to narrower talks and Paraguay's diplomatic constraint. [13][17]

This does not imply that trade with China is impossible for Paraguay, or that a China-wide accord is the only path to commercial growth. It means the political design of negotiations, recognition requirements and product coverage are unusually consequential for that member. Unilateral market opening could lower some input costs but would not solve river transport, electricity governance or border delays by itself.

## 10. Bolivia and Venezuela: enlargement is not the same as active membership

Bolivia became a State Party in July 2024 and has up to four years from its ratification step to incorporate the acquis. The country is therefore not equivalent to a founding member that has already implemented decades of common rules. Its potential contribution includes Andean transport access, energy links and mineral resources, but these become practical integration only after infrastructure, customs and regulatory adaptation. The EU–Mercosur instruments signed in January 2026 cover the four founding participants, not Bolivia. [8][14]

Venezuela is a different case. It is formally listed as a State Party but remains suspended from the rights and obligations attached to membership; this is **not** an ordinary voluntary departure. Treating Venezuela as a regular trading member or describing Bolivia as merely a candidate would distort the 2026 institutional map. The cases also demonstrate that sanctions related to democratic standards and disputes over tariff flexibility operate under distinct legal mechanisms. [2][14]

```map
title: Regional positions in Mercosur, October 2026
Brazil and Argentina | Founding industrial and commercial core | Automotive ties and different macroeconomic strategies
Paraguay and Uruguay | Founding smaller economies | Consensus rights and demand for external market access
Bolivia | State Party in transition | Acquis incorporation after July 2024
Venezuela | State Party suspended | No active membership rights and obligations
China, United States, EU | External trade and political partners | Different tools, not a unified geopolitical bloc
```

## 11. The United States: bilateral agreements, tariffs and strategic competition

US trade diplomacy works through market access, reciprocal tariff bargaining, rules, industrial policy and broader security relationships. In 2026 the US–Argentina agreement creates a concrete test of whether a major member can establish preferential conditions independently. For Brazil, US tariff measures and trade-policy differences influence diversification incentives. A Congressional Research Service report dated 29 October 2026 reviews contemporary US–Brazil tariff disputes and recalls the failure of an earlier Free Trade Area of the Americas project. [6][15][22]

The United States does not need to demand Mercosur's dissolution to alter its incentives. A bilateral concession valuable to one member can generate a temptation to reinterpret common rules; a restriction on another member can encourage it to diversify. But a political statement of friendship with Washington is not evidence of a credible withdrawal plan. The contractual terms, domestic approval processes and affected tariff lines are the appropriate observable evidence.

## 12. The European Union: a negotiated opening with incomplete ratification

The EU–Mercosur political agreement was renewed in December 2024. Two instruments were signed on **17 January 2026**: a comprehensive partnership agreement and an interim trade agreement. **The latter has applied provisionally since 1 May 2026**; the full partnership agreement awaits further European ratifications, and the interim accord is not the same thing as final ratification of the whole package. On 21 January, the European Parliament requested a Court of Justice opinion, delaying its consent process. These legal distinctions govern the degree of certainty available to exporters and investors. [8][9]

The agreement opens opportunities for Mercosur agribusiness, services and selected manufacturers while exposing domestic industries to European competition. European farm interests, environmental standards, sanitary controls and safeguard mechanisms shape effective access even where tariffs are reduced. The Brazilian government's published schedule says Mercosur's offer covers about **91% of tariff codes** and **85% of Brazil's import value from the EU**, with phase-ins and exclusions; these are schedule-coverage measures, not an estimate of GDP or realized welfare gains. [8][21]

Neither blanket endorsement nor categorical rejection follows from those schedules. Productivity, rules of origin, exchange rates, infrastructure, firm capabilities and implementation determine outcomes over time.

## 13. The EFTA and Singapore agreements: why dates and participants matter

Mercosur's external opening is not limited to the EU. EFTA's agreement with the four founders was signed in September 2025. It entered into force for **Brazil and Iceland on 1 October 2026**; EFTA lists **1 November** as the planned entry-into-force date for Uruguay and Norway, which was still future at this article's cutoff. The status of Argentina, Paraguay, Liechtenstein and Switzerland was still pending on EFTA's account. It would be incorrect to describe the entire agreement as universally in force. [10]

The Singapore agreement offers another staggered example: it began applying for Paraguay and Uruguay in February and March 2026 respectively and for Brazil and Singapore in August, while Argentine ratification was still pending in Singapore's August statement. Differences in domestic implementation do not automatically signify political exit. They are a normal feature of an intergovernmental trading architecture. [11]

## 14. India, Asia and the cost of neglecting other partners

In September 2026 Mercosur and India launched negotiations to expand their existing preferential agreement; India also announced a protocol concerning electronic certificates of origin. The distinction between **launching negotiations**, **signing a procedural protocol** and **bringing a new trade agreement into force** is essential. The original preferences cover a limited set of tariff lines, far short of comprehensive free trade. [12][23]

Asia is not synonymous with China. India, Singapore, Japan, South Korea and Southeast Asian production networks offer distinct markets and competitive pressures. Their value may lie in pharmaceuticals, services, manufacturing inputs, digital documentation and investment as much as commodity exports. Mercosur could use common negotiation to obtain scale while allowing differentiated phase-ins; without common strategy, countries can race to offer concessions that diminish their leverage collectively.

## 15. China: commercial centrality without a common strategic doctrine

China's position follows from real demand for soybeans, minerals, energy and food, and from its supply of manufactured inputs, equipment and consumer goods. The 2023 ministry figures show a large export and import relationship. These trade shares do not demonstrate control over Mercosur policy or uniform political preference across its governments. Brazil's tentative openness to China-oriented bloc talks reported in 2026 coexists with manufacturers' concerns about import competition; Argentina's US alignment and Paraguay's Taiwan diplomacy further complicate consensus. [4][17]

A broad free-trade agreement would have different consequences from a narrow tariff-preference arrangement or customs facilitation. Agriculture might gain market security, while import-competing sectors would face sharper competition. Even the direction of net trade diversion depends on origin rules, exchange rates and the baseline tariffs. China also has an incentive to negotiate bilaterally where political and economic institutions permit; that prospect can itself change Mercosur's bargaining position.

## 16. Two competitive logics of integration

There are at least two coherent but competing approaches. One holds that common external negotiation gives Mercosur scale: a bloc can exchange concessions with major markets on terms that a small member cannot obtain alone. The other argues that consensus delays valuable openings and leaves members dependent on the slowest government's preferences. Both contain mechanisms worth testing; neither implies that all countries experience the same welfare effects.

A functioning compromise would require a clearly delimited exception system, notification, enforcement of origin rules and safeguards for members suffering trade diversion. An unbounded right to negotiate individually would turn the common external tariff into a nominal label. Conversely, refusing every flexibly designed opening can lead governments to seek legally ambiguous workarounds and erode trust. Institutional design—not whether leaders share an ideology—determines the durability of the compromise.

```flow
Common external tariff and joint bargaining → shared negotiating scale → potential access gains and internal coordination costs
Unilateral external concession → differential tariffs and rules → origin checks and trade-diversion risk → pressure for waivers or legal dispute
```

## 17. The external tariff exceptions already contain a laboratory of flexibility

In October 2026 Mercosur's consolidated rules authorized ordinary national lists of up to **100 tariff codes each for Argentina and Brazil, 649 for Paraguay and 225 for Uruguay**, with distinct expiry dates. They also authorized **50 temporary codes per founder** under specific conditions, including a test tied to recent partners' exports for those codes. The combined headline maximum is not a finding that every country actually uses all available exceptions, nor that every bilateral treaty concession qualifies for one. [5]

```chart
type: bar
title: Ordinary authorized national exceptions to the common external tariff, effective 4 Oct 2026
unit: maximum NCM tariff codes (not actual use; excludes temporary lists)
Argentina | 100
Brazil | 100
Paraguay | 649
Uruguay | 225
```

The differentiated caps reveal how the organization already negotiates asymmetry: the country with more structural dependence on external inputs is allowed greater room to deviate. However, a list of exceptions to the common tariff is legally and economically different from a reciprocal agreement that binds a country to permanent treatment of a foreign partner. A proper compatibility audit must compare individual NCM lines, origin requirements, coverage dates and services or technical provisions, not merely total agreement items.

## 18. Can Argentina legally leave? The Treaty sets out a route

Articles **21 and 22** of the Treaty of Asunción establish a withdrawal procedure. A member must communicate its intention expressly and formally to the others and, within **60 days**, deliver the instrument of denunciation to Paraguay's foreign ministry. Once denunciation is formalized, the withdrawing state ceases to enjoy and bear the general rights and obligations of member status, while trade-liberalization provisions and mutually agreed aspects continue for **two years**. [24]

This is neither a UK-style Article 50 process nor immediate tariff abolition. The two-year continuation is a legal transition, not a guarantee that all later agreements, investments, citizenship arrangements, sectoral rules and external preferences automatically survive. Political negotiations and domestic legislation would still determine much of the practical outcome. Crucially, no documented formal Argentine denunciation was identified in the source record reviewed through the cutoff; ideological tension alone is not an exit notice.

## 19. The economics of exit: four separate accounts

| Channel | Potential gains from greater autonomy | Exposure requiring negotiation |
|---|---|---|
| External tariff | Faster bilateral preferences, lower import input costs | Loss of common bargaining weight, origin/circumvention questions |
| Intra-bloc goods | Reopen policy choices over domestic industry | Preferential access, automotive regimes, compliance costs |
| Investment and supply chains | Freedom to redesign incentives | Uncertainty over plants and supplier contracts spanning borders |
| Institutions and mobility | Domestic rule-making independence | Residence, professional, customs, legal and dispute-settlement arrangements |

An exit estimate is not credible without counterfactual agreements, tariff-line exposure, input-output tables, sector employment, border-cost estimates and a timetable. If Argentina could retain nearly all bilateral preferences through new arrangements, the trade cost could be limited; if renegotiation failed and automotive linkages were disrupted, the same formal withdrawal could be expensive. An exact GDP-loss percentage unsupported by a model would give the appearance of precision without evidence.

## 20. Why the Argentine automotive corridor matters beyond headline exports

Brazilian and Argentine automakers buy components, make investment decisions and allocate vehicle models across a cross-border production system governed partly by specific automotive agreements. A manufacturer does not choose plant location only from one tariff rate: parts availability, local-content rules, exchange rates, labor cost, tax rules, financing and predicted future policy also matter. Exit uncertainty can delay investment before any border tariff changes. [18]

This is a reason to distinguish *trade creation* from *trade diversion*. Regional preferences can allow suppliers to attain scale and generate learning, but they can also divert imports from more productive outside suppliers. Without firm-level counterfactuals, describing every regional production job as a benefit of protection or every protected job as waste would be unwarranted. A credible reform would measure productivity, entry and exit, wage effects, import content and export performance by sector.

## 21. Logistics, electricity and geography are sticky integration assets

The Paraná–Paraguay river system, road and rail border corridors, electrical interconnections and binational energy facilities bind neighbors regardless of ideological alliances. A transport bottleneck can eliminate the savings from lower customs duties; an electricity interconnection can protect grid reliability without creating a common energy market. These assets are expensive, fixed in place and governed by contracts or binational arrangements beyond the Mercosur trade framework.

Paraguay's position between large markets, Uruguay's port role, Bolivia's Andean links and Argentina's and Brazil's industrial corridors create interdependence that could outlast formal membership. In an exit case, cross-border transport and energy agreements might remain in force on their own legal bases, but customs coordination and investment predictability could deteriorate. Physical interdependence is an incentive to negotiate; it is not a legal prohibition on departure.

## 22. Monetary and fiscal non-convergence as recurring sources of grievance

Mercosur has never operated a single currency or a shared fiscal stabilization budget comparable to a monetary union. Exchange-rate shocks change relative prices quickly. When a currency depreciates, exporters can become more competitive while industries in neighbors demand protection; importers relying on external currency may face abrupt financing constraints. Inflation dispersion complicates comparisons of wages and productivity. Those problems are not automatically caused by Mercosur, and leaving would not eliminate exchange-rate volatility.

Macroeconomic coordination requires credible national institutions, transparently monitored trade restrictions and early warning of capital-control or licensing changes. Agreements designed only around tariffs leave major determinants of commerce outside their scope. One reason the bloc seems politically quarrelsome is that incompatible macroeconomic cycles repeatedly enter trade negotiations through firms' complaints about unfair competition.

## 23. Climate, agriculture and sustainability as trade bargaining, not decoration

Mercosur's agricultural resources make it attractive to the EU and China, but also create disputes about land use, traceability, sanitary controls, deforestation and domestic farm protection abroad. A stricter import standard can reward traceable producers, exclude firms lacking compliance capacity or operate as a de facto barrier depending on design and implementation. Evidence needs to distinguish the environmental purpose, the cost of verification and the effect on market access. [8][21]

Energy transition and critical minerals may generate new investment, particularly in processing and transport, but geological abundance is not the same as refining capacity or retained local value added. If Mercosur's export growth consists mostly of raw materials while importing sophisticated machinery, the distributional issue shifts from tariffs to technology, investment and industrial services. The appropriate test is growth of qualified suppliers, productivity and wages, not export volume alone.

## 24. Political divergence: what it explains and what it does not

Political coalitions differ over industrial policy, tariffs, the United States, relations with China, the role of the state and governance in Venezuela. Leaders can use the bloc as a bargaining stage for domestic audiences; that can slow agreements. Yet the formal June 2026 presidential communiqué reaffirmed Mercosur's role and its institutional commitments despite those differences. The presence of Argentine Foreign Minister Pablo Quirno at that meeting is a factual indication of continued participation, not proof of long-term consensus. [13]

The key distinction is between **declared intent**, **negotiating position**, **legal act** and **observable economic change**. Exit rhetoric becomes materially significant when supported by formal notice, draft legislation, negotiating breakdown, withdrawal of representatives or tariff schedules inconsistent with an enforceable waiver. Mere ideological incompatibility is insufficient. The same discipline applies to claims that an external power is engineering the bloc's dissolution: influence must be demonstrated through documents, incentives or concrete policy changes, not assumed from shared geopolitical preferences.

## 25. Comparison with the EU, USMCA, Pacific Alliance and ASEAN

The European Union developed stronger common institutions, judicial mechanisms and legal integration than Mercosur; an EU comparison clarifies the cost of lacking supranational enforcement but should not assume the same historical or fiscal prerequisites. USMCA is a North American free-trade arrangement rather than a full customs union with a common external tariff. Its members retain national tariffs toward third countries, subject to agreement rules; its design therefore cannot be copied without changing Mercosur's premise.

The Pacific Alliance has emphasized trade openness and links to Asia, but it does not erase members' separate foreign commercial policies or make their economies identical. ASEAN has historically preserved state sovereignty while coordinating economic rules, with variation in implementation. The relevant comparison is not which regional label is superior. It is what degree of common external authority members are willing to finance and enforce, and which gains require supranational rules rather than negotiated coordination.

## 26. Five coherent futures—not five predictions

| Conditional scenario | Trigger and institutional response | Observable tests |
|---|---|---|
| Managed adaptation | Time-limited waivers and rules-of-origin controls agreed by consensus | Published carve-outs; fewer disputes; maintained intra-bloc trade |
| Selective deepening | EU and Asian agreements combined with regional customs and logistics reforms | Preference utilization; lower border times; new regional investment |
| Prolonged paralysis | Member vetoes and unclear bilateral legality persist | Unresolved cases; declining new agreements; regulatory fragmentation |
| Asymmetric reconfiguration | One founder seeks renegotiated participation or a different external-tariff regime | Formal negotiation texts; treaty amendments; origin-rule redesign |
| Formal withdrawal | A member invokes Asunción Articles 21–22 | Formal denunciation, two-year transition arrangements, sectoral treaties |

No probability is attached to these scenarios. Each depends on domestic political decisions, external bargaining, economic conditions and implementation capacities that are not yet observed. Formal withdrawal would be unmistakable in the legal record; a slow weakening of common policy could occur without a single dramatic announcement.

## 27. What would make adaptation economically meaningful?

An agenda measured by outcomes would track time to cross borders, the cost and recognition of certificates of origin, percentage of eligible exporters actually using EU or EFTA preferences, service-export barriers, mutual recognition of professional qualifications, transparent tariff exception use, freight costs and investment in regional suppliers. The India electronic-origin protocol illustrates a narrow instrument whose success can be checked through customs processing rather than diplomatic declarations. [12]

Long-term priorities also include reliable power and transport interconnections, consistent competition rules, smaller-firm access to trade documentation and sectoral transition support. These are not a blanket argument for more or less integration: the appropriate division between regional and domestic authority varies with the problem. Regional coordination is especially useful when one country's border policy imposes costs on its neighbors; domestic policy remains central to education, taxation and firm productivity.

## 28. Evidence thresholds for the next political confrontation

An analytical watchlist should treat an unsigned draft differently from an enacted tariff, a joint summit communiqué differently from compliance, and a reported diplomatic preference differently from a formal treaty decision. The priority documents are the USTR agreement and Argentina's implementation schedules; Mercosur's consolidated exception rules; Brazil's formal compatibility assessment if published; the legal status of EU, EFTA and Singapore instruments; changes in the bloc's accession and suspension records; and any withdrawal notification under Articles 21–22.

An opposing interpretation deserves explicit examination: external bilateral flexibility might help preserve membership by making common rules politically sustainable. Another warns that repeated exceptions could eliminate common negotiating power while retaining costly bureaucracy. Both should be judged against legal transparency, preference use, trade diversion, investment and disputes—not by assigning a country a geopolitical allegiance.

## 29. Synthesis: interdependence without automatic consensus

Mercosur's original political logic—replacing historical rivalry with institutional bargaining—survives alongside an incomplete market. In 2026 its external economic relationships have become more diversified while internal production integration remains uneven. Argentina's US accord, the EU's provisional trade application, China's role in commodity demand, the differentiated tariff exceptions and the gradual implementation of other agreements all reveal the same structural fact: countries share borders and commercial networks but do not share identical economic incentives. [4][5][6][8][10][11]

The future is consequently less a binary contest between union and rupture than a negotiation over the price of coordination. Deepening common infrastructure and customs systems can improve returns from the bloc; carefully bounded flexibility can reduce the incentive for departure; indefinite unilateralism can erode the legal value of the common tariff. A formal exit remains legally available, but it would trigger new commercial and institutional bargaining rather than instantly restoring unrestricted economic sovereignty. The decisive tests are in treaty instruments, tariff schedules, investment decisions and actual cross-border costs.

## Method, limits and source integrity

Data refer to their original observation years and definitions; the 2023 Mercosur totals exclude Bolivia, while the 2025 export shares in the IMF report describe **Brazil alone**. Authorized tariff-exception **caps** are not observed usage; provisional EU trade application is not full partnership ratification; announced talks are not treaties in force. Articles 21–22 establish a legal transition but do not estimate exit costs. No probability of Argentine or other member withdrawal, GDP loss, or post-exit market-access outcome is fabricated. Government statements establish institutional positions; where motives or expected effects are contested, the causal interpretation is conditional. This work complements rather than replaces the September 2026 Marginal Thinking structural Mercosur dossier.
