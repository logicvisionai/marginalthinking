# The world's strategic resource stocks: food, water, health, energy and the material foundations of security

**29 October 2026 · Marginal Thinking · Global System & Power · Strategic resource inventory and structural security assessment**

The modern world holds enormous quantities of grain, hydrocarbons, minerals, water, biological material, medicines and industrial capital. Yet a reserve beneath the ground cannot feed a city during a blockade; a warehouse of grain cannot prevent famine if households have no purchasing power; and a geological deposit of rare earths cannot manufacture a magnet without separation technology, electricity and qualified factories. Security depends on the conversion of physical endowment into usable, accessible and replenishable supply. It is a problem of **time, location, technology, institutions and distribution**, not simply quantity.

In October 2026, the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) projects **about 950 million tonnes of global cereal ending stocks for 2026/27**, with a stocks-to-use ratio of **31.7%**. This is a forecast of crop-year carryovers, not a count of publicly accessible humanitarian grain. The International Energy Agency (IEA) meanwhile reports that observed worldwide oil inventories at end-September 2026 stood **509 million barrels below end-February**, as disruption in the Middle East reduced accessible fuel supply. A world can therefore have a sizeable food inventory and eroding energy buffers simultaneously; one system does not compensate for another. [1][2][3]

The central finding is that **resource security is governed by the weakest usable link**. In energy the limiting factor may be refinery configuration, gas storage or tanker passage. In agriculture it may be nitrogen fertilizer, freshwater or income. In medicine it may be active pharmaceutical ingredients, licensed vaccine production or refrigeration. In advanced manufacturing it may be an obscure by-product refined by few plants rather than a scarce orebody. The results below classify the most consequential resource families, their published stocks or reserves where measurement exists, control points, failure modes and tests for resilience.

## 1. Measurement: six quantities that cannot be added together

A credible strategic inventory begins by rejecting the idea of one comparable worldwide stockpile. **Reserves** are economically extractable geological quantities under stated assumptions. **Resources** are broader geological endowments with different confidence and economic feasibility. **Physical inventories** are material already extracted or manufactured and stored at a location. **In-use capital stocks** are materials embodied in buildings, cables, machinery or medical equipment. **Capacity** measures potential production, refining, transport or delivery over time. **Flows** measure annual or daily output, imports and consumption. They are not interchangeable.

A reserve of 50 billion tonnes of phosphate rock cannot be compared directly with 950 million tonnes of cereal inventory, a 75% gas-storage fill ratio or 3.223 billion vaccine doses delivered. Even when units match, quality, location, seasonality, perishability, legal access and reporting coverage can make an apparent cross-country ranking meaningless. Publicly observed inventory series cover only part of global stockholding; some national oil, grain and medical stocks remain undisclosed. The absence of a number is an **information gap**, not zero physical stock. [1][4][5][6]

| Dimension | Correct object | Illustrative evidence | Principal misuse |
|---|---|---|---|
| Geological reserve | Economically recoverable deposit | USGS phosphate and rare-earth reserve tables, 2026 | Calling it emergency inventory |
| Carryover inventory | Physical stocks remaining after marketing year | FAO cereals, 2026/27 forecast | Assuming it is freely traded |
| Strategic government stock | Material dedicated to emergency release | US SPR, October 2026 | Dividing by total world consumption |
| Commercial stock | Firms' working inventories | IEA observed crude/product stocks | Equating all stock with national policy reserves |
| Stored energy | Working gas in underground storage | GIE European gas, 28 October 2026 | Treating fill percentage as energy quantity |
| Industrial capacity | Output possible under operating conditions | Mineral refining, pharmaceutical plants | Equating projects announced with production |
| Biological stock | Genetic and vaccine repositories | Svalbard and WHO-managed vaccine stockpiles | Assuming direct consumption or universal availability |

For every comparison, the relevant security metric is the **time to replace a lost function**, not only the size of the raw stock. A 10-day deficit of a crucial antibiotic can be more damaging than a year-long deficit of a substitutable metal; the opposite may hold for equipment with a five-year plant construction lead time. [5][7]

## 2. An analytical model of security under interruption

The useful planning identity is **effective cover = accessible, releasable usable stock / essential daily net draw**. This is a scenario calculation rather than a universal statistic. The numerator must subtract unusable minimum operating volumes, quality mismatches, transport constraints and legally inaccessible quantities. The denominator must reflect emergency consumption after demand reduction, substitution and prioritization—not an arbitrary world average.

A second measure is **time to replenish**: exploration and mine construction can require years; crop production is seasonal; refineries and pharmaceutical lines may require requalification; LNG can be diverted faster than a gas pipeline can be built; electricity cannot generally be warehoused at national-system scale. A third measure is **concentration at each stage**, using mine, refining, equipment, shipping and ownership data separately. National sovereignty over deposits is not the same as the control of a refinery or the right to release contracted material. [5][8]

```flow
Geological or biological endowment → extraction or cultivation → conversion and quality assurance → protected storage → transport and distribution → affordable access → essential service
Shock to any necessary stage → effective supply interruption → substitution, rationing or price response → unequal outcomes for households and industry
```

The chain is multidirectional. Diesel shortages raise agricultural machinery and freight costs. Water stress affects electricity and crop yields. Copper shortages delay power-grid investment, which in turn restricts copper processing. Hospital oxygen depends on electricity, equipment, trained operators and distribution. Thus there is no self-contained “energy reserve”, “food reserve” or “medical reserve”: each is supported by others.

## 4. The long history of strategic storage: from granaries to industrial networks

Imperial granaries, irrigation works and maritime grain corridors predate modern economics. Ancient states used storage to provision cities, armies and tax administration; their control of land and grain did not automatically translate into security when transport failed or political allocation excluded households. Early-modern naval empires relied on grain, salt, timber, metal, gunpowder inputs and shipyard capability. The industrial revolution replaced some biological constraints with fossil energy while creating new dependencies on coalfields, ports, machine tools, chemical production and capital.

The twentieth century transformed inventory policy. Total war revealed that iron ore did little without steelworks, oil fields little without refining, and armaments little without propellants, precision components and transport. The 1973 oil crisis helped produce the IEA's emergency-stockholding architecture in 1974. The institution requires member net importers to maintain oil reserves equivalent to at least **90 days of net imports**, with eligible government, agency and industry stocks. The rule does not mean that every IEA country holds exactly 90 days of domestic consumption, nor does it apply equally to net oil exporters. [5]

The COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated the importance of pharmaceutical manufacturing, personal protective equipment, logistics, skilled labor and access agreements. The 2022 energy shock exposed Europe's dependence on particular pipeline routes and gas-storage refill. Middle Eastern disruptions in 2026 again demonstrate why nominal crude availability cannot be converted automatically into diesel and jet fuel. A common historical feature is the transition from a shortage of commodity tonnes to a shortage of **usable services at the right place and time**. [2][5]

## 5. Food: large global grain carryovers, unequal access

The FAO's October 2026 outlook estimates world cereal production for 2026 at roughly **2.979 billion tonnes** and ending stocks for 2026/27 around **950 million tonnes**, with the stock-to-use indicator at **31.7%** compared with **32.0%** for the previous season. Rice ending stocks are forecast at **216.9 million tonnes**. These aggregate figures incorporate national, private, public and processing stocks under FAO statistical conventions; they are not a coordinated international buffer. [1][3]

```chart

unit: %

```

The graph compares two FAO ratios, not two observed warehouse counts. Even a global ratio above 30% cannot guarantee availability of a particular cereal in a particular region. Wheat, maize and rice differ in tradeability, consumption uses, milling constraints and state intervention. Grain held in an exporting country may be strategically restricted, while stocks held in a consuming country may not be accessible to the poorest households. Transport disruptions, currency depreciation and the cost of credit influence the real cost of moving grain across borders.

The Agricultural Market Information System's October 2026 monitor reports stronger wheat carryovers in important exporting countries, broadly unchanged maize stocks, higher rice carry-out forecasts and tightening soybean inventories. That divergence warns against describing “food stocks” as one uniform market. Soybeans also support animal-feed and vegetable-oil networks; maize competes among food, feed, industrial use and fuel production. A disruption to feed can raise future meat and dairy prices even when direct grain consumption is initially protected. [12]

## 6. Nutrition and purchasing power: the stock a household can actually use

The 2026 State of Food Security and Nutrition in the World estimates **645 million people experienced hunger in 2025** and approximately **2.1 billion** experienced moderate or severe food insecurity. These are estimates of human outcomes, not tonnes. The co-occurrence of hunger and large worldwide grain output rejects any theory that aggregate production alone secures nutrition. Income, war, household assets, sanitation, diet diversity, distribution, gender and social protection determine access. [13]

A famine risk model must include market prices relative to wages, nutritional composition and public-service continuity. Calories from cereals are not equivalent to protein, micronutrients and safe drinking water. A country can export agricultural commodities and still contain households unable to afford a healthy diet. Subsidies, targeted transfers, functioning roads and public health measures may improve consumption without adding geological reserves or raising aggregate farm output. Conversely, export restrictions can improve short-run domestic availability while transferring costs to vulnerable importers.

Food-security assessment therefore needs two simultaneous ledgers: a **physical balance** of production, stocks, imports and losses, and an **access balance** of purchasing power, ration entitlements, healthcare and nutrition. Claims of global sufficiency must be tested against both.

These regions are analytical exposure cards, not estimates of country reserve locations. Grain trade is mediated by inland railways, rivers, ports, shipping services, insurance and finance. A shortage at one port can bind a distant flour mill long before global harvests change. UNCTAD estimates that around **80% of international merchandise trade volume travels by sea**, and its 2025 review documents how route changes increased tonne-miles faster than shipped volumes in 2024. The problem is particularly acute for low-value bulk cargo whose freight costs are large relative to its selling price. [14]

## 8. Fertilizers: nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium and sulfur form one agricultural system

Modern crop productivity requires plant nutrients; the strategic supply chain differs for each. Nitrogen fertilizers depend strongly on natural gas feedstock or other energy-intensive ammonia routes. Phosphorus comes from phosphate minerals and industrial processing. Potassium depends on potash deposits and mining. Sulfur and sulfuric acid connect fertilizers with petroleum refining and metallurgical operations. Transport, credit and seasonal farm demand link these inputs to food prices with a delay: a missed planting-window application cannot simply be made up after harvest.

USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026 estimates **73 billion tonnes of global phosphate-rock reserves**, of which **50 billion tonnes are in Morocco**. This is approximately 68.5% of the reported world total on the same reserve basis. The United States Geological Survey also notes that phosphorus has no substitute as a plant nutrient, while known broader phosphate resources exceed the reserve estimate. None of this means the world is about to exhaust phosphate rock; short-term exposure is instead determined by mining, processing, fertilizer prices and geographic concentration. [15]

The chart subtracts Morocco's estimate from the USGS world total; **rest of world is a residual**, not a separately surveyed reserve. Import-dependent farms may be exposed to both fertilizer price spikes and degraded affordability even when the physical reserve horizon is long. In 2026, the IEA also documented sulfur-related cost shocks affecting both mineral and fertilizer value chains. [8]

## 9. Water: a renewable flow constrained by stored availability and quality

Global water cannot be responsibly reduced to one reserve figure. Oceans hold saline water; ice sheets and glaciers store freshwater with geographically restricted access; aquifers contain very different renewable and fossil components; rivers and reservoirs exhibit seasonal flows. A country with a large annual renewable freshwater figure can still have an acute basin-level shortage during a dry season. Groundwater withdrawals that exceed recharge consume capital that may take centuries or millennia to replace.

FAO's 2025 AQUASTAT data snapshot, communicated by UN-Water in January 2026, reports freshwater availability per person falling **7% over the preceding decade** and agriculture accounting for about **72% of global freshwater withdrawals**. Northern Africa, Southern Asia and Western Asia exhibit especially low freshwater resources per capita. These are different measures: per-capita availability reflects supply and population; withdrawals reflect use; neither alone measures safe drinking water or basin stress. [16]

Water security depends on rainfall distribution, reservoirs, aquifer recharge, water quality, institutions, irrigation efficiency, energy for pumping and competing industrial demand. Desalination can expand coastal supply but requires capital, electricity, maintenance and brine management. Reservoir capacity can buffer weeks or seasons; it cannot repeal a long-term precipitation decline. A meaningful inventory is **basin-specific and time-specific**, not a sum of all freshwater on Earth.

## 10. Soil, land, biodiversity and seed stocks

Arable land, soil organic matter, wetlands, pollinators and genetic diversity are productive assets that degrade or recover at different rates. They cannot be assessed merely by hectares. A hectare with depleted nutrients, erosion and unreliable water is not interchangeable with fertile, irrigated soil near roads and markets. Soil loss can be effectively irreversible on investment horizons; some land-use restoration improves function but cannot instantly restore fertility.

The Svalbard Global Seed Vault, created in 2008 as a backup for genebanks, held **more than 1.4 million seed samples in 2026** representing over 6,500 crop species and varieties according to the FAO Plant Treaty. This is an insurance mechanism for genetic diversity, not a commercial seed distribution system or an emergency food warehouse. Deposits are managed within legal and institutional arrangements; only viable seed regeneration, breeding, multiplication and farmer distribution can convert genetic preservation into new harvests. [11]

Food-security planning must track seed availability by planting season, locally adapted varieties, pathogen-resistant germplasm, irrigation and agronomic knowledge. Genetic repositories are a high-value long-duration option: their strategic significance can be enormous even when measured in tiny physical volumes.

## 11. Livestock and fisheries: living inventories, feed dependencies and infection

Cattle, poultry, swine and aquaculture inventories are stocks of living capital with biological lead times. They provide protein, milk, eggs, fertilizer and labor in some systems, but cannot be stored like dry wheat. Disease outbreaks, veterinary-service disruption, feed price spikes and transport bans can rapidly destroy or immobilize production. Fisheries depend on biological populations, access rights, ecosystems, fleets, refrigeration and sustainable catch regulation.

The correct balance tracks animal numbers by species and productive stage, feed stocks, veterinary medicines, slaughter and processing capacity, cold-chain reliability and fishing biomass assessments. Global tonnage of feed grain does not prove the ability to supply eggs or meat when breeding stock, animal health and electricity are unavailable. Antimicrobial misuse can create additional human-health risks, while a blanket assumption that all intensive production systems have identical epidemiological outcomes would be unsupported.

A policy aimed at keeping meat prices stable after a grain shock can fail if breeding herds are culled and regeneration takes years. Short-run livestock liquidation temporarily raises meat supply but reduces future breeding capacity. This is a case where increasing market flow today may weaken the productive stock tomorrow.

## 12. Petroleum: reserves beneath the ground versus barrels ready to release

Proved crude reserves measure economically recoverable petroleum under reporting rules; they are not storage tanks. Global crude extraction may be substantial while accessible inventories of diesel, fuel oil or aviation fuel deteriorate. The IEA's 14 October 2026 Oil Market Report describes September global observed oil stocks declining by **10 million barrels**, despite a **103 million-barrel rise in oil on water**, because on-land stocks fell by **113 million barrels**. Overall observed stocks were **509 million barrels below end-February**. These changes have different boundaries from a geological reserve estimate or a national emergency reserve ledger. [2]

The distinction between crude and products has immediate consequences. Refineries are designed for feedstock qualities and specific product slates; different crude grades cannot always be substituted without losing throughput or raising costs. The IEA reports OECD gasoil/diesel stocks of **565 million barrels in July 2026**, described as the lowest level since November 2023. A government releasing crude from a strategic reserve cannot guarantee a proportionate increase in diesel if refinery outages or shipping restrictions are the binding constraint. [2]

```flow
Crude extraction → crude storage and tankers → refinery with compatible feedstock → diesel, jet fuel and other products → terminals and trucking → farms, hospitals and industry
Crude reserves ≠ marketable crude inventory → refinery throughput ≠ requested product slate → emergency release ≠ delivered fuel
```

## 13. Emergency oil governance and the US Strategic Petroleum Reserve

The IEA obligation of **90 days of net oil imports** is a legal buffer for member net-importing countries; it includes approved public and industry stockholding and is calculated on defined net-import and stock-availability conventions. The 90-day figure cannot be applied to world oil demand or treated as a direct measure of how long a country could run unchanged without new imports. In March 2026, IEA members announced a collective **400-million-barrel emergency release**, illustrating how strategic inventory policy can respond to a geopolitical interruption. [5][17]

The US Department of Energy's site breakdown on **16 October 2026** gives approximately **282.2 million barrels** across four Strategic Petroleum Reserve facilities: Bayou Choctaw 31.4 million; Big Hill 89.1 million; Bryan Mound 138 million; West Hackberry 23.7 million. The DOE reports authorized capacity of **714 million barrels**. These numbers describe US government crude stored in particular salt caverns, not worldwide stocks, commercial inventory or refinery product reserves. [9]

## 14. Natural gas and LNG: storage is regional, seasonal and infrastructure-bound

Natural gas differs from oil because pipelines and liquefied natural gas supply chains have different costs, transit dependencies, regasification requirements and storage characteristics. Proven gas resources under the ground are not equivalent to pipeline capacity or gas available at a consuming terminal. Underground gas inventories provide seasonal smoothing but require injection and withdrawal infrastructure. A stored TWh is a volume of energy, while fill percentage is a share of a site's working capacity; the two must be displayed separately.

Gas Infrastructure Europe (GIE) reported **849.11 TWh** of EU working gas stored at a **74.96% fill rate** in its status on **28 October 2026**. Germany showed **61.73%**, Italy **90.88%**, the Netherlands **65.61%** and Poland **99.26%** on the same display. These values differ due to storage design, consumption, imports and national circumstances. A high fill percentage does not imply equivalent days of winter cover between countries. [6]

## 15. Coal, hydropower, power grids and electricity security

Coal can be stockpiled at mines, ports and power stations but quality, handling, combustion equipment and rail capacity matter. Hydropower stores gravitational potential in reservoirs, not dispatchable electricity independent of rainfall. Wind and solar depend on operating equipment, forecasts, network balancing and complementary flexibility; their primary “resource” is a recurring natural flow rather than an exhaustible stock. Nuclear stations rely on fuel inventories, reactor maintenance, conversion and enrichment capacity. Batteries store electricity for finite durations and are not equivalent to seasonal fuel reserves.

Power systems also require transformers, switchgear, transmission conductors, protection equipment, digital controls, trained workers and spare parts. A country's theoretical generation potential says little about the ability to deliver power when transformers cannot be replaced quickly. Large transformers are engineered products whose procurement, testing and installation can constrain restoration long after fuel supplies normalize.

Electricity security must therefore be mapped as **installed capacity → available generation → transmission → dispatch → reliable delivered energy**. Multiplying gigawatts of installed capacity by hours to claim guaranteed supply ignores outages, fuel limits, hydrology and network congestion. The critical strategic inventory may be equipment or repair crews rather than megawatt-hours.

## 16. Uranium: geological supply, conversion, enrichment and fuel fabrication

Uranium connects geology with a controlled industrial and regulatory cycle. Deposits may support mining over long periods, but reactor operation requires suitable uranium concentrate, chemical conversion, enrichment where applicable, fuel fabrication, licensed components, safeguards and compatible logistics. Kazakhstan, Canada, Australia, Namibia and other producers occupy different positions in the chain; the location of resources, current mine output and enrichment services are distinct sources of dependence.

OECD-NEA and IAEA Red Book reporting quantifies **identified recoverable uranium resources** under specified cost thresholds, rather than a worldwide emergency store of reactor-ready assemblies. Estimates can change as prices, exploration and reporting standards change. Nuclear fuel can sometimes be held for relatively long intervals, but the usable buffer varies by reactor technology, procurement contracts and regulatory approval. The economic consequence of an enrichment interruption need not resemble the consequence of an ore shortage. [19]

Countries evaluating nuclear autonomy must audit the entire fuel cycle and equipment service network. Building more mines without access to conversion or fuel fabrication can fail to improve reliability. Conversely, diverse contractual supply and fuel inventories can reduce short-run risks without domestic uranium deposits.

## 17. Critical minerals: reserve geography is not manufacturing geography

The USGS reports **44 million tonnes** of rare-earth-oxide-equivalent reserves in China, **21 million** in Brazil, **6.3 million** in Australia and **1.9 million** in the United States in its 2026 Mineral Commodity Summaries. These figures are geological reserve estimates under USGS conventions, not stocks of separated neodymium, dysprosium or magnets. Individual rare earths have different extraction, separation and industrial applications. [15][20]

The 2026 IEA Global Critical Minerals Outlook finds that **the average share of the leading refining country for major energy minerals excluding rare earths rose to 72% in 2025**, from 70% in 2023. China remains the leading refining jurisdiction for many materials, while Indonesia has a distinct role in nickel. The agency reports that Chinese rare-earth refining concentration fell from above 90% in 2023 to around **85% in 2025** amid new capacity in the United States and Malaysia. This refines, rather than erases, the finding that processing concentration can be more strategically consequential than geology. [8][21]

The IEA also highlights China accounting for more than 90% of supply in selected refined materials such as gallium and graphite. Concentration must always identify the stage, calendar year, definition and material. A company that produces ore but has to export it for separation has different leverage than a company that owns qualified magnet-making capacity and associated intellectual property.

## 19. Copper, steel and the paradox of renewable infrastructure

Electrification may reduce dependence on imported fossil fuels while increasing dependence on copper, transformers, grid electronics, steel, aluminum and skilled construction labor. A copper mine requires trucks, power, water, reagents and smelting; grid construction requires copper and aluminum whose smelters depend on reliable electricity. This is a feedback loop, not an argument against electrification.

The IEA 2026 outlook estimates that projected copper supply in 2035 could be roughly **25% below requirements** under its stated project and demand assumptions. It is a **scenario gap**, not an observed shortage in 2026 or a claim of imminent total copper depletion. Project maturation, recycling, demand change and substitution could alter the outcome. The IEA finds China reached around **50% of global copper smelting capacity in 2025**, which means that mined-copper diversification does not automatically diversify refined supply. [8]

Because a small missing component can delay a very large project, the appropriate inventory includes transformer's qualified suppliers and production queues, high-voltage cables, electrical steel, switchgear, construction contractors and port access—not just LME-listed metal. For military and civilian infrastructure alike, continuity of service can depend on one manufactured component with a long lead time.

## 20. Strategic by-products and the disproportionate cost of small markets

Gallium, germanium, tellurium, rhenium and other minor materials often originate as by-products or co-products of larger metal-processing systems. Increasing their spot price may not rapidly increase physical supply if production depends on aluminum, zinc or copper throughput and specialized recovery circuits. A small amount of material may be necessary for an extremely expensive downstream product. The monetary value of the raw-material market thus understates interruption damage.

The IEA identifies gallium, magnet rare earths, yttrium, graphite, tungsten, tellurium, cobalt and germanium among highly exposed materials because of concentration, limited substitutes or strategic uses. It describes trade restrictions and scarce high-purity processing equipment. One-sided control should not be inferred from one mine share: parent-metal production, metallurgical recovery, ultra-high-purity refining, qualified suppliers and contracts all matter. [8]

Security policy can be more effective when it stockpiles **certified usable materials** and establishes alternative refining capability, rather than simply purchasing large volumes of unprocessed ore. Such measures incur storage, obsolescence and quality-control costs and must be designed for specific industrial demand, not arbitrary tonnes.

## 21. Recycling, urban mines and substitution

Buildings, vehicles, transmission cables and machinery embody a vast in-use stock of copper, iron, aluminum and other metals. Recoverable scrap can reduce future mine dependence, but its timing is determined by product life cycles, collection systems and metallurgy. Metal currently installed in a functioning grid is unavailable to a recycling facility without destroying another service. “Urban mine” is therefore a long-run resource, not a free crisis stock.

The IEA's 2026 analysis envisages the secondary-supply contribution for key energy minerals rising from roughly 10% today toward 20% by 2040 under stated policies. This is a modelled outlook, not a measured 2040 outcome. Recycling of complex batteries and rare-earth magnets requires collection infrastructure, process know-how, energy and purity assurance. In some sectors technical substitution can reduce exposure; in others it shifts demand to another critical mineral. [8]

A resilient supply policy combines recycling with demand efficiency, design for disassembly and industrial redundancy. The indicators must distinguish gross recycling capacity, actual recovered output and materials delivered to approved downstream customers.

## 22. Pharmaceuticals: no reliable one-number global inventory

Medicine security differs sharply from petroleum and cereal security. There is no credible public global sum of all usable antibiotics, insulin, anesthetics, diagnostic reagents, blood products and medical devices. Products have distinct shelf lives, temperatures, sterile requirements, patents, manufacturing licenses and dosage compatibility. A warehouse of inappropriate formulations cannot necessarily substitute for a missing critical product.

The relevant chain comprises **active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs), excipients, sterile production, fill-finish, quality-release laboratories, packaging, procurement, distribution and point-of-care delivery**. Some products require uninterrupted cold chains or controlled oxygen systems. National agencies and hospital procurement systems see different slices of the inventory. A major factory interruption can have international consequences well before publicly visible aggregated inventories indicate scarcity.

A credible security investigation would select an essential-medicines list, track molecules and presentations separately, map registered production sites and alternative suppliers, record regulatory approval lead times, then determine actual hospital and national reserve cover. Pretending that purchase expenditure or drug sales measure doses available for emergencies would mislead.

## 23. Vaccines: manufacturing throughput, emergency stockpiles and equitable access

UNICEF's 2025 Supply Annual Report records **3.223 billion vaccine doses delivered to 103 countries**, not held in a worldwide reserve at year-end. Its program-level deliveries include **1.582 billion polio vaccine doses**, **468 million measles-containing doses**, **60.65 million cholera vaccine doses** and **72.55 million yellow-fever doses**. These are 2025 UNICEF delivery quantities, not total world manufacture or current unexpired inventory. Program categories have different schedules and formulations and are not substitutes. [10][22]

WHO's International Coordinating Group (ICG) describes emergency stockpile arrangements including a target of **six million yellow-fever doses** and a **five-million-dose cholera stockpile target**, with historical documentation of shipment and replenishment arrangements. Such targets do not assert that the stocks stood at their target on 29 October 2026. They also cannot be combined with UNICEF's annual delivered doses as though all doses were held simultaneously. WHO reports a rise in oral cholera vaccine production from about **30 million doses in 2022** to roughly **80 million in 2025**, while outbreaks still pressure supply. [7][23]

Medical access ultimately depends on outbreak surveillance, allocation decisions, trained vaccinators, syringes, cold-chain equipment, local authorization, trusted communication and sanitation. A stockpile that cannot be allocated and deployed quickly offers less protection than its nominal size suggests.

## 24. Oxygen, blood, diagnostics and hospital power

Medical oxygen is both a clinical product and an industrial gas service. Hospital access may come from cryogenic air-separation facilities, pressure-swing adsorption generators, cylinder systems and piped distribution. Each depends on electricity, maintenance, certified purity, pressure controls and personnel. A city may have industrial oxygen production while its health facilities face clinical oxygen shortages because delivery, purity assurance or financing breaks down.

Blood and blood products have strict processing, compatibility, storage and expiry conditions; a “national number of units” is unusable without group, component and location. Diagnostic reagents, laboratory equipment and consumables may constrain disease detection before hospital beds fill. In emergencies, maintaining electricity, water, cold storage and local transport can be as important as expanding medication purchases.

These systems resist one global headline because stockholding is decentralized and products are not fungible. The security indicator should report **facility coverage by indispensable function**, with audited minimum operating quantities, geographic distribution and replenishment performance.

## 25. Emergency humanitarian stocks and their scale limits

The World Food Programme-managed United Nations Humanitarian Response Depot (UNHRD) network operates **five hubs** in Italy, Ghana, Malaysia, Panama and the United Arab Emirates. Its 2025 summary records **580 consignments**, assistance reaching **93 countries and territories**, and **8,040 metric tonnes dispatched**. The hubs pre-position medical kits, shelter supplies, ready-to-use foods and logistics equipment for many partners. These are logistics and dispatch statistics, not the size of a permanent global food reserve. [24]

The strategic benefit of pre-positioning is reduced response time and the ability to combine procurement, warehouses, aircraft, ports and last-mile partners. It is costly to hold large perishable inventories, and unexpected crises may occur away from known staging areas. Global readiness therefore requires regional redundancy, surge contracts, customs arrangements and secure access rather than one giant warehouse.

International solidarity is operationally constrained by who can fund relief, which governments permit movement, how humanitarian actors are protected and whether transport corridors remain open. Geopolitical control over access can be a stronger immediate variable than global commodity availability.

## 26. Transport, ports, shipping and the financial infrastructure of inventories

The location of a barrel, sack, vaccine vial or transformer matters as much as its ownership. Commodity chains operate through maritime chokepoints, ports, inland railway terminals, cold-storage sites, pipelines, container networks and roads. A warehouse connected to a blocked port may possess technically intact inventory with zero immediately accessible supply for overseas customers.

UNCTAD reports that approximately 80% of merchandise trade by volume moves by sea. In 2024 rerouting caused ton-miles to grow **5.9%**, compared with **2.2%** growth in maritime trade volume. A longer voyage effectively ties up ships, crews, fuel and working capital. Insurance, letter-of-credit terms, sanctions compliance and port access can additionally delay cargo even when the physical material exists. [14]

```flow
Conflict, drought or port disruption → rerouting and higher freight → delayed fertilizer, energy and medical deliveries → lower production or higher household prices → nutrition, health and fiscal consequences
Warehouse stock without transport rights → inaccessible supply → rationing and substitution → social and industrial losses
```

A proper world stock atlas must therefore attach **transport options and lead times** to physical assets. Mapping only mines and grain silos produces an incomplete picture of who can maintain vital services during stress.

The location of a reserve, the jurisdiction of a controlling company and the destination of output must be recorded as separate entities.

## 29. Strategic actors: who controls which decision?

Four distinct powers must be mapped: **resource title** (who owns the mineral or land); **operational control** (who runs the mine, refinery, port or warehouse); **allocation power** (who can prioritize, release, export or withhold product); and **technical substitution power** (who can qualify a new supplier or technology). They may be held by different states and firms, and they may change during war or emergency legislation.

Saudi oil fields operated through concession arrangements, Chile's state-owned copper company, private agricultural traders, public grain authorities, pharmaceutical manufacturers and WHO-supported vaccine allocation mechanisms are not interchangeable governance structures. Strategic dependence is not automatically evidence of hostile control. An adversary's stated objective, a company's profit motive and a state's lawful export decision need different attribution.

The Marginal Thinking Resource Control Atlas already uses resource class, controller nature, control mode, asset geography and limitations. A quantitative extension must preserve these fields, add precise product/time definitions and avoid implying that owning company shares grants sovereignty over the underlying subsoil or a maritime strait.

## 30. Scenario I: prolonged disruption of a major energy corridor

Assume an energy transport corridor remains disrupted for a quarter, without assuming that all regional production ceases. Lost throughput, vessel diversion, insurance premiums, specialized refinery feedstock and the availability of alternative routes determine the effective shortfall. Government crude stocks can reduce some price pressure, but diesel and aviation-fuel availability may remain constrained by refinery output. Agricultural transport, fertilizer prices and hospital operating budgets then face indirect pressure.

**Observable tests:** IEA product inventories and refinery runs; tanker flows and freight; strategic releases; diesel-to-crude price spreads; fertilizer prices; inflation and public-service rationing. **Counterfactual:** if alternative product exports and refining recovery restore delivered diesel despite expensive crude, the strongest cross-sector damage forecast weakens. The October 2026 IEA data are evidence of current vulnerability; this scenario is a conditional extension, not a forecast of a specific attack. [2]

## 31. Scenario II: consecutive adverse harvests and fertilizer interruption

Suppose two major exporting regions experience poor crop yields while nitrogen and phosphate fertilizer costs rise and several importing countries face currency weakness. Global carryovers provide an initial buffer, but distribution may become politically restricted. Lower-income net food buyers spend more on staples, with dietary diversity worsening even before calorie availability falls. Governments may choose transfers, tariff reductions, emergency imports or export restrictions, each with distributional and fiscal effects.

**Observable tests:** crop-condition data, AMIS commodity balances by grain, FAO ending-stock updates, fertilizer deliveries before planting, wages relative to food baskets, child nutrition and sovereign import financing. **Counterfactual:** if high opening stocks in accessible exporting jurisdictions, alternative crops and targeted income support stabilize food consumption, a poor harvest need not produce famine. Rainfall is a hazard; famine depends on exposure, institutions and entitlement. [1][12][13]

## 32. Scenario III: mineral processing export controls

Consider restrictions on highly purified graphite, gallium or magnet materials that disrupt downstream factories even though ore remains available in other countries. Firms draw inventory, allocate supplies to highest-value production, seek technical substitutes and qualify new sources. The costs depend on component concentration, qualification time, patent rights, environmental permitting and the availability of high-purity equipment.

**Observable tests:** export licensing, actual customs flows, refined-material inventories, factory utilization, alternate-source qualification and investment in processing. **Counterfactual:** documented stock buffers, technology substitution or competitive new capacity may prevent extended production loss. The IEA's 2026 review describes genuine export restrictions and concentration; it does not justify asserting that every supply curtailment causes identical worldwide damage. [8]

## 33. Scenario IV: pandemic, medicine shortages and health-system stress

A novel or renewed infectious-disease wave could simultaneously increase demand for diagnostics, oxygen, vaccines and protective equipment while reducing manufacturing labor and transport availability. Stockpiles can reduce initial scarcity but require compatible products, surveillance-based allocation and regulatory quality control. A country may have vaccine doses at a central warehouse but insufficient cold-chain capability in affected districts.

**Observable tests:** case surveillance, hospital oxygen interruption, local stockout rates, licensed manufacturing throughput, dose wastage, distribution time and excess mortality. **Counterfactual:** early disease surveillance, functioning routine care, distributed manufacturing and public trust can prevent a severe supply shock. Scenario severity must not be equated with the mere appearance of a pathogen. [7][10][23][24]

## 34. Scenario V: compound electricity-water-food shock

A severe drought lowers reservoir inflows and crop yields while raising electricity demand for pumping and cooling. Water-intensive mining or fertilizer production competes with urban needs. Energy rationing then reduces irrigation, cold storage and industrial output. Existing stockpiles become less useful if warehouses, roads, treatment plants and hospitals lose power.

**Observable tests:** basin inflows, groundwater levels, hydro generation, grid reserve margin, agricultural water allocation, food losses and hospital outages. **Counterfactual:** diversified generation, interconnections, water-demand management and regionally distributed storage can interrupt the cascade. The scenario illustrates why a mineral-rich or agriculturally productive country can still be structurally insecure when its enabling infrastructure is fragile. [16]

## 35. Brazil: major resource endowment and the processing challenge

Brazil is a major agricultural producer and exporter, has significant water systems and oil production, and possesses large estimated rare-earth reserves. Yet natural abundance is not synonymous with comprehensive strategic autonomy. The USGS estimates **21 million tonnes of rare-earth-oxide-equivalent reserves**, while a separate industrial assessment must establish how much material can actually be mined, separated, converted into metals and fabricated into components. The country also depends on imported or globally priced fertilizer inputs and participates in maritime, financing and equipment chains that can suffer disruption. [15][20]

The priority is not autarky in every commodity. It is to identify vital functions for which feasible substitutes are absent over emergency time horizons: industrial transformers and power electronics; selected pharmaceutical products and APIs; nitrogen, phosphate and potash access during planting; refinery product balances; defensible water infrastructure; and transport routes to food-consuming regions. A meaningful public policy must compare the costs of storing goods, building domestic processing, diversifying contracts and expanding recycling.

Brazil's negotiating strength comes from pairing geological, agricultural and energy assets with credible regulation, industrial skill, infrastructure and long-term delivery performance. Sovereignty over a mineral deposit is a legal foundation; sustained strategic advantage requires productive and institutional capability.

## 36. Where global inventory information is genuinely missing

There is **no single public verified global stock figure** for insulin, antibiotics, essential APIs, high-purity gallium, specialized transformers, all commercial fertilizer inventories, all national grain reserves or every underground hydrocarbon inventory on a common reporting date. Private stocks, military holdings, confidential procurement and rapidly changing commercial warehouses complicate measurement. Some public databases quantify capacities rather than stock. Filling such gaps with inferred global totals would be false precision.

The appropriate response is an **evidence-grade register**:

| Evidence grade | Acceptable record | Reporting treatment |
|---|---|---|
| A — observed physical stock | Verified inventory with date, owner/coverage and unit | Publish exact scope and update cycle |
| B — official estimated stock | FAO agricultural forecast, survey or statistical estimate | Publish method, vintage and uncertainty |
| C — recoverable reserve | USGS/NEA resource or reserve under stated assumptions | Never label as warehoused emergency supply |
| D — production capability | Operating, audited throughput or supplier capacity | Publish as output/time, not inventory |
| E — scenario or announced capacity | Project, stress case, investment announcement | Exclude from observed stock totals |
| Unknown | Opaque state or firm stockholding | Record unknown; do not assume zero |

At country level, disclosures should include physical stock, days of essential consumption cover, seasonal accessibility, release authority, quality and expiry, reserve location and logistics, import share, refining dependence and tested response arrangements. Cross-national comparison must use matching definitions.

## 37. Strategic investment decisions: stockpiles versus productive resilience

A large warehouse protects against temporary flow interruptions but can immobilize capital, require rotation and become obsolete. Domestic refining offers longer-term options but may be expensive, require skilled labor and expose the host to environmental risks. Diversified international contracts reduce concentration but do not guarantee access during simultaneous geopolitical shocks. Recycling is valuable for mature material streams but cannot immediately replace components still in use.

A robust security portfolio combines four instruments: **targeted rotating inventory for short disruptions; diversified suppliers and corridors; domestic or allied capability for difficult-to-substitute processes; and demand-side adaptation**. Emergency reserves work best where the product is standardized, storable and periodically required. Industrial redundancy is preferable where a process or component cannot be warehoused in economically useful quantities. Social protection is essential when the greatest risk is affordability rather than global physical scarcity.

The social distribution of costs matters. Paying for strategic assets through energy tariffs, fiscal subsidies or high food prices can protect one sector while weakening households. Every intervention needs a counterfactual: compared with which feasible alternative, at what cost, under which probability and severity of interruption?

## 38. A decision framework for governments and industrial operators

```mindmap
Global resource security
- Physical availability
  - Geological reserves and biological assets
  - Commercial inventories and emergency stocks
  - Usable and verified quality
- Transformation
  - Refining and chemical processing
  - Manufacturing and regulatory approval
  - Electricity, water and skilled labor
- Access
  - Export rules and allocation authority
  - Ports, pipelines and last-mile delivery
  - Household affordability and public provision
- Time
  - Days of emergency cover
  - Seasonal production and refill
  - Years to build substitute facilities
- Resilience
  - Supplier and geographic diversity
  - Recycling, substitution and demand restraint
  - Emergency coordination and transparent monitoring
```

An effective risk assessment asks: *Which essential service fails first, which stock is physically available to prevent that failure, who may release it, where must it travel, what substitutes are qualified, and how long until normal supply is restored?* A high inventory-to-use ratio is reassuring only if the product, location, time horizon and access rights match the crisis.

For an auditable global resource observatory, the minimum unit of evidence is **commodity × stage × jurisdiction × owner/operator × date × unit × stock/flow/reserve definition × release conditions × source URL**. Updating a mineral reserve estimate once per year and an oil inventory every week may both be appropriate; pretending these data have one common timestamp is not.

## 39. Contradictory interpretations and falsification

One school emphasizes physical scarcity: finite deposits, growing population and climate damage eventually raise costs. Another emphasizes technological adaptation: exploration, substitution, efficiency, recycling and new process routes can expand effective supply. Neither view is sufficient without measurement. Recoverable reserves often rise with exploration and prices, but the discovery of more ore does not guarantee that processing or logistics become available quickly. Conversely, concentrated refining does not guarantee permanent monopoly when investment and technical alternatives advance.

The 2026 evidence favors a more precise diagnosis: **many of the most acute security risks are discontinuities between stages of supply**, while some local water, land and ecological stresses are intrinsically physical. This view would weaken if audited inventories and diversified processing capacity demonstrated that essential goods can be delivered through severe multi-region disruption without major welfare loss. It would strengthen if repeated shocks caused long stockouts despite abundant reported reserves and aggregate output.

Claims must remain calibrated: the IEA's 2035 copper supply gap is a scenario; FAO 2026/27 cereal stocks are forecasts; DOE and GIE readings are dated observations; USGS mineral reserves are estimates; WHO targets are institutional stockpile design, not an assertion of current fill. These are distinct evidence categories and should never be collapsed into one dramatic “world running out” narrative.

## 40. Conclusion: the strategic stock is usable civilization

The decisive global asset is not a mountain of ore, a cavern of crude or a national warehouse in isolation. It is the ability to keep nutrition, sanitation, electricity, medicine, production and communication functioning when several inputs fail simultaneously. A reserve acquires security value only through the institutions and technologies that can turn it into a reliable service. Industrial capability acquires security value only when it can obtain inputs, operate under stress and deliver to the people who need it.

The highest-priority reforms differ by commodity. Food requires seasonal inventories **and** access protection. Water requires basin governance and quality assurance. Fuels require product-specific emergency cover and usable refining. Minerals require processing diversity and certified components. Health requires licensed manufacturing, refrigerated delivery and functioning primary care. Power systems require durable equipment, repair capability and diverse generation. The aggregate planet has substantial resources; the security of its populations depends on the resilience and fairness of the networks that connect them.
