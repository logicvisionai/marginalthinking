# Slavery in Brazil and the world: the political economy of coercion, abolition and enduring inequality

**Cutoff: 24 October 2026. Historical embarkations, national censuses and contemporary model-based estimates are distinct.**

## Forms of slavery across societies and the problem of false equivalence

Slavery long predates the Atlantic system and appeared in different legal and labor forms in Africa, Asia, Europe, the Americas and the Middle East. War captives, hereditary enslavement, debt bondage and state-imposed labor were not interchangeable. Their legal scope, capacity for manumission, social roles, trade exposure and duration differed. A comparative economic history must distinguish the institution that treats human beings as property from other coercive obligations without minimizing violence in either. The UNESCO Routes of Enslaved Peoples program emphasizes transregional histories and the agency of enslaved communities. [7]
A global chronology cannot be reduced to a single empire or ethnic group. Merchant networks and states connected local coercion to distant demand; some societies supplied captives while others financed shipping, agriculture or consumption. Responsibility must be examined in specific contracts, administrations and periods. Explaining an institution's revenue does not justify it: profits generated through coercion and denied autonomy are not measures of welfare. Counterfactual analysis includes the productive alternatives available to free people, human costs of displacement and the lasting allocation of land and political rights.

## The Atlantic system: shipping, mortality and the commodity circuits

The SlaveVoyages database estimates approximately **12.52 million people embarked** on transatlantic slaving voyages between 1501 and 1866, by reconstructed voyage and national carrier. Embarkation is not arrival: deaths, ship losses and record coverage prevent treating the 12.52 million figure as the number who landed alive. The carrier class labelled **Portugal/Brazil** accounts for roughly **5.85 million embarkations**, not an exact count of arrivals on Brazilian territory. Combining these definitions without qualification creates misleading claims about national totals. [1]
The economic mechanism joined coercive capture and sale in Africa, maritime transport and credit, plantation investment, export commodities and consumption in Europe and the Americas. Ships required capital, insurance, food, water and enforcement; plantations demanded land, machinery and constant labor discipline. European merchants, American owners and participating African actors occupied different positions and captured different incomes. Aggregate ocean freight profits do not reveal captives' suffering or the social destruction of their home communities. Price series require correcting for region, age, skill and legal status, without reducing human beings to accounting categories as the totality of their historical experience.

## Brazil: sugar, gold, coffee and geographically changing labor demand

Enslaved labor under Portuguese colonization and imperial rule supplied sugar plantations, urban services, mining activities and later coffee production. The composition changed over centuries as settlement, export prices, labor availability and land access shifted. The concentration of enslaved people in specific provinces was related to agricultural profitability and internal trade, but an export boom should not be described as proof that forced labor was economically efficient in a social-welfare sense. Owners externalized violence, deprivation, suppression of wages and lost freedom onto enslaved workers. [4][7]
The domestic credit system depended partly on slaveholding estates and enslaved people were treated as pledged property under period law. Loans used to expand plantations could reproduce coercion while building export infrastructure. Urban businesses and rural commerce also relied on free and freed workers. Consequently, a binary portrait of an economy containing only slaves and owners erases complex labor markets and the roles of indigenous groups, migrants and formerly enslaved people. A good study separates legal compulsion, bargaining constraints and actual work rather than equating all poor labor with legal slavery.

## The 1872 census: what the 1.51 million figure shows

The first nationwide Brazilian census recorded a later-adjusted population of **10,112,061** people, including **1,510,806 classified as enslaved**, approximately 15% at that snapshot. The dataset is exceptionally important because the legal population categories directly measured bondage in the empire. But one cross-section in 1872 does not count everyone enslaved across three centuries, nor does it capture all informal coercive relations or mortality from earlier trafficking. The IBGE notes the census's difficult implementation, archival publication and subsequent revisions. [4][5][6]
Population denominators also matter. The slave share of a province, the absolute number and the fraction of workers in agriculture answer different questions. One should not infer freedom of the remaining 85% in modern equality terms: freed persons, descendants, indigenous groups and poor citizens faced discrimination, inequality and limited representation. Nor does a changing share necessarily mean changing conditions for those still enslaved. Economic evaluation requires wages, property access, schooling, mortality, household formation and regional production after emancipation.

## Resistance, abolitionism and legal chronology

Enslaved people resisted through everyday negotiation, flight, community formation, revolt and litigation where possible. Quilombos, Black associations, free and freed activists, journalists, lawyers and abolitionist movements challenged coercion; abolition was not a gift handed down by a monarch acting alone. Brazil's prohibition of the transatlantic trade in 1850, the 1871 Free Womb Law, the 1885 Sexagenarian Law and the **1888 Lei Áurea** altered different legal relations. Their limitations, incomplete protection and enforcement meant that reform did not instantly dissolve economic dependence. [7][13]
Comparisons with British parliamentary suppression of the trade from 1807 and US emancipation in the Civil War show why stopping trafficking differs from abolishing ownership of people. The US Emancipation Proclamation applied under wartime conditions, while the Thirteenth Amendment provided constitutional abolition with an exception associated with punishment after conviction. Neither a single statute nor military victory automatically supplied land, schools, income and equal civil protection to the emancipated. National chronologies require their own jurisdictions and cannot be blended into one 'world abolition date'. [8][9][10]

## Land, property, race and the long-term economic inheritance

Abolition changed legal ownership relations but did not automatically redistribute land and financial wealth previously concentrated among large slaveholders. Brazil's land and credit structures, regional migration, continuing discrimination and access to education conditioned the opportunities of formerly enslaved households and descendants. The resulting inequality should not be attributed to a single law or quantified by an invented share of present-day GDP. Historical persistence requires linking property records, local schools, wages, credit access and institutions across generations. [4][11]
Economic historians distinguish the direct cost of exploitation from its institutional legacy: unequal property, limited bargaining and stereotypes can endure after formal law changes. In some regions, labor contracts and migration provided alternatives; in others, debt, political dependence and land scarcity restricted mobility. The correct analysis compares observable communities and policies while acknowledging selective archives. The beneficiaries of export capital and the people subjected to extraction were not identical, and their descendants entered the modern economy from very different asset positions.

## A world comparison without flattening regional histories

In the Americas, plantation and mining production integrated slavery with Atlantic finance and industrial consumption. In other regions, slavery could be connected to households, armies, courts, ports and commerce in ways not captured by the Atlantic voyage database. Modern country borders did not necessarily exist in the same form. Race, kinship and law assigned status differently among societies; this variation does not make forced labor benign. A careful global study should not extrapolate Brazil's plantation mechanisms to every medieval or African institution. [1][7]
The supply of enslaved people often involved violence and political coercion well beyond the purchase point. However, assigning a single ethnic or national group sole responsibility erases competing state and merchant roles, chronology and demand-side incentives. The correct geographic frame follows the network: places of capture, commercial exchange, maritime and overland routes, workplaces, consuming markets and eventual settlement. Without comparable records, countries should not be ranked on a fabricated composite measure of 'slave economy dependence'.

## Modern slavery: a contemporary statistical category, not direct legal continuity

The **ILO, IOM and Walk Free** estimated that **49.6 million people** were in conditions categorized as modern slavery in **2021**, including **27.6 million in forced labor** and **22 million in forced marriage**. The widely reported rounded total is 50 million. These estimates, released in 2022, combine household surveys and victim case data, not a global census of legal slaveholders. They describe coercion and inability to refuse or leave, not the continued legality of nineteenth-century chattel ownership. [2][3]
Modern forms include restrictions on movement, debt bondage, confiscation of identity documents, threats, commercial sexual exploitation and certain state-imposed practices. Informal supply chains can transmit coercion to consumers who do not see workplaces directly. Enforcement depends on inspections, accessible justice, migrant rights, procurement rules and protections that do not punish victims. The numbers have sampling and definition limits, especially in inaccessible states and clandestine industries; one must not turn a global prevalence estimate into a precise present-day count for an unobserved municipality.

## Capital accounting, reparative policies and what can be measured

A slavery-related firm might report profits, debt, property values and export revenue, but no balance-sheet surplus can incorporate the full cost of denied freedom, physical harm, family separation and stolen earnings. Even narrowly economic valuation is difficult: historical prices are not equivalent to present-day welfare, and the relevant counterfactual wages, productivity and rights are not observed. Material damages, restitution claims, heritage research and policies for education, land and antidiscrimination involve different legal and policy frameworks. [1][4]
Evaluations should identify beneficiaries, mechanisms and outcomes instead of offering precise, unsubstantiated 'world debt' figures. Records of individual transactions can support specific restitution claims; aggregate inequality can motivate public policy, but its entire causal decomposition across centuries requires careful research. A program may improve schooling while failing to address property access; a tax transfer may alleviate consumption without reducing political discrimination. Indicators should include income, wealth, morbidity, incarceration, education, land titles and civic representation, separately reported rather than collapsed into a single moral score.

## Contrary evidence, archival limits and inference tests

Sources cannot reduce all historical labor dependence to a single legal type or assign responsibility to an ethnic population by ancestry. Atlantic embarkations do not measure inland bondage, and modern forced-labor prevalence is not a legal stock of human property. Evidence requires comparable statistics, court records, workers' accounts, property records and causal policy evaluations.
