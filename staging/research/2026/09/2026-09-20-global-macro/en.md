# Global Macro — September 20, 2026

The weekend opens with a market that is calm in price but still restrictive in financing. Friday's U.S. close showed the S&P 500 up 0.2% and Nasdaq up 0.4%, even as the Treasury curve remained unusually expensive: the official September 18 close put the 2-year at 4.67%, the 10-year at 4.94% and the 30-year at 5.29%. Oil eased to $103.87 for Brent and $100.30 for WTI, reducing the immediate inflation impulse without removing the physical and security risks around Gulf supply. The result is not a broad risk-on regime; it is a selective willingness to hold earnings-sensitive assets while sovereign yields, fund flows and geopolitical hedging continue to signal caution.

The new weekend delta comes from China. Benchmark loan prime rates were left unchanged for a sixteenth month, with the one-year LPR at 3.00% and the five-year at 3.50%, despite weak credit demand. At the same time, U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Chinese Vice Premier He Lifeng are due to meet on Sunday to discuss trade, AI security, rare earths and broader economic issues ahead of the Trump-Xi meeting. That combination matters because the immediate China question is no longer simply whether Beijing cuts rates: weak domestic demand, local-government balance sheets, external technology restrictions and trade negotiations are interacting with the global tightening cycle.

## What changed since the previous edition

- China's September LPR fixing was unchanged at **3.00% for one year and 3.50% for five years**. Monetary easing remains constrained by bank margins and a weak credit-demand channel rather than by a lack of low nominal rates alone.
- The U.S.-China agenda moved closer to markets: Sunday's Bessent-He meeting is expected to cover **AI, rare earths, trade and economic issues**, making technology access and critical inputs immediate policy catalysts rather than background strategic themes.
- Friday's official U.S. Treasury close was **4.67% for 2Y, 4.94% for 10Y and 5.29% for 30Y**. The curve remains upward sloping from 2Y to 30Y, so lower oil has not removed the long-duration financing constraint.

## Lower oil has reduced the shock, but the long end is not validating normalization

The most important cross-asset divergence is now between energy and sovereign duration. Crude has retreated from the week's stress highs, yet the 10-year Treasury is still near 5% and the 30-year above 5.2%. If oil remains lower, headline inflation pressure should ease at the margin. But long yields also reflect expected policy, real rates, Treasury supply and term compensation, so they do not need to fall one-for-one with crude.

This matters for equities. Nasdaq strength beside a 10-year yield near 5% implies that investors still assign enough earnings growth to large technology companies to offset a higher discount rate. The Russell 2000's Friday decline points in the other direction: smaller firms generally have less diversified financing access and are more exposed to domestic borrowing costs. The divergence is therefore economically coherent rather than proof that rates no longer matter.

## China's unchanged rates expose a demand problem that cheaper credit alone may not solve

Keeping the LPR unchanged was expected, but the decision reinforces a broader constraint. China's loan growth has slowed as property and local-government borrowing weakened, while new industries have not fully replaced that demand. A PBOC adviser argued this weekend that policy needs to repair local-government, financial-sector and corporate balance sheets and raise household income, warning that AI-led export strength can coexist with weak domestic consumption.

The market implication is that additional monetary easing would not automatically produce a proportional increase in investment or consumption. The transmission depends on whether households, developers, local governments and private firms are willing and able to borrow. That makes fiscal repair, income policy and the treatment of local-government liabilities as important as the next policy-rate move.

## U.S.-China talks put AI and rare earths into the near-term macro calendar

The Bessent-He meeting is a political catalyst with direct economic channels. AI security affects access to advanced computing and models; rare-earth policy affects manufacturing inputs; tariffs affect landed costs and trade volumes. A negotiated reduction in specific barriers would matter through identifiable prices and supply chains. Failure to stabilize the relationship would keep a risk premium around technology access, critical inputs and cross-border investment.

The correct market test is not whether the meeting produces conciliatory language. It is whether there is a verifiable change in tariffs, export controls, licensing, critical-mineral access or investment rules. Until then, the event is a catalyst, not evidence of a structural reset.

```flow
U.S.-China policy talks → tariffs / technology controls / rare-earth access → input costs and supply-chain choices → corporate investment and trade volumes → earnings, FX and industrial-commodity demand

Lower Gulf oil-risk premium → lower marginal fuel and freight pressure → less near-term inflation stress → some relief for consumers and margins

High sovereign yields → expensive refinancing and higher discount rates → pressure on leveraged borrowers and long-duration valuations → selective rather than broad risk-taking
```

## Europe and Japan show different versions of the same inflation constraint

Europe ended Friday weaker, with the STOXX 600 down 1.1%, as autos and telecoms led losses. Volkswagen's reduced outlook showed why the region cannot be read only through rates: China exposure, restructuring costs and company-specific earnings revisions are interacting with expensive energy and financing. Lower oil helps, but it does not repair weak demand or company balance sheets by itself.

Japan tightened to 1.25% on Friday, yet the yen weakened after two BOJ members dissented. The currency reaction indicates uncertainty about the persistence and speed of future tightening. For global investors, the relevant variable is the expected rate differential over time, not the delivered hike alone.

## Fund flows still contradict the calm in headline indices

Global equity funds recorded their largest weekly outflow in nine months in the week through September 16. U.S. equity funds posted a fourth consecutive weekly outflow. At the same time, Asian equity funds attracted capital, and Chinese investors have been increasing allocations to foreign assets through newly expanded authorized channels.

These flows do not identify the marginal buyer of every index constituent, but they prevent a simple interpretation of Friday's U.S. equity gains as generalized risk appetite. Capital is differentiating by region, liquidity and earnings visibility.

## Gold and crypto are giving different readings of protection and liquidity

Gold's resilience near $4,390/oz despite tighter central banks and a firm dollar suggests that geopolitical and inflation protection demand remains material. The useful test next week is whether gold can stay firm if U.S. real yields rise further. If it can, protection demand is overcoming a conventional headwind; if not, the rates channel is reasserting itself.

U.S. spot Bitcoin ETFs ended the week with only about **$6 million of net inflows**, despite a strong **$433 million Friday inflow**, while Ether ETFs lost roughly **$140 million** over the week. The late rebound therefore does not establish a broad liquidity impulse across digital assets. It shows that Bitcoin-specific demand recovered sharply at the end of a volatile week.

## Brazil: high carry remains, but fiscal and external-rate signals matter more at the margin

Brazil enters the new week with the Selic at **13.75%** after the Copom's fifth consecutive 25-basis-point cut. The nominal cushion over U.S. rates remains large, but it narrowed because the Fed raised its target range to **3.75%–4.00%** while Brazil cut. That makes the real more sensitive to fiscal expectations, global yields and commodity prices than a static comparison of policy rates would suggest.

The Copom itself continues to flag the effect of fiscal policy on monetary policy and financial assets. With activity decelerating and inflation expectations still above target, the local curve can remain restrictive even if the policy rate declines. Brazil therefore belongs in the same global theme as the United States: the policy rate and the long end of the curve can move for different reasons.

## Dislocations and second-order effects

**Large U.S. technology versus smaller companies.** The combination of Nasdaq resilience and Russell 2000 weakness suggests that high yields are being absorbed unevenly. The asymmetry would weaken if earnings revisions broadened and credit conditions eased; it would strengthen if real yields rose while small-company refinancing deteriorated.

**China's low rates versus weak borrowing demand.** The potential dislocation is between the apparent availability of cheaper credit and the willingness of balance sheets to use it. A material recovery in household borrowing, property activity or local-government finances would challenge this interpretation.

**Gold versus real yields.** Gold remaining firm while real yields are high is unusual enough to monitor. The thesis is invalidated if easing geopolitical risk causes gold to reconnect decisively with the traditional negative relationship to real yields.

**Frontier local-currency debt.** JPMorgan's planned GBI-EM Edge index, covering about $330 billion across 26 economies, can change benchmark visibility and accessibility for smaller sovereign local-currency markets. That is not evidence of inflows yet. The institutional question is whether benchmark inclusion produces persistent foreign participation without overwhelming local liquidity or currency risk.

## Risks and conditional paths

A more constructive path would combine durable Gulf export capacity, Brent holding near or below the recent $100–105 range, stable or falling long sovereign yields and evidence that equity participation broadens beyond a narrow set of large companies. In that environment, lower energy inflation could improve real household income and corporate margins before central banks materially ease.

A more adverse path would begin with renewed verified loss of Gulf export capacity, another rise in long-term sovereign yields or a concrete deterioration in U.S.-China trade and technology rules. Energy importers, leveraged borrowers and companies dependent on low discount rates would face the clearest transmission.

The strongest evidence against the cautious assessment is the continued resilience of U.S. large-cap earnings and equity prices. If credit spreads remain contained and earnings revisions stay positive, listed corporate financing conditions may be less restrictive than sovereign yields imply. The underappreciated risk is the opposite: calm indices may obscure deterioration among smaller borrowers and in fund flows until refinancing needs become immediate.

## What to watch next

Sunday's Bessent-He meeting is the first near-term political catalyst. The useful outputs are concrete changes in tariffs, AI controls, rare-earth access or trade rules rather than diplomatic tone. In markets, watch the U.S. 10-year around 5%, the 2s30s slope, Brent around $100–105, gold versus real yields, the yen after the BOJ hike, U.S. equity participation and weekly fund flows. In Brazil, the next test is whether the local long end follows the Selic lower or continues to demand compensation for fiscal and inflation risk.
