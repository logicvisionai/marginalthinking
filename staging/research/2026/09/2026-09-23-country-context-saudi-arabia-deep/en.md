# Saudi Arabia: from dynastic-rentier state to investment state — oil power, sovereign capital and the limits of diversification

## Executive assessment

Saudi Arabia is in the middle of a state-led transformation that is deeper than a conventional diversification program. The central change is institutional: hydrocarbon rents that were historically converted primarily into public employment, infrastructure, subsidies, services and reserve accumulation are increasingly being converted through the Public Investment Fund (PIF), sector strategies, industrial policy, logistics infrastructure, tourism, technology, mining, defense localization and large urban projects into assets intended to generate future productive capacity and non-oil income.

That does not mean the Saudi economy has ceased to be oil-dependent. The strongest evidence instead points to a **dual transformation**. Non-oil output, private employment, tourism, logistics, mining, financial markets and new service sectors have expanded materially, while oil still anchors exports, fiscal space, the balance of payments, the sovereign balance sheet and the state's ability to absorb execution risk. The key analytical question is therefore not whether diversification exists. It does. The harder question is whether the new non-oil economy can reproduce itself with progressively less marginal support from hydrocarbon cash flow and state-directed capital.

The evidence through September 2026 is mixed but substantial. The IMF estimates real GDP grew 4.6 percent in 2025, with non-oil activity remaining strong. Vision 2030's 2025 reporting states that non-oil activities account for more than half of total output. Tourism reached roughly 123 million domestic and inbound trips in 2025 under the official statistical framework, with tourism spending around SAR304 billion. PIF reports more than US$900 billion in assets under management and more than US$199 billion invested in new Saudi projects over 2021–2025. Saudi female labor-force participation has risen far above its 2016 baseline, and unemployment among Saudi citizens has fallen substantially, although quarterly indicators remain volatile.

At the same time, several constraints are visible. Petroleum still accounted for roughly two thirds of merchandise exports in late 2025 and early 2026. The 2026 budget was set at SAR1.313 trillion of expenditure against SAR1.147 trillion of revenue, implying a planned deficit of SAR165 billion, about 3.3 percent of GDP; public debt was projected to rise to roughly 32.7 percent of GDP. The IMF argues that the external position in 2025 was moderately weaker than warranted by fundamentals and that medium-term fiscal consolidation remains necessary. Foreign direct investment as a share of GDP remained below the Vision 2030 interim target. National non-oil exports have grown less consistently than the broader category that includes re-exports. These distinctions matter because they separate genuine domestic productive capacity from logistics and transshipment gains.

The PIF itself is entering a second phase. Its 2026–2030 strategy explicitly shifts from rapid balance-sheet expansion toward value realization, investment efficiency, risk-adjusted returns and greater private-sector participation. That recalibration is economically significant because it implies that Saudi policymakers recognize a constraint inherent in the first phase of transformation: the state can create sectors and infrastructure rapidly, but durable diversification ultimately requires private demand, independent cash flow, export competitiveness and productivity rather than indefinite sovereign funding.

The 2026 regional conflict provided an unplanned stress test. The IMF reported severe disruption to shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, lower oil-export volumes and weaker non-oil confidence, while the East-West pipeline to Red Sea ports and Aramco's overseas inventories reduced the immediate loss of oil deliveries. Higher oil prices more than offset the volume decline in the IMF's baseline, creating a revenue windfall even as real activity slowed. This episode demonstrates both Saudi Arabia's resilience and the continued centrality of hydrocarbon geography: diversification of sectors does not eliminate common exposure to shipping, energy infrastructure and regional security.

Saudi Arabia's political structure is highly centralized. The Basic Law defines the system as a monarchy, with succession confined to descendants of the founder, King Abdulaziz. The King, Crown Prince, Council of Ministers, Council of Economic and Development Affairs, security institutions and PIF form a concentrated decision architecture capable of reallocating resources quickly. This can accelerate coordinated infrastructure and industrial policy. It also concentrates execution and portfolio risk: when the state is simultaneously regulator, investor, land allocator, infrastructure provider and major buyer, weak price signals or project-selection errors can propagate across sectors.

Social transformation is equally material. Women's participation in education, employment, mobility and public life has expanded markedly over the past decade; entertainment, tourism and cultural industries have widened the domestic consumption and employment base. Saudi authorities present these changes as part of modernization under Vision 2030. Human-rights organizations, while acknowledging some reforms, continue to report restrictions on expression, association, due process and aspects of family law, as well as abuses affecting migrant workers. A neutral assessment should hold both evidence sets together rather than treat either as a complete description of Saudi society.

The kingdom's foreign policy is similarly multi-vector rather than easily classified as alignment with a single bloc. The United States remains the primary defense partner and in 2025 the two governments announced a Strategic Defense Agreement, major defense purchases, AI cooperation and large investment commitments. China is Saudi Arabia's largest goods-trade partner in several recent periods and a major energy customer and industrial partner. Russia remains central through OPEC+ and energy diplomacy. Relations with Iran, restored diplomatically through the 2023 Beijing agreement, experienced severe stress during the 2026 regional conflict but diplomatic channels remained open and Saudi Arabia later welcomed a U.S.–Iran agreement to halt military operations. Saudi Arabia has not established diplomatic relations with Israel and continues to state that recognition of a Palestinian state is a condition for normalization.

The central assessment is therefore **medium-high confidence**: Saudi Arabia has moved beyond a classical distributive rentier model toward an **investment state** that uses hydrocarbons as financing, energy and strategic leverage while attempting to construct new productive ecosystems. The transition is real. Its ultimate success cannot yet be inferred from non-oil GDP growth or completed projects alone. The decisive test is whether non-oil exports, private capital formation, productivity, citizen employment, portfolio returns and internationally competitive firms continue to deepen when the state becomes more selective in allocating capital.

**Information cutoff: 28 September 2026.**

## Research question and evidence discipline

The report asks:

**Can Saudi Arabia convert hydrocarbon rents and sovereign financial capacity into a diversified productive system whose firms, exports, employment and fiscal base remain viable as direct state support becomes more selective?**

This question requires separating several concepts that are often collapsed:

1. non-oil GDP from non-oil export competitiveness;
2. state-created demand from autonomous private demand;
3. construction spending from productive utilization of completed assets;
4. FDI announcements from realized foreign capital and operating capacity;
5. sovereign-wealth growth from risk-adjusted portfolio performance;
6. job localization from productivity growth;
7. formal social reform from uniform implementation;
8. diplomatic diversification from strategic equidistance.

The evidence is classified by source and function.

| Evidence class | Examples | Best use | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Saudi primary statistics | GASTAT, Ministry of Finance, SAMA | output, trade, labor, fiscal and monetary data | official definitions and revisions must be tracked |
| State-corporate reporting | Aramco, PIF, Ma'aden, GAMI | operating data, strategy, investment and sector capacity | management reporting is not independent impact evaluation |
| Multilateral analysis | IMF, World Bank | macro consistency, financial-sector and fiscal analysis | models depend on assumptions and official data |
| Legal/institutional texts | Basic Law, succession law, labor and family law | formal political and legal architecture | law does not automatically establish implementation |
| External rights monitoring | OHCHR mechanisms, HRW, Amnesty | documented rights concerns and implementation gaps | may rely on cases, interviews and external access rather than national samples |
| Academic history | Cambridge, Library of Congress and peer-reviewed work | long-run mechanisms and competing interpretations | historical interpretation varies by author |

```flow
Hydrocarbon rent → fiscal and sovereign balance sheet → public investment / PIF / infrastructure → private-sector opportunity → productive capacity → exports, profits, wages and taxes → reduced marginal dependence on oil
```

The transformation is structurally successful only if the right side of that chain becomes increasingly capable of financing itself.

## Geography is an economic asset and a security constraint

Saudi Arabia combines three geographies in one state.

The eastern region contains the core oil and gas system: Ghawar and other fields, processing infrastructure, petrochemical complexes and Gulf export terminals. The western region contains the Red Sea ports, Jeddah, the holy cities, tourism corridors and Yanbu's energy infrastructure. Riyadh, in the interior, has become the central administrative, financial and increasingly corporate headquarters hub.

This geography creates strategic redundancy. The East-West pipeline allows crude to move from the eastern producing system to Yanbu on the Red Sea, bypassing the Strait of Hormuz for part of export volumes. The IMF concluded that this redundancy materially reduced the impact of the 2026 Hormuz disruption.

It also creates shared exposure. Tourism depends on aviation and regional confidence; industry depends on imported machinery and intermediate goods; water systems depend on energy-intensive desalination and long-distance transmission; oil and petrochemicals depend on ports and pipelines. A security shock can therefore transmit across sectors that appear diversified in national accounts.

```map
Riyadh | government, finance, PIF, corporate headquarters and technology | central capital-allocation node
Makkah and Madinah | pilgrimage and service economy | durable global religious demand and infrastructure pressure
Jeddah and Red Sea corridor | logistics, tourism and maritime access | alternative external route and non-oil services
Yanbu | refining, petrochemicals and Red Sea oil terminal | strategic redundancy for eastern crude
Northern and western mineral belts | phosphate, gold, copper and exploration | emerging mining and processing base
NEOM / northwest | urban, tourism, industrial and energy experimentation | high capital intensity and execution risk
```

## Three Saudi state formations matter more than a single founding date

Saudi state formation is often compressed into a single story beginning with a political-religious alliance in eighteenth-century Diriyah. The historical record is more complex.

The first Saudi state expanded from central Arabia in the eighteenth century and was destroyed in 1818 by forces of Muhammad Ali of Egypt acting under Ottoman authority. A second Saudi polity emerged in Riyadh in the nineteenth century and collapsed in 1891 amid internal rivalry and competition with the Al Rashid of Ha'il. Abdulaziz ibn Saud recaptured Riyadh in 1902 and then built the modern state through a multi-decade process that incorporated Najd, al-Ahsa, Ha'il, the Hijaz and other territories. The Kingdom of Saudi Arabia was proclaimed in 1932.

The relationship between the Al Saud and the religious reform movement associated with Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab is an important part of this history but should not be treated as a complete causal explanation. Traditional accounts date a political-religious pact to around 1744. Academic scholarship has emphasized the role of religious mobilization in centralization, but more recent work also stresses firearms, trade, local rivalries, taxation and military organization. The state emerged through a combination of ideology, coercion, commerce and coalition-building.

This distinction matters today because Saudi political legitimacy has multiple historical foundations: dynastic rule, custodianship of the two holy mosques, state-building, distributive capacity, national identity and, increasingly, performance under Vision 2030.

## The incorporation of the Hijaz changed the scale of Saudi legitimacy

Control of Makkah and Madinah transformed the political position of Abdulaziz. The ruler of a central Arabian state also became custodian of Islam's two holiest mosques and administrator of the Hajj.

Pilgrimage created economic and diplomatic linkages before oil. It generated customs, commerce, transport, hospitality and international contact. After oil, the state could finance enormous expansions of holy-site infrastructure, airports, roads, health systems and crowd management.

Religious tourism is therefore not simply one diversification sector among many. It is an economic activity embedded in the state's political and religious role. The modern Pilgrim Experience Program attempts to convert this historically durable demand into a larger service ecosystem.

## Political architecture remains monarchical and highly centralized

Saudi Arabia's Basic Law of Governance states that the system is monarchical and that succession is confined to the sons and descendants of King Abdulaziz. The Succession Commission Law provides a formal process for selecting a crown prince, while the King retains central executive and appointive authority under the Basic Law and related statutes.

The government distinguishes judicial, regulatory and executive authorities, but this is not a separation-of-powers model equivalent to a parliamentary democracy. The Shura Council participates in legislation and policy review but is appointed, not elected as a national legislature. The Council of Ministers exercises executive functions. The Council of Economic and Development Affairs coordinates economic strategy.

Since 2017, Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman has held unusually broad responsibility across economic transformation, security and sovereign investment and has served as prime minister since 2022. The concentration creates a short chain between strategic decision and implementation.

Economically, this can reduce coordination failures. Land, infrastructure, financing, licensing, procurement and industrial policy can be moved in parallel. But the same system can create correlated risk because institutions may share the same strategic assumptions and because independent market discipline enters later in the project cycle.

## The state-religion relationship is changing without disappearing

The Saudi state historically relied on a division of roles between dynastic political authority and a religious establishment rooted in the reform tradition associated with Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab. The balance has never been static.

Over the past decade, the state has reduced the autonomous coercive role of religious institutions, expanded entertainment and tourism, opened additional forms of public cultural activity, increased women's participation in work and public life and codified areas of law that previously depended more heavily on judicial discretion.

This should not be described as secularization in a European constitutional sense. The Basic Law still grounds state authority in the Qur'an and Sunnah, and Islamic law remains central to the legal order. The transformation is better understood as **greater state control over the institutional interpretation and administration of religion**, alongside a broader economic and social policy agenda.

## Oil converted territorial rule into a modern fiscal state

The 1933 oil concession with Standard Oil of California preceded commercial production at scale. Oil exports transformed the state's fiscal constraint.

Before oil, central government revenue was limited relative to the territory it governed. Petroleum created an external rent stream that did not require broad taxation of citizen income. That allowed rapid expansion of roads, electricity, schools, hospitals, security institutions, water systems, housing and public employment.

Aramco gradually moved from foreign concessionary control to Saudi ownership. Full state ownership, the development of domestic technical capacity and the later partial public listing transformed the company into both a global corporation and a core institution of the Saudi fiscal state.

The mechanism is central:

```flow
Low-cost hydrocarbon production → export revenue → Aramco taxes/dividends → government and PIF balance sheets → infrastructure, wages, services and investment → domestic demand and state capacity
```

Vision 2030 attempts to add a second loop:

```flow
Oil-financed capital → new sectors and infrastructure → private firms and exports → wages, profits and non-oil taxes → reinvestment independent of additional oil transfers
```

The second loop is the diversification test.

## The 1970s boom created the distributive state at scale

The 1973 oil-price shock dramatically increased Saudi export revenue and accelerated public investment. Infrastructure, ministries, education, health and urban development expanded. Foreign labor entered in large numbers because domestic labor supply and skills could not match the speed of construction and service expansion.

This produced a durable political economy: citizens gained access to public employment, subsidized services, low direct taxation and state-financed development; expatriate labor became essential to the private sector; domestic business expanded around public contracts and consumption.

The arrangement supported rapid modernization but created three long-term structural problems.

First, public-sector wages became an implicit benchmark for citizen employment expectations. Second, private firms developed a strong incentive to use lower-cost foreign labor. Third, fiscal capacity remained sensitive to oil prices even as the economy became physically more complex.

## Lower oil prices exposed the fiscal structure before Vision 2030

The 1980s oil-market downturn and later price cycles repeatedly forced expenditure adjustment. The 1990–91 Gulf crisis added large security costs. The 2000s oil boom then allowed renewed accumulation of reserves, infrastructure spending and public-sector expansion.

The 2014–2016 oil-price decline was a decisive policy shock because it arrived after decades of demographic growth and rising expenditure commitments. It made the opportunity cost of relying on hydrocarbon revenue more visible and formed the fiscal context in which Vision 2030 was launched in 2016.

Vision 2030 was therefore not merely a branding exercise around tourism. It was a response to an intertemporal fiscal problem: how to convert a finite and volatile hydrocarbon advantage into productive assets, private employment and tax bases before demographic and technological changes reduce the state's room for maneuver.

## Vision 2030 changed the state from distributor to active portfolio manager

The most important institutional change under Vision 2030 is the expanded role of the PIF.

The traditional rentier model distributes resource income through the budget. The investment-state model allocates part of national wealth through a sovereign balance sheet that owns, creates and finances companies and projects.

PIF reports more than US$900 billion in assets under management in 2025. Between 2021 and 2025 it reports more than US$199 billion of cumulative investment in new Saudi projects. It also reports a large cumulative contribution to real non-oil GDP.

These figures measure scale, not proof of additionality. A sovereign fund can raise GDP by spending even if some projects later produce weak returns. The more demanding test is whether PIF investments create firms and ecosystems that attract independent capital and generate competitive cash flow.

The PIF's 2026–2030 strategy explicitly recognizes this transition. It describes a move from rapid growth toward sustained value creation, with stronger emphasis on investment efficiency, risk-adjusted returns and private-sector participation.

That shift is one of the strongest pieces of evidence that the Saudi model is entering a second phase.

## PIF is simultaneously an investor, industrial-policy institution and macroeconomic actor

PIF's economic role is unusual because it operates at several levels.

It invests globally to preserve and compound wealth. It creates domestic companies. It finances infrastructure and real estate. It anchors new sectors. It can crowd private firms into supply chains. It can also influence aggregate investment, credit demand, land values and labor demand.

This creates potential synergies. A tourism destination can be coordinated with airlines, airports, hospitality, entertainment and local supply chains. A mining strategy can be coordinated with rail, ports, power and downstream processing.

It also creates concentration risk. If several ecosystems depend on the same pool of sovereign capital, imported construction capacity and government-backed demand, they can compete with one another for finance and labor. The IMF's interest in sovereign-bank linkages and bank exposure to large projects reflects this systemic dimension.

## Giga-projects should be evaluated as portfolios, not symbols

NEOM, Diriyah, Qiddiya, Red Sea developments and other major projects are often treated in external debate as shorthand for either Saudi modernization or excessive ambition. Neither framing is analytically sufficient.

A large project should be decomposed into assets. Airports, hotels, utilities, housing, industrial zones, data centers and transport links can have different economics. Some assets may reach operating scale while others are delayed, redesigned or reprioritized.

The PIF's 2026 strategy includes NEOM as one of six Vision Portfolio ecosystems while simultaneously emphasizing selectivity and capital efficiency. This is evidence of continuation with recalibration, not simple abandonment or unchanged acceleration.

The correct indicators are completion, occupancy, utilization, private co-investment, revenue, operating cost and return on invested capital.

## The fiscal transition is real but oil still finances risk absorption

The 2026 budget projected revenue of SAR1.147 trillion and expenditure of SAR1.313 trillion, producing a planned deficit of SAR165 billion, around 3.3 percent of GDP. Public debt was projected to rise from about 31.7 percent of GDP in 2025 to 32.7 percent in 2026.

These debt ratios remain low relative to many advanced economies, and Saudi Arabia retains sovereign assets and central-bank reserves. The issue is not immediate solvency. The issue is the direction of fiscal dependence while investment commitments remain large.

Non-oil revenue has increased through value-added tax, fees and other instruments. This changes the relationship between the state and households because public finance is no longer based as exclusively on distributing external rent.

The fiscal balance should therefore be analyzed through the **non-oil primary balance**, which measures how much spending is supported by non-oil revenue. The IMF continues to recommend medium-term consolidation and more selective spending.

| Fiscal mechanism | Traditional structure | Current transition | Analytical risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| oil revenue | dominant financing source | still central but increasingly complemented | price and volume volatility |
| VAT and fees | limited role historically | larger recurrent non-oil revenue | cost-of-living and business burden |
| public debt | relatively small | rising from low base | financing large investment pipeline |
| PIF | reserve/portfolio role | active domestic development investor | return and concentration risk |
| private capital | supplementary | expected to crowd into state-created ecosystems | state projects may crowd out rather than crowd in |

## The dollar peg is a stabilizing institution with a cost

The Saudi riyal's peg to the U.S. dollar anchors expectations and reduces currency risk for oil trade, imports and investment. SAMA maintains foreign reserves and regulates a well-capitalized banking system.

The trade-off is monetary autonomy. Saudi interest-rate conditions are strongly influenced by U.S. monetary policy even when the domestic cycle differs. During investment booms, this can contribute to strong credit growth; during periods of global tightening, financing conditions can become restrictive.

The peg is sustainable when fiscal and external balances preserve confidence and reserves. Diversification does not reduce the peg's importance automatically because imported capital goods and dollar-linked energy revenue remain central.

## Banks are increasingly tied to the investment cycle

Saudi banks have benefited from population growth, mortgages, corporate investment and large projects. The IMF judged the banking sector well-positioned in 2026, with strong capital and liquidity buffers, but highlighted the need to monitor foreign-currency funding, sovereign-bank linkages and exposures to large projects.

This is a key transition risk. When government, PIF and banks finance the same investment cycle, asset quality can become correlated. A project delay can affect contractors, developers, banks and public entities simultaneously.

Capital markets therefore matter as a diversification of funding, not just a financial-sector objective. Equity, sukuk, bonds, private credit and foreign participation can distribute risk beyond banks and the sovereign balance sheet.

## Diversification is stronger in GDP than in merchandise exports

Saudi non-oil output has grown substantially, but merchandise exports remain dominated by petroleum and petrochemicals.

GASTAT reported petroleum at 67.4 percent of goods exports in December 2025 and 68.7 percent in February 2026. Non-oil exports including re-exports have grown rapidly, but the distinction between national exports and re-exports is important. In Q4 2025, re-exports rose strongly while national non-oil exports declined slightly year on year.

A logistics hub can create value through re-exporting, warehousing and distribution. That is legitimate diversification. But it is different from domestic industrial deepening.

The report therefore tracks three separate variables:

1. national non-oil exports;
2. re-exports and logistics flows;
3. services exports such as tourism and transport.

## Aramco remains the economic anchor even as the economy diversifies

Aramco's 2025 annual report recorded adjusted net income around US$105 billion and continued large capital expenditure. Its significance extends beyond profit.

The company supplies fiscal revenue, dividends, engineering capability, procurement demand, research capacity, gas development, refining and chemicals. It is also a mechanism for industrial localization through supplier programs and domestic procurement.

Saudi diversification is therefore partly **downstream from oil**, not outside it. Petrochemicals, refining, materials, industrial services and energy-intensive manufacturing can diversify products and capabilities while remaining connected to hydrocarbon feedstock.

This is economically different from dependence on crude exports alone, but it does not eliminate exposure to energy markets.

## Gas is becoming a domestic industrial strategy

The Jafurah unconventional gas development is central to the next phase of Saudi energy policy. Aramco expects Jafurah to ramp toward 2 billion standard cubic feet per day of sales gas by 2030, alongside ethane and liquids production. The Fadhili expansion and Tanajib facilities also increase gas-processing capacity.

More domestic gas can substitute for liquid fuels in power generation, free crude and products for export, supply petrochemical feedstock and support industrial electricity demand.

Gas therefore links hydrocarbon development to diversification. The mechanism is not "oil versus clean energy"; it is a portfolio in which gas, renewables and storage can reduce the opportunity cost of burning exportable liquids domestically.

## Renewable energy is scaling from projects toward a system

The Vision 2030 Annual Report 2025 describes a broad renewable pipeline of approximately 64 GW of proposed projects and major expansion of solar, wind and storage.

The key distinction is between announced, awarded, under-construction and grid-connected capacity. Saudi Arabia has achieved very low auction prices on some solar and wind projects, but low contract prices do not by themselves establish system-wide decarbonization.

Integration requires transmission, storage, flexible gas generation, demand management and grid modernization.

The economic value is larger than emissions reduction. Low-cost electricity can support desalination, mining, data centers, green hydrogen and manufacturing if it is delivered reliably.

## Water is strategic infrastructure, not a background utility

Saudi Arabia's arid climate makes water an economic constraint on cities, agriculture, tourism and industry. Desalination therefore functions like core national infrastructure.

In August 2026, the Saudi Water Authority reported four Jubail and Ras Al-Khair desalination projects with combined design capacity of 2.5 million cubic meters per day approaching or moving through implementation stages.

Modern reverse-osmosis systems can reduce energy intensity relative to older thermal desalination. But desalination still requires capital, power, pipelines and environmental management of brine.

Water policy is therefore directly linked to energy transition and urban expansion. A new city or industrial cluster is not economically complete until its water production and transmission have been financed.

## Mining is the strongest candidate for a new resource-based export system

Saudi policy describes mining as the third pillar of industry after oil and petrochemicals. The official estimate of mineral wealth has been raised to roughly SAR9.4 trillion, about US$2.5 trillion.

The estimate is not equivalent to proven commercially recoverable reserves. Its significance is as a geological-resource inventory that can justify exploration.

The sector is already producing phosphate, aluminum and gold at scale through Ma'aden and related projects. Ma'aden reported record phosphate fertilizer production in 2025 and substantial aluminum and gold output.

The next step is to move from exploration licenses to mines, processing and export value chains in copper, zinc, gold, phosphate, bauxite and potentially rare-earth-related activities.

The execution test is similar to other diversification sectors: geological potential becomes economic capacity only with drilling, reserves, permits, water, power, transport, processing, financing and customers.

## Defense localization links security spending to industrial policy

Saudi Arabia historically imported a large share of its defense equipment. Vision 2030 set a target to localize more than 50 percent of military expenditure on equipment and services by 2030.

GAMI reports that the localization rate reached 24.89 percent in 2024, up from a low baseline, and that hundreds of firms and licenses now participate in the sector. World Defense Show 2026 highlighted procurement and industrial-participation agreements.

Defense localization can create engineering, electronics, maintenance, software and advanced-manufacturing capability with civilian spillovers. But it also has risks: protected procurement can produce expensive domestic capacity if scale or export markets are insufficient.

The correct measurement is not only localization percentage. It is domestic value added, intellectual-property ownership, engineering capability, export competitiveness and lifecycle sustainment.

## Tourism has become a measurable non-oil demand engine

Saudi Arabia opened more broadly to international leisure tourism after 2019 while continuing to expand religious tourism.

The Ministry of Tourism reported approximately 122.6 million domestic and inbound tourist trips in 2025 and roughly SAR303.7 billion in spending. Vision 2030's annual report states that the original 100 million visitor target was exceeded early and the 2030 ambition was raised to 150 million.

Tourism creates foreign exchange, service employment and regional development. It also uses large quantities of infrastructure capital and imported labor.

The productive test is whether destinations achieve repeat visitation, hotel occupancy, local supply chains and private returns after opening. A destination with high construction spending but low utilization is capital formation without equivalent service productivity.

## Pilgrimage is structurally different from leisure tourism

Hajj and Umrah demand derives from religious practice rather than discretionary destination branding. That gives Saudi Arabia a globally unique service export.

Investment in airports, rail, digital visas, crowd management, hotels and health services can increase the economic value created per pilgrim while improving capacity and safety.

Because the state is custodian of the two holy mosques, the sector also carries non-economic obligations. Maximizing revenue cannot be the only policy objective; affordability, access, security and religious administration matter.

## Demography creates both a labor opportunity and a migrant dependence

The 2022 census counted 32.2 million residents, of whom 18.8 million were Saudi citizens and 13.4 million were non-Saudis. Saudis were unusually young: 63 percent were under age 30.

More recent World Bank estimates place total population near 37 million in 2025, reflecting continued migration and population growth.

The labor market therefore contains two distinct demographic systems.

The citizen population is young and needs rising numbers of skilled jobs. The expatriate population supplies large parts of construction, services, domestic work, health, engineering and other sectors.

Diversification can actually increase demand for foreign labor in the short run because new projects require skills and scale faster than the domestic workforce can adjust.

## Saudization is a productivity problem, not only a quota problem

Saudi labor policy uses sector-specific localization requirements, training, wage support and regulation to increase citizen employment.

The results are visible. Vision 2030 data show Saudi unemployment substantially below its 2016 baseline. GASTAT reported a record-low 6.3 percent Saudi unemployment rate in Q1 2025, though it later rose to 7.5 percent in Q3, illustrating quarterly variation. Female labor-force participation has risen from roughly 23 percent around the Vision baseline to the mid-30s.

The next challenge is job quality and productivity.

If localization simply replaces a lower-paid expatriate worker with a higher-paid citizen in the same production process, unit labor cost rises. Sustainable localization requires automation, management improvement, training and movement into higher-value activities.

Therefore wages, tenure, promotion and productivity are better indicators than localization quotas alone.

## Women's economic participation is one of the clearest structural changes

Women have gained access to a wider range of employment, mobility, entrepreneurship and public activity over the past decade. The female labor-force participation rate has risen sharply and already exceeded the original Vision 2030 target, which was subsequently revised upward.

This matters macroeconomically because it expands effective labor supply and increases the return on female education.

It also changes household behavior. Dual-income households alter housing demand, transport, childcare and consumption. Firms gain access to a larger talent pool.

Formal legal change has not removed all gender distinctions. The 2022 Personal Status Law codified family-law rules and Saudi authorities argue that it strengthens predictability and protects rights including alimony and custody. Human-rights organizations argue that the same law retains unequal provisions concerning guardianship and marital obligations.

A neutral assessment should describe the law and the disagreement rather than collapse legal modernization and substantive equality into one variable.

## Migrant labor remains essential and contested

Non-Saudi workers are not a marginal labor category; they represent a large share of the resident population and private workforce.

Saudi authorities state that labor reforms introduced since 2021 reduced workers' dependence on individual sponsors by expanding job mobility and exit procedures and that wage-protection systems have expanded coverage.

Human-rights organizations continue to report wage theft, recruitment debt, occupational safety problems, domestic-worker vulnerability and residual sponsorship-related dependency.

Both evidence sets can be true simultaneously: formal rules can improve while enforcement remains uneven across sectors and worker categories.

The economic importance is direct. A diversification model built on construction, hospitality, logistics and care services depends on attracting labor while avoiding a low-productivity dual market in which citizens and migrants occupy structurally separate tiers.

## Social liberalization expands domestic demand but remains state-directed

Entertainment, cinema, concerts, sports, tourism and cultural events have grown rapidly. These sectors create jobs and retain consumer spending that previously leaked abroad.

The change is often described politically, but its economic mechanism is straightforward: domestic consumers gain more service options, foreign visitors gain more reasons to travel, and firms invest in venues, hospitality, media and retail.

The liberalization remains top-down. Political association and public dissent remain much more restricted than consumer and cultural choice.

That asymmetry is a central feature of the current Saudi model: broader social and economic participation under concentrated political authority.

## Rights and legal reform require source separation

Saudi authorities emphasize codification of laws, judicial modernization, women's economic participation, worker-protection reforms and state institutions dedicated to human rights.

External organizations including Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International document continued restrictions on expression and association, use of the death penalty, due-process concerns and migrant-worker abuses. Their 2026 reports describe a particularly high number of executions in 2025.

These sources do not answer exactly the same question. Government sources identify laws, programs and official implementation. Rights organizations investigate violations and cases.

The report therefore avoids an aggregate label such as "liberalizing" or "repressive" as a substitute for analysis. It tracks concrete domains separately: labor mobility, family law, judicial procedure, expression, association, criminal punishment and social participation.

## Education and human capital are now the constraint behind capital abundance

Saudi Arabia can finance factories and infrastructure faster than it can create experienced technical workforces.

Vision 2030 therefore links university outcomes, vocational education and labor-market alignment to economic diversification. The IMF notes that employment outcomes for graduates have improved but remain below long-term targets in several categories.

The problem is qualitative. Advanced manufacturing, mining, AI, defense and financial services require engineering, software, management and technical skills that cannot be created through localization quotas alone.

Human-capital policy therefore determines whether imported technology becomes domestic capability.

## AI and digital infrastructure are emerging as a new capital-intensive strategy

Saudi Arabia is investing in data centers, cloud infrastructure, digital government and artificial intelligence. PIF's strategy identifies advanced manufacturing and innovation as a core ecosystem and emphasizes AI in investment operations.

The United States and Saudi Arabia announced an AI memorandum of understanding in 2025, linking access to advanced U.S. systems with safeguards around technology and foreign influence.

AI fits the Saudi model because it uses capital abundance, cheap energy potential and centralized infrastructure planning.

Its constraint is similar to other sectors: imported compute does not automatically create domestic intellectual property or productivity. The meaningful indicators are utilization, local model development, skilled employment, research output and applications in Saudi firms.

## The United States remains the primary security partner

Saudi-U.S. ties are built around defense, energy, investment, technology and regional security.

In November 2025 the White House announced a U.S.-Saudi Strategic Defense Agreement, additional defense sales, critical-minerals cooperation and AI agreements, following major commercial and defense commitments earlier in the year.

Official U.S. statements described the United States as Saudi Arabia's primary strategic partner.

The relationship is not frictionless and has historically been affected by oil policy, regional wars, human rights and differing approaches to Iran and Israel. But the depth of defense interoperability, arms supply and financial ties makes it qualitatively different from Saudi relations with China or Russia.

## China is simultaneously an energy customer, supplier and strategic economic partner

China has become Saudi Arabia's largest goods-trade partner in many recent periods and a major buyer of energy.

The relationship now extends into petrochemicals, industrial investment, digital infrastructure and diplomacy. In June 2026, Chinese and Saudi foreign ministers described the relationship as a comprehensive strategic partnership.

The China relationship does not imply Saudi substitution of the United States. The economic and security relationships are functionally different.

Riyadh's strategy is better described as **portfolio diplomacy**: preserve the U.S. security architecture while deepening trade, energy and investment relationships with Asian powers.

## Relations with Iran combine de-escalation mechanisms and enduring rivalry

Saudi Arabia and Iran restored diplomatic relations through the 2023 Beijing-brokered agreement after years of confrontation.

That restoration reduced one channel of escalation but did not eliminate strategic rivalry. The two states have different security relationships, regional partners and political systems.

In early 2026 Saudi and Iranian foreign ministers still discussed regional stability and diplomacy. During the subsequent regional conflict, Saudi Arabia publicly accused Iran of attacks affecting the kingdom and other Gulf states and expelled Iranian diplomatic personnel. In June, Saudi Arabia welcomed the U.S.–Iran agreement to end military operations and begin negotiations.

The sequence demonstrates why "rapprochement" should not be interpreted as alliance. Diplomatic channels can coexist with deterrence and acute conflict.

## Russia is important mainly through energy coordination and strategic optionality

Saudi-Russian relations expanded through OPEC+, investment dialogue and high-level diplomacy.

OPEC+ gives Riyadh and Moscow a mechanism to coordinate production policy despite different fiscal structures and geopolitical alignments.

The relationship provides Saudi Arabia with strategic optionality but does not match the institutional depth of the U.S. defense relationship or the scale of Chinese trade.

The main economic indicator is not political rhetoric but whether energy coordination remains effective during periods when Saudi and Russian market incentives diverge.

## GCC integration can make diversification more resilient

Saudi Arabia is the largest economy in the Gulf Cooperation Council. Deeper GCC customs, logistics, rail, power and financial integration can increase the effective market size available to Saudi firms.

In 2026 the GCC continued work on logistics integration involving the Gulf Railway Authority and Customs Union Authority.

This matters for sectors that cannot achieve efficient scale on Saudi domestic demand alone.

GCC integration also increases redundancy. Ports, grids and land corridors can help absorb localized shocks if infrastructure and regulations are interoperable.

## Israel and Palestine remain a constraint on regional normalization

Saudi Arabia has no diplomatic relations with Israel as of the information cutoff.

The Saudi Ministry of Foreign Affairs has repeatedly stated that the kingdom will not establish diplomatic relations with Israel without an independent Palestinian state on the 1967 lines with East Jerusalem as its capital. Saudi statements in 2026 continued to oppose settlement expansion and support a two-state settlement.

This official position places a political condition on the normalization process that had been discussed with the United States before the Gaza war.

The analytical consequence is that potential Saudi-Israel economic integration remains contingent rather than part of the realized diversification base.

## BRICS status is institutionally ambiguous and should not be overinterpreted

The 2026 Indian BRICS presidency website lists Saudi Arabia among full members. However, Reuters reported in September 2026, citing sources familiar with Saudi policy, that Riyadh had still not formally completed accession and was participating without final commitment.

These accounts are inconsistent. The report therefore treats Saudi BRICS status as **contested/administratively ambiguous at the cutoff** rather than using membership as evidence of a geopolitical bloc choice.

Regardless of the label, Saudi Arabia engages BRICS states extensively through trade, energy and investment while maintaining deep relations with the United States and G20 institutions.

The broader strategic pattern is diversification of partnerships, not demonstrated abandonment of existing alliances.

## Climate policy is constrained by both heat and hydrocarbon advantage

Saudi Arabia faces extreme heat, water scarcity and high cooling demand. These physical conditions make climate adaptation economically important independent of global emissions policy.

The kingdom simultaneously seeks to preserve value from hydrocarbons and expand renewables, carbon management, gas, hydrogen and lower-emissions industrial processes.

This creates a dual strategy: defend the competitiveness of oil and gas while investing in technologies that could matter in a lower-carbon world.

The success criterion is not announcements but the cost and operating performance of power, water, industrial decarbonization and export projects.

## Scenario 1: Productive investment state

PIF becomes more selective, private investment rises, non-oil exports deepen, national firms scale internationally and citizen productivity improves.

**Transmission:** oil and sovereign capital → infrastructure and sectors → private capital and technology → competitive firms → exports and taxes → lower marginal dependence on oil.

This is the intended Vision 2030 path. Evidence supports movement in this direction, but it is not complete.

## Scenario 2: Diversified demand, concentrated funding

Saudi Arabia develops many non-oil sectors, but their final demand remains strongly dependent on government budgets, PIF spending and hydrocarbon-backed liquidity.

**Transmission:** oil revenue → sovereign spending → tourism/construction/services → employment and GDP → continued requirement for state injections.

This can produce high non-oil GDP without equivalent fiscal independence.

## Scenario 3: Selective consolidation

Fiscal and capital constraints force further reprioritization. Some projects are delayed or reduced while mining, logistics, tourism, energy, AI and industrial clusters with stronger economics receive capital.

**Transmission:** tighter capital discipline → cancellation/delay of lower-return assets → higher utilization and returns on surviving projects → slower headline construction but stronger balance-sheet quality.

PIF's 2026 strategy makes this scenario plausible and does not necessarily represent failure.

## Scenario 4: Regional-security constraint dominates

A prolonged Gulf, Red Sea or Iran-related security shock raises insurance and logistics costs, damages infrastructure or depresses tourism and FDI.

**Transmission:** security shock → trade/aviation disruption → higher cost and weaker confidence → slower private investment → greater reliance on fiscal support → delayed diversification.

The 2026 Hormuz disruption demonstrates the mechanism even though Saudi buffers reduced the immediate impact.

## What would materially change the assessment

The assessment would strengthen if:

- national non-oil exports rise consistently faster than re-exports;
- private fixed investment grows while the public investment impulse moderates;
- FDI approaches Vision 2030 targets through realized projects rather than announcements;
- PIF portfolio companies generate rising cash returns and attract external equity;
- large projects show utilization and operating revenue;
- Saudi citizen wages and productivity rise in the private sector;
- defense and mining localization produces exports and proprietary capability;
- water and power infrastructure keeps pace with new urban and industrial demand.

The assessment would weaken if:

- non-oil growth falls sharply whenever government capital spending slows;
- debt and deficits rise without corresponding productive returns;
- banks accumulate concentrated exposure to underperforming projects;
- large projects require repeated sovereign recapitalization;
- national non-oil exports stagnate despite investment;
- labor localization increases costs without productivity;
- regional conflict repeatedly closes core logistics corridors.

## Indicators

- Real non-oil GDP and private non-oil GDP.
- Non-oil primary fiscal balance.
- Government debt and liquid reserves.
- National non-oil exports excluding re-exports.
- Services exports and tourism receipts.
- FDI inflows and realized project stock.
- PIF total shareholder return, dividends, asset disposals and private co-investment.
- Utilization and revenue of giga-project assets.
- Saudi unemployment, female labor participation, private wages and productivity.
- Expatriate employment composition and labor-rights enforcement.
- Bank credit concentration and project-finance exposures.
- Aramco dividends, capex, crude production and gas ramp-up.
- Renewable capacity actually connected to the grid.
- Desalination capacity, energy intensity and water transmission.
- Mining reserves converted from resources, output and downstream processing.
- Defense localization measured by domestic value added and IP.
- Hormuz and Red Sea shipping conditions.
- U.S., China, Iran, Russia and GCC economic-security arrangements.

## Limitations and confidence

**Overall confidence: medium-high.**

Macroeconomic and fiscal data are comparatively strong and current. Confidence is high that Saudi Arabia has achieved real sectoral diversification, larger female labor participation, rapid tourism expansion, deeper sovereign investment and meaningful industrial-policy development.

Confidence is medium on the long-run return of giga-projects and the share of non-oil activity that would persist under materially lower state investment, because many projects are still in build-out.

Confidence is medium on FDI additionality and private-sector crowding-in because announced agreements, gross inflows and operating projects are not identical.

Political and rights analysis separates formal law, government statements and external monitoring. Human-rights organizations document individual cases and patterns but do not provide a nationally representative survey of political attitudes. Saudi official sources document reforms and institutions but are not independent evaluations of enforcement.

The official estimate of SAR9.4 trillion in mineral wealth is a geological/economic-resource estimate, not a proven reserve valuation. Renewable project pipelines are not equivalent to installed operating capacity.

The 2026 regional-security environment is unusually volatile. Oil flows, fiscal revenue, shipping and diplomatic relations should be revalidated in future use.
