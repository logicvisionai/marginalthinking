# Taiwan's semiconductor-centered political economy: democratic consolidation, external surplus and strategic exposure across the Strait

Taiwan combines four characteristics that rarely coexist at comparable scale: a high-income export economy, one of the world's most concentrated advanced semiconductor manufacturing ecosystems, a competitive democratic political system, and a security environment shaped by a sovereignty dispute with the People's Republic of China (PRC). In 2026 those characteristics are reinforcing one another more strongly than at any point since the global semiconductor and artificial-intelligence investment cycle accelerated. Real GDP is projected by Taiwan's Directorate-General of Budget, Accounting and Statistics (DGBAS) to grow 11.05 percent in 2026 after 8.76 percent in 2025, while merchandise exports and the current-account surplus have surged on AI-related demand. At the same time, defense spending, grid investment, demographic policy and supply-chain diversification are increasingly treated as parts of the same resilience problem.

The central structural change is not simply that "Taiwan makes chips." It is that decades of industrial policy, engineering specialization, supplier clustering, export finance and the pure-play foundry business model created a production system whose most advanced segments are difficult to reproduce quickly elsewhere. That concentration gives Taiwan unusual economic importance, but it also creates exposure. Semiconductor manufacturing depends on imported energy, water systems, global equipment and materials, foreign customers, logistics and a dense domestic workforce. The same concentration that makes Taiwan strategically important creates incentives for the United States, Japan, Europe and firms themselves to diversify selected capacity abroad.

The political status of Taiwan cannot be reduced to a single uncontested formula. The government based in Taipei exercises effective administration over Taiwan, Penghu, Kinmen, Matsu and smaller islands under the constitutional framework of the Republic of China (ROC). The government of the People's Republic of China has never administered Taiwan since its establishment in 1949 but claims Taiwan as part of China and states that national unification is a core objective, while saying peaceful unification is preferred and retaining the option of force. Taiwan's current government states that the ROC and PRC are not subordinate to one another and that Taiwan's future must be determined by its people. Most states diplomatically recognize the PRC rather than the ROC while many maintain substantial unofficial relations with Taiwan. The United States recognizes the PRC as the sole legal government of China, acknowledges the Chinese position that there is one China and Taiwan is part of China, and maintains its own "one China" policy, unofficial relations with Taiwan and obligations under the Taiwan Relations Act. These positions overlap in some areas and differ in others; the distinctions are analytically important.

## Taiwan's strategic weight comes from production concentration, not legal status alone

Taiwan's economic importance is disproportionate to its population of roughly 23 million. The semiconductor ecosystem is concentrated around Hsinchu, Taichung, Tainan, Kaohsiung and related science parks, with Taipei and New Taipei functioning as corporate, financial and design centers.

TSMC's 2025 annual report states that advanced process technologies at 7 nanometers and below generated 74 percent of its wafer revenue and that 3-nanometer technologies alone represented 24 percent. Its 2-nanometer process entered high-volume manufacturing in the fourth quarter of 2025, with additional N2P and A16 processes scheduled for volume production in the second half of 2026. TSMC reports that its major 12-inch, 8-inch and 6-inch fabs in Taiwan remain the core of its manufacturing network even as the company expands in Arizona, Japan and Germany.

The concentration is an economic network rather than a single company. Foundries depend on integrated-circuit design houses, packaging and testing firms, specialty chemical suppliers, photomask providers, machine-tool and facility contractors, power utilities, water systems, universities and research institutes. Taiwan also hosts major firms in electronics manufacturing services, servers, networking, printed circuit boards and components.

## The current AI boom is amplifying an already export-intensive economy

DGBAS estimated real GDP growth at 12.93 percent year on year in 2026Q2 after revising 2026Q1 growth to 15.43 percent. Its August outlook projected 11.05 percent real growth for 2026 and 6.04 percent for 2027.

These rates are exceptional for a high-income economy and are heavily connected to AI, high-performance computing, semiconductor investment and information-and-communications equipment. They should not be treated as a permanent trend growth rate.

Taiwan's Ministry of Economic Affairs reported that 2025 exports reached approximately US$640.7 billion, up 34.9 percent. Information, communication and audiovisual products reached 39.2 percent of exports and electronic components 34.7 percent, reflecting the concentration of the export basket in technology-intensive goods.

By August 2026, monthly exports had reached about US$82.4 billion in preliminary trade data. The magnitude of growth illustrates the strength of the current external cycle but also raises concentration risk: a slowdown in AI capital expenditure or a semiconductor inventory correction would transmit rapidly into output, investment and tax revenue.

## Export geography has shifted materially toward the United States

The structure of Taiwan's external demand changed sharply during the U.S.-China trade conflict, pandemic-era supply-chain restructuring and the AI infrastructure boom.

The Ministry of Economic Affairs reported that in 2025 the United States accounted for approximately 31 percent of Taiwan's exports, surpassing China and Hong Kong combined at 26.6 percent. The U.S. share had increased by roughly 19 percentage points from 2017, while the China/Hong Kong share fell by roughly 15 percentage points.

This does not mean economic ties with mainland China disappeared. Cross-Strait trade remains large, particularly in electronic components, intermediate goods and manufacturing networks. Taiwanese companies also retain substantial production and investment footprints in mainland China.

The change is better described as **rebalancing rather than decoupling**.

```flow
U.S.-China trade and technology restrictions → firms reallocate final assembly and high-value production → Taiwan and ASEAN gain selected production → U.S. demand for AI servers and computing rises → U.S. becomes Taiwan's largest export market in 2025
```

The structural implication is important: Taiwan's security relationship with the United States and its largest export market are now more closely aligned than they were a decade ago, while its largest geopolitical dispute remains connected to a still-large commercial relationship with mainland China.

## The current-account surplus gives Taiwan unusual financial resilience

Taiwan has long combined export competitiveness with persistent external surpluses.

The Central Bank of the Republic of China (Taiwan) reported a current-account surplus of US$58.49 billion in 2026Q2 and US$121.03 billion for the first half of 2026. The goods surplus widened sharply as technology exports expanded.

Foreign-exchange reserves reached US$601.9 billion at the end of August 2026. These reserves provide liquidity and support confidence in the financial system, although they are not a sovereign wealth fund and should not be interpreted as freely spendable fiscal assets.

The central bank maintains a managed-floating exchange-rate regime in practice: it states that the NT dollar is principally determined by market forces but that it intervenes when large short-term flows produce disorderly movements. In August 2026 the bank explicitly cited intervention to smooth volatile capital flows.

The policy interest rate remained 2 percent after the September 2026 monetary-policy meeting. The central bank's challenge is unusual because export and investment growth are extremely strong while consumer-price inflation remains around 2 percent and housing/credit concentration remains a financial-stability concern.

## Taiwan's external surplus coexists with domestic distribution questions

Fast technology-led GDP growth does not distribute evenly.

DGBAS reported a 2025 household income quintile ratio of 6.17 and a Gini coefficient of 0.341. These figures do not indicate extreme inequality by global standards, but they coexist with large differences between technology-intensive regions and occupations and lower-productivity services.

Housing affordability is a recurring political issue, particularly in Taipei, New Taipei and Hsinchu. The central bank has used selective credit controls to reduce excessive concentration of bank lending in real estate and reported that real-estate loans as a share of total bank lending declined from a June 2024 peak of 37.6 percent to 35.2 percent by May 2026.

The macroeconomic tension is therefore not simply "growth versus stagnation." It is how an economy driven by capital-intensive technology investment transmits income gains to households, services and younger workers while land and housing remain scarce in the main economic corridors.

## Taiwan's semiconductor system was built through state coordination and private specialization

The semiconductor industry's development was not spontaneous.

The Industrial Technology Research Institute (ITRI) was established in 1973. In 1976, ITRI signed a technology-transfer and licensing agreement with RCA and sent engineers to the United States for training. A demonstration fabrication line followed.

UMC was spun out of ITRI in 1980. The government also developed Hsinchu Science Park and funded research infrastructure.

Morris Chang returned to Taiwan in the mid-1980s and led ITRI before helping establish Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company in 1987. TSMC's key institutional innovation was the pure-play foundry model: manufacture chips designed by customers rather than compete with them through its own branded semiconductor products.

That business model reduced the capital barrier facing fabless design companies and allowed specialization across the global chain.

## "Silicon shield" is an analytical hypothesis, not an automatic security guarantee

Taiwan's semiconductor importance is sometimes described as a "silicon shield": the idea that major economies have a strong incentive to prevent disruption because they depend on Taiwanese chips.

The mechanism is real to a point. A severe interruption of Taiwan's semiconductor production would affect global electronics, cloud infrastructure, AI systems, automobiles and industrial supply chains. Governments and firms therefore have strong economic incentives to preserve stability.

But semiconductor concentration does not mechanically determine military or political behavior.

A conflict itself could damage fabs, cut power and water, interrupt imports of chemicals and spare parts, halt shipping or prevent engineers from reaching facilities even if fabrication plants were never directly attacked. Advanced fabs also cannot operate indefinitely without global inputs.

The same strategic importance can motivate diversification abroad. TSMC's Arizona, Kumamoto and Dresden investments are partly business responses to customer demand, industrial policy and resilience. They reduce single-location risk while leaving substantial leading-edge R&D and production in Taiwan.

The more accurate concept is **strategic interdependence**, not invulnerability.

## Overseas fabs diversify risk without reproducing the full cluster overnight

TSMC Arizona's first fab entered volume production using 4-nanometer technology in late 2024. The second Arizona facility has been built for 3-nanometer and more advanced processes, while a third facility began construction in 2025. TSMC has announced a broader U.S. expansion that includes additional fabs, advanced packaging and R&D.

In Japan, the first JASM fab began volume production at the end of 2024 and a second fab is being built with plans that include advanced process technology. In Germany, the Dresden project focuses on specialty technologies serving automotive and industrial customers.

At the same time, TSMC's 2025 annual report states that the company is preparing multiple phases of 2-nanometer fabs in Hsinchu and Kaohsiung and will continue investing in leading-edge and advanced packaging in Taiwan.

This pattern matters. Geographic diversification does not imply a simple transfer of the semiconductor ecosystem away from Taiwan. The company is building a more distributed network while also increasing leading-edge capacity domestically.

The long-run balance will depend on labor, subsidies, customer proximity, export controls, energy, construction costs and the ability of foreign sites to reproduce supplier density and engineering learning.

## Energy is Taiwan's most obvious physical industrial dependency

Taiwan imports the overwhelming majority of its primary energy.

Its electricity system is therefore inseparable from maritime trade and industrial resilience. Taipower reported that in 2025 national electricity generation was approximately 47.8 percent natural gas, 35.4 percent coal and 13.1 percent renewables, with the remaining share coming from other sources including the final period of nuclear generation.

Taiwan's last operating reactor, Maanshan Unit 2, reached the end of its operating license and stopped in May 2025. Legislative changes later created a legal route under which nuclear plants could potentially seek continued or renewed operation subject to safety review and regulatory requirements. Nuclear policy remains politically contested.

The government continues to prioritize natural gas, renewable energy, storage, grid resilience and conservation. MOEA's 2025 electricity supply-demand report explicitly identifies AI data centers and semiconductor demand as important drivers of future electricity consumption.

The industrial constraint is straightforward:

```flow
AI and semiconductor investment → higher electricity demand → LNG / coal / renewables / storage / grid investment → reliability and cost → fab utilization and competitiveness
```

A semiconductor fab can be world-leading technologically and still be limited by power quality, water and grid resilience.

## LNG dependence creates a maritime resilience problem

Natural gas reduces local air pollution and carbon intensity relative to coal but increases dependence on continuous seaborne fuel deliveries.

Taiwan has limited indigenous fossil-fuel resources and lacks pipeline connections to external gas systems. LNG therefore arrives by sea and depends on receiving terminals, storage and shipping routes.

This creates a distinction between energy transition and security resilience. Increasing gas generation can reduce coal emissions while simultaneously increasing dependence on maritime logistics unless storage and supply diversity rise in parallel.

The government has responded with new LNG terminals, renewable deployment, storage and grid projects. The question is whether infrastructure can keep pace with electricity demand from advanced manufacturing and AI.

## Water and climate resilience matter because advanced fabs are utility-intensive

Taiwan receives significant rainfall but its geography produces uneven water availability. Steep mountains move rainfall rapidly to the sea, reservoir sedimentation reduces storage efficiency, and droughts can affect western industrial corridors.

The 2021 drought demonstrated the economic sensitivity of semiconductor and industrial water demand. Since then, recycling, reclaimed water, inter-basin transfers and new reservoirs or pipelines have received greater attention.

Climate risks also include typhoons, extreme rainfall and heat. These are not peripheral environmental issues. They affect grid reliability, transport, water availability and business continuity.

TSMC and other major manufacturers increasingly design facilities around redundancy in electricity, water and disaster response. National resilience therefore includes civil infrastructure as much as military capability.

## Taiwan's demographic constraint is now structural

Taiwan is moving rapidly into a super-aged demographic structure.

The National Development Council's August 2026 projections estimate a registered population of 23.19 million in 2026, with 20.9 percent aged 65 or older and 67.9 percent aged 15–64. Under the medium projection, total population falls to 21.97 million by 2035, 17.52 million by 2055 and 12.15 million by 2075.

Taiwan's total fertility rate fell to 0.695 in 2025, according to the NDC's latest population assumptions.

## Immigration and automation will increasingly supplement the domestic workforce

Taiwan already relies on migrant workers in manufacturing, construction, care and other sectors. Demographic decline increases the economic importance of foreign labor, international students and skilled immigration.

Automation can offset some labor scarcity in factories and logistics, but it cannot fully replace human capital in R&D, healthcare, education, defense or advanced engineering.

The semiconductor sector creates a particular tension: its global success raises demand for highly trained engineers even as the number of young people entering the labor market declines.

The long-run competitiveness question therefore shifts from simply producing graduates to retaining talent, attracting foreign specialists and raising productivity outside the technology sector.

## Taiwan's democratic transition transformed the legitimacy of the political system

The Republic of China government relocated its center of government to Taiwan after losing control of mainland China to the Chinese Communist Party in 1949.

Martial law remained in force from 1949 until 1987. During this period political opposition, press freedom and civil organization were restricted, while the state also pursued land reform, education expansion, export-led industrialization and infrastructure development.

The lifting of martial law in 1987 accelerated political liberalization. Opposition parties became legal, parliamentary institutions were reformed and direct presidential elections were introduced in 1996.

Power has since alternated between the Kuomintang (KMT) and Democratic Progressive Party (DPP), with the Taiwan People's Party (TPP) emerging as a third force.

The political system is now competitive, but institutional conflict has increased because the presidency and legislature are controlled by different partisan coalitions.

## The 2024 election created divided government

Lai Ching-te of the DPP won the January 2024 presidential election with 40.05 percent of valid votes. Hou Yu-ih of the KMT received 33.49 percent and Ko Wen-je of the TPP 26.46 percent.

The legislative election produced 52 KMT seats, 51 DPP seats, eight TPP seats and two independents in the 113-seat Legislative Yuan.

No party therefore won an outright legislative majority.

This divided government has had material policy consequences. Budget approval, constitutional procedure, defense spending and the allocation of fiscal resources have become arenas of institutional conflict.

The 2026 central-government budget was approved only after a long delay. That delay affected planned defense procurement and illustrates a broader point: Taiwan's security capacity depends not only on military technology but on ordinary democratic budget institutions.

## Constitutional conflict is part of the current political economy

Taiwan's Constitutional Court invalidated amendments to the Constitutional Court Procedure Act in December 2025, finding serious procedural defects and violations of separation of powers.

The dispute emerged after legislative amendments changed quorum and voting requirements and after repeated failures to confirm judicial nominees left the Court operating with fewer justices than normal.

The episode demonstrates institutional stress but also constitutional adjudication functioning as a constraint within the political system.

It would be analytically wrong to treat every inter-branch dispute as democratic breakdown. It would be equally wrong to dismiss prolonged institutional deadlock as irrelevant. Budget execution, energy law, industrial subsidies and defense procurement can all be slowed by conflict between branches.

## Political identity has changed over three decades

Survey data from National Chengchi University's Election Study Center show a long-run rise in the share of respondents identifying as Taiwanese rather than exclusively Chinese, while combined identities remain significant and exclusive Chinese identity is much smaller than in the early 1990s.

The same research center tracks preferences on independence, unification and maintenance of the status quo. Those categories are more complex than a binary choice: large shares of respondents have historically preferred versions of maintaining the status quo, either indefinitely or with a future decision deferred.

These surveys should not be converted into a prediction of constitutional change.

Their significance is that the social identity environment in which cross-Strait policy operates is materially different from the one that existed during the early democratization period.

## The sovereignty dispute has several distinct layers

Four layers should be separated.

**Effective administration:** Taipei exercises day-to-day governmental control over Taiwan, Penghu, Kinmen, Matsu and associated islands. Beijing does not administer those territories.

**PRC position:** the PRC states that Taiwan is part of China, that the government in Beijing is the sole legal government of China and that national reunification is an internal matter. The Anti-Secession Law authorizes "non-peaceful means" under specified conditions if possibilities for peaceful reunification are deemed exhausted.

**Taipei's current position:** the Lai administration states that the ROC and PRC are not subordinate to each other, that it seeks to maintain peace and the cross-Strait status quo, and that Taiwan's future must be determined by its people.

**International diplomatic practice:** most countries recognize the PRC diplomatically and do not maintain formal diplomatic relations with Taiwan, while many maintain unofficial economic, cultural and security relationships with Taipei. Taiwan participates in the WTO as the Separate Customs Territory of Taiwan, Penghu, Kinmen and Matsu and in APEC as Chinese Taipei.

These layers overlap but are not identical.

## UN General Assembly Resolution 2758 resolved representation of China; its implications for Taiwan are contested

In 1971, UN General Assembly Resolution 2758 recognized representatives of the PRC as the only lawful representatives of China at the United Nations and expelled the representatives of Chiang Kai-shek.

The text does not mention Taiwan by name.

The PRC argues that the resolution resolved China's representation in a way that confirms the one-China principle and precludes separate Taiwan representation in the UN system.

Taiwan's government argues that the resolution addressed only the representation of China and did not determine Taiwan's sovereignty or authorize the PRC to represent Taiwan's population. The United States has also publicly rejected the PRC interpretation that Resolution 2758 establishes an international consensus on Taiwan's ultimate political status.

The report does not adjudicate between these legal-political claims. It distinguishes the text of the resolution from later state interpretations of its consequences.

## Cross-Strait economic integration is being restructured rather than eliminated

Taiwanese manufacturers invested heavily in mainland China from the 1990s onward, particularly in electronics assembly, components and export production.

Direct transport, tourism and trade expanded substantially during the Ma Ying-jeou administration from 2008 to 2016. The 2010 Economic Cooperation Framework Agreement reduced tariffs on selected products and symbolized deeper economic engagement.

Since then, several forces have changed the model: rising Chinese wages, mainland industrial upgrading, U.S.-China tariffs, technology controls, pandemic disruption and Taiwanese policies supporting reshoring or diversification.

Taiwan's Ministry of Economic Affairs reports that the China/Hong Kong share of exports fell to 26.6 percent in 2025 while the U.S. share rose to 31 percent.

This is a large shift, but China remains economically important.

## ECFA has become both a commercial arrangement and a political instrument

The ECFA remains formally important but preferential treatment for some products has been suspended by Beijing.

Taiwan's Mainland Affairs Council characterizes these suspensions as political economic coercion and argues for reducing excessive dependence on mainland markets.

Beijing states that economic measures are responses to what it describes as separatist policies and continues to promote selected forms of economic and social integration, including exchanges with groups and localities it considers supportive of closer relations.

Both governments therefore use economic policy as part of broader cross-Strait strategy.

For firms, the relevant issue is less ideological: tariff preferences, market access, customs treatment and political risk affect where factories and supply chains are located.

## Military pressure is persistent but variable rather than a continuous invasion scenario

Taiwan's Ministry of National Defense publishes daily counts of People's Liberation Army aircraft and naval vessels operating around Taiwan.

Activity varies significantly. For example, Taiwan reported 28 PLA aircraft and seven PLAN ships in the 24-hour period ending 18 September 2026, with 24 aircraft crossing the Taiwan Strait median line or entering portions of Taiwan's ADIZ. Other days in the same month recorded much lower aircraft counts.

The PRC Ministry of National Defense states that PLA operations around Taiwan are lawful activities within Chinese sovereignty claims and are intended to oppose "Taiwan independence" and external interference. Taipei describes many such operations as gray-zone pressure or harassment.

These descriptions reflect opposing political interpretations. The observable fact is that military air and naval activity around Taiwan has become routine and can surge rapidly.

```flow
Routine PLA air/naval activity and periodic large exercises → Taiwan surveillance and readiness costs → defense procurement and civil resilience investment → stronger U.S./partner security cooperation → Beijing cites external interference and separatism → continued military pressure
```

This feedback loop increases crisis-management complexity even when neither side is conducting a declared war operation.

## A blockade scenario is economically distinct from an amphibious invasion

Public debate often treats all Taiwan conflict scenarios as one event. They differ materially.

A full amphibious invasion would require large-scale joint operations across the Strait.

A blockade or quarantine-like operation would focus on shipping, ports, airspace and inspection or exclusion zones.

Missile and air strikes could target military or infrastructure nodes without an immediate landing.

Cyberattacks, cable disruption, disinformation, coast-guard pressure and economic sanctions can be used below the threshold of major kinetic war.

Taiwan's 2025 National Defense Report explicitly analyzes joint blockade, firepower-strike and amphibious scenarios. PRC official statements reject Taiwan's characterization and frame military activity as exercises within China's sovereignty.

For economic analysis, blockade-type disruption is particularly important because Taiwan depends on seaborne energy and trade even if industrial facilities remain physically intact.

## Taiwan is raising defense spending and emphasizing asymmetric systems

Taiwan's Ministry of National Defense calculates 2026 defense-related spending at NT$949.5 billion under a NATO-style definition, including selected coast-guard and veterans expenditures, equivalent to 3.32 percent of GDP.

The core MND annual budget was set at NT$561.4 billion, with additional funds and special budgets supporting fighter aircraft, maritime capability, resilience and asymmetric systems.

The 2026 general budget was delayed in the legislature and passed later in the year, demonstrating the gap between planned and executable defense resources.

Policy emphasizes mobile missiles, drones, air defense, command resilience, ammunition, survivable communications and civil resilience alongside conventional aircraft and ships.

The term "asymmetric" should not imply that inexpensive systems replace all conventional forces. Air defense, naval control, logistics and reserve mobilization remain capital-intensive.

## Conscription reform expands manpower but does not solve the demographic problem

Taiwan restored one-year compulsory military service for eligible men beginning in 2024 after years of shorter service.

The reform increases the pool of trained personnel and is part of a broader reserve and mobilization effort.

But the number of military-age citizens will decline as low fertility works through the population structure.

Defense manpower therefore competes with industry, healthcare and technology for younger workers. Better training, reserve organization, automation and professional retention will become more important as demographic pressure increases.

## Civil resilience has become part of defense policy

Taiwan's security planning increasingly includes communications redundancy, energy, food, medical capacity, emergency response, cybersecurity and local civil-defense preparation.

This reflects the nature of potential disruption. A modern economy can lose critical functionality through power outages, port closures, data-center failure, undersea cable cuts or payment-system disruption without territorial occupation.

The 2026 Han Kuang exercise included scenarios involving communications disruption and degraded digital services.

Civil resilience also has peacetime value because the same systems are used during earthquakes and typhoons.

## The United States is Taiwan's most important security relationship but not a formal diplomatic ally

The United States ended formal diplomatic relations with the ROC and recognized the PRC in 1979.

It maintains extensive unofficial relations with Taiwan through the American Institute in Taiwan. The Taiwan Relations Act provides the domestic legal framework for those relations and states U.S. policy regarding Taiwan's defensive capacity and opposition to coercive changes.

Washington's "one China" policy is not identical to Beijing's "one China principle." The United States recognizes the PRC as the sole legal government of China and acknowledges the Chinese position regarding Taiwan while maintaining unofficial relations with Taipei.

U.S. administrations have generally opposed unilateral changes to the status quo and supported peaceful resolution of cross-Strait differences.

Arms sales, training, technology controls, investment and semiconductor policy increasingly connect economic and security relations.

## Japan's interest is geographic as well as economic

Taiwan lies close to Japan's southwestern islands and near sea lanes important to Japanese trade and energy imports.

Japan also depends on Taiwanese semiconductor production and has supported new TSMC/JASM capacity in Kumamoto.

Japanese governments increasingly refer to peace and stability in the Taiwan Strait as important to regional and international security while maintaining diplomatic relations with the PRC and unofficial relations with Taiwan.

The geography of the Ryukyu island chain means that a major Taiwan Strait crisis would be difficult to isolate from Japanese airspace, shipping and U.S. bases in Japan.

This does not determine how Japan would act in a specific crisis; it explains why Taiwan is a recurring issue in Japanese security planning.

## Europe matters more in technology than in direct security capability

The European Union and individual European states maintain unofficial relations with Taiwan within their respective one-China policies.

Europe is commercially important to Taiwan and occupies critical positions in semiconductor equipment, automotive demand and investment.

ASML's lithography systems are indispensable inputs to leading-edge fabrication, subject to Dutch and multinational export-control frameworks.

TSMC's Dresden investment adds a direct industrial connection focused on automotive and industrial chips.

European governments also increasingly issue statements supporting peace and stability in the Taiwan Strait, but their military role remains far smaller than that of the United States and regional actors.

## Southeast Asia is becoming a larger production and diversification partner

Taiwanese firms have expanded production in Vietnam, Thailand, Malaysia and other Southeast Asian economies.

The drivers include labor costs, customer diversification, tariffs, geopolitical risk and the need to locate assembly closer to final markets.

ASEAN accounted for more than 15 percent of Taiwan's exports in recent years and is a major destination for electronic components and ICT goods.

This creates a more distributed electronics system: advanced chips and servers may be produced in Taiwan while assembly, components and customer-specific production move across Southeast Asia.

The result is not simply "friend-shoring." It is a commercial re-optimization of production under geopolitical constraints.

## Taiwan's industrial policy is moving from semiconductors toward AI systems

The next phase of industrial policy aims to extend Taiwan's semiconductor advantage into AI servers, data centers, robotics, communications and software.

MOEA data show that information and audiovisual products overtook electronic components as Taiwan's largest export category in 2025, driven heavily by AI servers and related systems.

This matters because value capture broadens beyond wafer fabrication.

If Taiwan can integrate chips, servers, networking, power electronics and AI software, it can reduce the risk of remaining only a manufacturing node in a value chain whose highest margins accrue elsewhere.

But AI infrastructure increases domestic electricity and land demand and may intensify labor competition.

## The economy has a concentration problem even when the concentration is globally competitive

High specialization can be a rational source of productivity.

The problem emerges when macroeconomic performance, exports, capital expenditure, tax revenue and labor demand become highly correlated with the same technology cycle.

Taiwan's 2025–2026 acceleration is heavily dependent on AI and HPC demand. That creates upside when global capital expenditure rises but amplifies cyclicality if cloud providers or chip customers reduce orders.

The correct diversification question is therefore not whether Taiwan should weaken semiconductors. It is whether other sectors can become productive enough that national growth is less synchronized with one technology cycle.

## Services productivity is the domestic counterpart to semiconductor strength

Taiwan's manufacturing productivity and export performance are globally competitive, but many domestic service sectors have lower productivity.

Aging increases demand for healthcare, care services, finance and local services precisely when the working-age population is shrinking.

Productivity gains outside manufacturing are therefore essential to maintaining living standards.

Digitalization, AI adoption, urban reform and better capital allocation can matter as much to long-run welfare as another semiconductor fab.

This is one reason GDP growth alone is an incomplete measure of Taiwan's structural success.

## Indigenous history and multiple migrations complicate simple national narratives

Taiwan was inhabited by Austronesian Indigenous peoples long before large-scale Han migration.

Dutch, Spanish, Qing, Japanese and ROC administrations successively governed parts or all of the island across different periods.

Large-scale Han migration from Fujian and Guangdong created Hoklo and Hakka communities. Japanese colonial rule from 1895 to 1945 transformed infrastructure, administration, education and agriculture while imposing imperial political control.

After 1945, ROC rule introduced another major political and demographic transition, including postwar migration from mainland China.

These layered histories are relevant because language, identity, land, transitional justice and political memory remain part of contemporary society.

## The 1947 violence and martial-law period remain politically relevant through transitional justice

The February 28 Incident of 1947 began after a confrontation involving state agents and expanded into island-wide unrest and violent suppression by ROC authorities.

The subsequent authoritarian period, commonly associated with the White Terror, involved political imprisonment, executions and restrictions on opposition.

Democratization created institutions for compensation, archival work and transitional justice.

Historical interpretation remains politically contested in some details, but the existence of state repression during the authoritarian period is well documented and formally acknowledged by Taiwan's democratic institutions.

This history helps explain why constitutional rights, civilian control and democratic identity feature strongly in contemporary political discourse.

## Taiwan's economic development combined land reform, U.S. support and export industrialization

Postwar economic development preceded semiconductors.

Land reform changed rural property relations. U.S. aid provided finance, technology and macroeconomic support in the 1950s and 1960s. The state promoted infrastructure, education and export-processing zones.

Small and medium-sized manufacturers integrated into global production networks. Flexible subcontracting became a distinctive advantage in electronics and machinery.

As wages rose, Taiwan moved toward more capital- and technology-intensive production.

Semiconductors therefore emerged from an existing export-manufacturing base rather than replacing an agrarian economy in one step.

## The New Taiwan dollar and capital controls evolved with export development

Taiwan historically managed capital flows and the exchange rate closely during industrialization.

Financial liberalization gradually expanded, but the central bank still intervenes to smooth volatile capital flows.

Large trade surpluses create persistent appreciation pressure while exporters value exchange-rate stability.

This creates policy tension. A stronger currency can raise household purchasing power and reduce imported inflation but compress exporters' NT-dollar revenue. A weaker currency supports exporters but can raise import and energy costs.

The central bank's policy is therefore embedded in the industrial structure, not only inflation targeting.

## Semiconductor success also creates environmental and land-allocation trade-offs

Advanced fabs use large amounts of electricity, water, industrial land and construction capacity.

Local communities may value jobs and tax revenue while opposing land acquisition, water allocation or environmental impacts.

Industrial concentration in western Taiwan also competes with housing and other infrastructure demand.

These trade-offs will intensify as 2-nanometer, advanced packaging and AI data-center investment expand simultaneously.

The relevant question is not whether fabs are "good" or "bad." It is how infrastructure costs and environmental externalities are allocated and whether public planning keeps pace with private investment.

## Taiwan's position in global technology controls is structurally sensitive

Taiwanese firms operate within U.S., Taiwanese, Dutch, Japanese and other export-control regimes.

Restrictions on the shipment of advanced chips or manufacturing equipment to selected Chinese entities affect Taiwanese firms because mainland China remains a major market.

This produces a strategic constraint: the semiconductor ecosystem benefits from access to U.S. design tools, Dutch lithography and Japanese materials, while commercial demand also exists in China.

Technology controls therefore change customer mix, product design, compliance costs and investment location.

Taiwan has limited ability to remain neutral when suppliers and customers are separated by regulatory regimes.

## A severe Strait crisis would transmit globally through multiple channels

The semiconductor channel receives most attention, but the transmission would be broader.

```flow
Taiwan Strait crisis → shipping/insurance disruption → energy and raw-material constraints in Taiwan → industrial output declines
Taiwan Strait crisis → semiconductor supply shock → global electronics / AI / autos / machinery disruption
Taiwan Strait crisis → financial risk repricing → Asian FX/equities/credit stress
Taiwan Strait crisis → sanctions/export controls → U.S.-China trade contraction and supply-chain fragmentation
Taiwan Strait crisis → aviation and regional military activity → Japan/Philippines logistics and tourism effects
```

This does not mean a conflict is inevitable or that a precise global GDP loss can be inferred. It explains why Taiwan Strait stability has become a macroeconomic variable rather than only a bilateral political question.

## The strongest evidence supports resilience and vulnerability at the same time

| Proposition | Evidence supporting it | Evidence limiting it | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Taiwan's technology position is difficult to substitute quickly | dense cluster, TSMC leading-edge ramp, advanced packaging and supplier ecosystem | U.S., Japan and Europe are expanding capacity and subsidies | strong near-term concentration, gradual geographic diversification |
| Export dependence on China has fallen | U.S. reached ~31% of exports in 2025; China/HK fell to 26.6% | mainland China remains a major market and production base | substantial rebalancing, not decoupling |
| AI is producing exceptional growth | 2025 growth 8.76%; 2026 forecast 11.05%; export surge | cycle is concentrated and investment-led | strong current expansion, high cyclical sensitivity |
| External finances are resilient | >US$600bn reserves; huge current-account surplus | reserves do not remove physical trade/energy exposure | high financial resilience |
| Energy is a critical vulnerability | heavy reliance on imported LNG/coal and maritime routes | route diversity, storage, renewables and grid investment reduce some risk | persistent structural constraint |
| Demography is deteriorating | fertility 0.695 in 2025; 65+ share 20.9% in 2026 | migration, automation and productivity can mitigate labor scarcity | high-confidence long-run pressure |
| Democratic institutions remain functional | competitive elections, alternation, court review, public contestation | divided government and constitutional/budget disputes slow policy | resilience with rising institutional friction |
| Semiconductor importance deters conflict | disruption would impose major global costs | strategic value cannot guarantee political or military behavior | plausible incentive, not a security guarantee |
| Defense capacity is increasing | higher budgets, asymmetric systems, longer conscription, resilience planning | procurement delays, manpower constraints and political budget conflict | improving but implementation-dependent |
| Cross-Strait economic links are declining | export and investment diversification | ECFA, trade and corporate networks remain significant | structural diversification, continuing interdependence |

## A technology-led resilience path would deepen diversification without hollowing out the domestic cluster

Under one structural path, Taiwan continues to keep leading-edge R&D, advanced nodes and packaging domestically while overseas fabs provide additional capacity near key customers.

Energy and grid investment keep pace with AI and semiconductor demand. Skilled immigration and automation partially offset demographics. Private services productivity improves.

Cross-Strait trade remains significant but a smaller share of total exposure.

This path does not require Taiwan to become economically independent of China or militarily self-sufficient. It requires multiple dependencies to become less concentrated.

## A distributed semiconductor system could reduce Taiwan's singularity while preserving its leadership

A second path involves faster replication of advanced manufacturing in the United States, Japan and Europe.

Taiwan remains the largest and most capable cluster but the marginal strategic cost of disruption falls as customers gain alternatives.

This could reduce one component of Taiwan's strategic leverage while also reducing pressure on domestic land, water and power.

The effect on security is ambiguous. Less production concentration could reduce global economic exposure to a Strait crisis, but it could also reduce the costs Taiwan itself faces from single-location concentration.

## A prolonged political-security freeze could preserve the status quo without resolving underlying tensions

Cross-Strait political dialogue can remain limited while trade continues and military activity remains persistent but below the threshold of conflict.

Taiwan continues defense investment and supply-chain diversification. Beijing continues military, diplomatic and economic pressure while also offering selected exchange and integration measures.

The result would be a costly but stable-enough equilibrium.

Its weakness is cumulative normalization of military operations around Taiwan, which can reduce warning time and increase the risk of miscalculation.

## A coercive crisis would test physical resilience before macro-financial resilience

A quarantine, blockade-like episode, major exercise with shipping restrictions, cyberattack or infrastructure disruption could impose costs without immediate amphibious warfare.

Taiwan's large reserves would support the financial system but could not generate LNG, spare parts or shipping access.

The binding constraints would move from foreign exchange to inventories, ports, electricity, communications and logistics.

This is why civil resilience and energy policy are now security variables.

## What would materially change the structural assessment

The assessment would strengthen on economic resilience if Taiwan:

- sustains high national value added in semiconductors while overseas capacity expands;
- increases renewable generation, storage, LNG inventory and grid redundancy faster than industrial demand;
- reduces the China/Hong Kong share of trade without losing overall export competitiveness;
- raises services productivity and real household income outside the technology corridor;
- stabilizes fertility or attracts substantially more skilled migrants;
- improves budget execution and defense procurement across divided government.

It would weaken if:

- AI export growth reverses without replacement demand;
- leading-edge investment shifts abroad faster than the domestic ecosystem upgrades;
- power or water constraints delay major industrial projects;
- demographic decline creates persistent engineering, healthcare or military manpower shortages;
- cross-Strait military activity materially reduces shipping or investor confidence;
- domestic political conflict prevents implementation of core energy, fiscal or defense programs.

## Evidence limits are part of the conclusion

Taiwan has unusually strong public economic statistics, corporate disclosure and electoral data, so the economic side of this assessment has higher evidentiary quality than many geopolitical country studies.

The principal uncertainty lies in security intentions and crisis behavior. Military activity can be observed; political intention cannot be inferred mechanically from sortie counts. PRC, Taiwan, U.S. and allied statements are strategic communications as well as policy signals.

The legal and diplomatic status of Taiwan is contested. The report therefore distinguishes effective administration, constitutional claims, diplomatic recognition and institutional participation rather than collapsing them into one category.

Semiconductor capacity can be measured more precisely than "strategic leverage." Production concentration is observable; its effect on deterrence is inferential.

Long-run demographic projections are not forecasts with certainty. They are conditional projections based on fertility, mortality and migration assumptions, though the direction of aging and population decline is already well established.
