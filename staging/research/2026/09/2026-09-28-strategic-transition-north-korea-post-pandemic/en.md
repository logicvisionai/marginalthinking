# North Korea's post-pandemic transition: state re-centralization, military-industrial expansion and external reorientation

The Democratic People's Republic of Korea (DPRK) is undergoing a structural transition that cannot be described adequately as either simple economic recovery or simple isolation. The most defensible interpretation of the 2023–2026 evidence is that the country has moved out of the exceptional pandemic-border-closure shock into a new equilibrium characterized by five simultaneous changes: recovery concentrated in the state-directed economy; stronger administrative control over trade and distribution; continued reliance on markets and informal exchange at household level; a more important military-industrial relationship with the Russian Federation; and an institutional shift away from the previous inter-Korean reunification framework toward a more explicit state-to-state confrontation with the Republic of Korea (ROK).

This assessment does not assume that official DPRK claims are accurate, nor does it treat external estimates from the ROK, the United States, United Nations bodies or research institutes as direct measurements inside the country. North Korea is among the most difficult large political economies in the world to measure. Since early 2021, the United Nations Country Team has operated from Bangkok rather than Pyongyang; the IAEA has had no inspectors in the DPRK since 2009; current GDP is not published by DPRK authorities in a form comparable with standard national accounts; market prices are reconstructed from networks of informants and cross-border information; and military, sanctions-evasion and human-rights evidence often depends on satellite imagery, customs mirror data, interviews with people who have left the country, intercepted logistics, official statements and probabilistic attribution.

The research therefore uses an evidence hierarchy rather than a single narrative. Observable customs data, satellite imagery, treaty texts, crop remote sensing and institutional documents receive greater weight for the specific questions they can answer. DPRK state media are used primarily to identify stated priorities, laws and official claims. ROK institutions are used as external estimators and are labeled as such. UN human-rights findings are reported together with their methodology and access constraints. Claims by the DPRK, ROK, United States, Russia, China or Japan about intentions are attributed to those actors rather than converted into facts.

On the economy, the Bank of Korea (BOK) estimates real DPRK GDP grew 3.5 percent in 2025 after 3.7 percent in 2024 and 3.1 percent in 2023, following the severe 2020 contraction associated with border closure and pandemic controls. Manufacturing, construction and agriculture contributed to the 2025 estimate. BOK also estimates external merchandise trade, excluding inter-Korean trade, rose to US$3.13 billion in 2025 from US$2.70 billion in 2024. These are not DPRK official national accounts. BOK states explicitly that it estimates North Korean output from production quantities supplied by relevant institutions and values it using South Korean prices and value-added ratios. The numbers are therefore best interpreted as a consistent external model of direction and sectoral change, not precise measurement of household income or the informal economy.

China remains the dominant observable trade partner. KDI estimates that China accounts for roughly 95 percent of North Korea's recorded external trade, supplying industrial inputs, fuel-related products, textiles, chemicals, machinery and consumer goods. Chinese customs recorded approximately US$1.37 billion of exports to the DPRK and US$279 million of imports from the DPRK in the first eight months of 2025, for total recorded bilateral merchandise trade of about US$1.65 billion. At the same time, Russian ties have changed in quality. The 2024 Russia-DPRK Comprehensive Strategic Partnership Treaty deepened security cooperation, and Russian authorities publicly acknowledged DPRK military personnel participating in operations in the Kursk region. This relationship creates channels for foreign currency, logistics, technology, goods and military-industrial exchange that are not fully visible in ordinary merchandise trade statistics.

Domestic economic governance appears to be moving toward stronger state direction after the more permissive market accommodation that evolved from the 1990s through parts of the Kim Jong Un period. The 2026 Ninth Workers' Party Congress and subsequent state sessions emphasized stabilization, consolidation, industrial upgrading, regional development, basic industries, artificial intelligence and continued defense modernization. External analysts at KDI and 38 North interpret recent policy as stronger central management of trade and distribution without eliminating market mechanisms. This distinction matters: North Korea is not returning to the pre-1990s economy, because household survival, retail distribution, enterprise incentives and foreign-currency use have been reshaped by three decades of marketization. The transition is better described as **re-centralization over a marketized social base**.

The social consequences are harder to measure than industrial output. FAO remote-sensing analysis found generally favorable conditions for the 2026 main-season crops, but lack of regular in-country UN access prevents a comprehensive current assessment of household food consumption. Historical evidence shows why the distinction between harvests and access matters. The 1990s food crisis combined the collapse of external trade and energy supply, severe agricultural input shortages, institutional breakdown in the public distribution system and repeated floods and drought. Markets then expanded partly as coping institutions. A favorable crop year in 2026 therefore does not establish food security, just as market activity does not demonstrate equal purchasing power.

Human-rights evidence requires the same discipline. OHCHR's 2025 ten-year assessment drew on 314 interviews with victims and witnesses who had left the country, UN and civil-society material and other corroborating information. The DPRK rejected the Human Rights Council mandate but referred OHCHR to its Universal Periodic Review submissions. The country participated in the 2024 UPR, accepted 143 recommendations and has engaged with the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities. OHCHR nevertheless assessed the overall rights situation as showing no broad improvement and reported stronger surveillance and restrictions in several areas. Because OHCHR lacks unrestricted current in-country access, these findings should be treated as a structured body of externally gathered evidence with identifiable limitations—not dismissed, and not represented as if they came from a comprehensive domestic census.

On security, the evidence is stronger that North Korea's nuclear and missile capabilities have continued to expand than it is about exact arsenal size. The IAEA has had no verification access since April 2009 and therefore cannot draw safeguards conclusions based on inspections. Its 2025 safeguards report, using satellite imagery, open sources and DPRK state-media imagery, identified continued operation-related indications at Yongbyon and Kangson uranium-enrichment facilities and new construction. The exact quantity and configuration of nuclear material remains uncertain. The DPRK describes nuclear deterrence as necessary for state security; the UN Security Council resolutions prohibit its nuclear-weapons and ballistic-missile programs and maintain legally binding sanctions. These are competing legal-strategic positions that should not be conflated with empirical measurements of capability.

The central assessment is **medium confidence**. North Korea appears more institutionally stable and economically active than during the 2020–2022 border-closure shock. The state has greater access to foreign trade, larger construction and industrial programs, a stronger relationship with Russia and a restored flow of commerce with China. At the same time, stronger central control, opaque statistics, sanctions, weak energy availability, agricultural constraints, restricted international access and the heavy resource requirements of defense limit the extent to which official-sector growth can be assumed to translate into broad improvements in household welfare.

The key transition is therefore not "collapse versus survival." It is whether the DPRK can sustain a hybrid model in which the state recentralizes strategic allocation and trade while retaining enough market flexibility to supply households, finance enterprises and generate incentives—and whether closer relations with Russia and China can provide inputs and foreign currency without producing a new form of external dependency.

**Information cutoff: 28 September 2026.**

## Evidence limits what can be stated with confidence

North Korea cannot be assessed with the same evidentiary precision as economies with open statistical systems and unrestricted field access. Formal laws, treaties and official statements establish institutional design and declared policy, but not implementation by themselves. Chinese customs and other mirror statistics capture observable merchandise trade but omit undeclared, military and informal flows. Bank of Korea estimates provide a consistent external model of output direction and sectoral change, while satellite monitoring, FAO observations, UN documentation and interview-based evidence answer narrower questions with their own access and selection limits.

Claims in this report are therefore tied to the type of evidence that can sustain them. Official positions are attributed to the actors making them; externally modeled output is identified as an estimate; satellite-observed activity is not converted into an exact inventory; and evidence about household conditions or rights is presented with the access limits of the collecting institution. Where independent verification is not possible, the uncertainty is part of the finding rather than something hidden behind a single point estimate.

## Geography makes energy, transport and security inseparable

North Korea occupies the northern half of a mountainous peninsula between China, Russia and the Republic of Korea, with coastlines on the Yellow Sea and Sea of Japan/East Sea. Mountainous terrain limits arable land, concentrates transport corridors and raises the cost of internal logistics. Most population, agriculture and industry are concentrated in western plains, river valleys and urban-industrial corridors, while mineral and hydropower resources are distributed more broadly.

The Yalu/Amnok and Tumen rivers form much of the northern boundary with China and Russia. These borders are economic interfaces as much as security lines. Sinuiju-Dandong is the most important conventional trade corridor with China. The Tumen corridor connects the DPRK to northeastern China and Russia. Rajin/Rason provides an ice-free port with strategic value for regional logistics.

To the south, the Demilitarized Zone is not a commercial border in the ordinary sense. Inter-Korean trade has fallen to effectively zero in recent years. This places disproportionate weight on northern trade routes.

Energy geography is equally consequential. The DPRK has substantial coal and hydropower resources but limited domestic oil production. Thermal power, hydropower, aging grids and imported petroleum products form an energy system vulnerable to weather, infrastructure quality and external supply. Electricity shortages constrain factories, mines, irrigation, rail and household consumption simultaneously.

```map
title: North Korea's structural economic geography
Pyongyang and western industrial belt | Administration, manufacturing, construction and demand concentration | State investment and grid reliability shape output
Sinuiju-Dandong corridor | Principal observable trade interface with China | Customs throughput and border policy transmit into domestic prices
Northeastern industrial/mining belt | Minerals, metals, chemicals and ports | Energy and transport constraints determine utilization
Rason/Tumen corridor | Access to Russia and northeast China | Strategic logistics value rises with Russia-DPRK cooperation
Agricultural western plains | Rice, maize and food supply | Floods, drought, fertilizer, fuel and irrigation affect yields
DMZ and inter-Korean boundary | Security frontier rather than normal trade corridor | Political relations determine whether southern economic integration exists
```

## State formation: partition, war and reconstruction created the institutional baseline

Korea was annexed by Japan in 1910 and remained under Japanese colonial rule until 1945. At the end of the Second World War, the United States and Soviet Union accepted a temporary division around the 38th parallel for the surrender and administration of Japanese forces. Rival political systems consolidated in the two occupation zones. The Democratic People's Republic of Korea was proclaimed in the north in 1948 under Kim Il Sung; the Republic of Korea had been established in the south.

In June 1950, North Korean forces crossed the 38th parallel and the Korean War began. United Nations-authorized forces led by the United States intervened on the ROK side; the People's Republic of China later entered the war on the DPRK side, while the Soviet Union supplied significant support. The fighting produced enormous military and civilian losses and extensive destruction across the peninsula.

The 1953 Armistice stopped major combat but did not create a comprehensive peace treaty. That institutional fact continues to matter. North Korean security policy developed in a technically unresolved war environment with U.S. forces remaining in the ROK and a U.S.-ROK alliance, while the DPRK built security ties first with the Soviet Union and China and later pursued a more autonomous strategy.

War destruction also shaped postwar economic organization. Central planning, state ownership, industrial reconstruction and mobilization were not abstract ideological choices alone; they were mechanisms for rebuilding a heavily damaged industrial system under Cold War conditions.

## Postwar industrialization initially delivered significant physical reconstruction

In the 1950s and 1960s, the DPRK rebuilt industry with significant Soviet, Chinese and Eastern European assistance. The northern half of the peninsula had inherited much of colonial-era heavy industry, hydroelectric capacity and mineral production. State planning directed investment toward steel, machinery, chemicals, mining, electricity and defense.

Available historical estimates are incomplete and often politically contested, but most economic histories agree that North Korea's industrial performance in the first postwar decades compared more favorably with the South than later outcomes would suggest. The divergence became much larger from the 1970s onward as South Korea integrated deeply into global manufacturing and export markets while the DPRK accumulated external debt, faced technology and energy constraints and retained a more closed production system.

Juche evolved into the state's central ideological framework, emphasizing political independence, economic self-reliance and military autonomy. Self-reliance never meant literal autarky. The DPRK imported oil, technology, machinery and inputs and depended materially on socialist trade relationships. The gap between ideological self-reliance and physical external dependence became increasingly important when those trade relationships changed.

## Political authority became increasingly concentrated and succession became dynastic in practice

Kim Il Sung remained the central political figure from state founding until his death in 1994. Kim Jong Il then became the dominant leader, and Kim Jong Un succeeded him after Kim Jong Il's death in 2011. This three-generation succession is a de facto dynastic pattern, although the constitution does not describe the leadership office as legally hereditary.

The DPRK is a party-state. Current constitutional texts released outside the DPRK continue to state that state activities are conducted under the leadership of the Workers' Party of Korea. The Supreme People's Assembly is formally the highest organ of state power and adopts laws, budgets and plans; the Cabinet administers the economy; the State Affairs Commission is a central leadership organ; and the WPK establishes policy direction through its congresses, central committee, Politburo, secretariat and specialized departments.

A constitutional text released by the ROK Ministry of Unification in May 2026, incorporating DPRK amendments through March, indicates expanded formal powers for the President/Chairman of the State Affairs Commission, including authority over senior appointments and nuclear command. Because the DPRK has not made an independently authenticated full English version readily available through normal international legal databases, exact translations and amendment chronology should be treated carefully. The direction of concentration is nevertheless consistent with DPRK public reporting on the 2026 Ninth Party Congress and Supreme People's Assembly.

Institutional concentration can create execution capacity in priority programs. It can also reduce independent feedback and make policy errors more costly when lower-level actors have incentives to report compliance rather than failure. This is an organizational mechanism, not a judgment about ideology.

## The 1990s crisis was a system shock, not a single-cause famine

The collapse of the Soviet Union removed preferential trade, fuel and other forms of economic support. The DPRK's ability to import petroleum, fertilizer, machinery and food deteriorated sharply. Industrial output fell, electricity shortages worsened and agricultural mechanization and irrigation suffered.

Natural disasters compounded the shock. FAO/WFP missions documented extensive floods in 1995 and 1996 and severe drought in 1997. They also documented shortages of fuel, fertilizer, spare parts and foreign exchange, falling agricultural productivity and severe stress in the Public Distribution System.

The correct causal description is therefore multi-factor:

```flow
Collapse of Soviet-linked trade and finance → fuel/input shortages → industrial and electricity contraction → weaker fertilizer, irrigation and mechanization
+
Floods/drought and limited arable land → lower harvests
+
Reduced foreign exchange → lower commercial food imports
→ Public Distribution System stress → household food-access crisis → survival strategies and market expansion
```

This matters for contemporary analysis because it shows that North Korean food security depends on more than harvest volume. Energy, transport, foreign exchange, distribution institutions and household purchasing power interact.

## Markets emerged because households and enterprises needed a second allocation system

During the 1990s crisis, state distribution could not fully supply households. Informal and semi-formal markets expanded as people traded food, household goods and services. Cross-border commerce with China grew in importance. Women became particularly important participants in retail market activity, in part because many men remained attached to formal workplaces while household income increasingly depended on trade.

The state subsequently alternated between legalization, regulation, taxation, restriction and attempts to reassert formal control. The July 2002 economic-management measures adjusted prices and wages and gave some enterprises greater operating flexibility. The 2009 currency reform attempted to redenominate currency and restrict private wealth holdings; external research and defector testimony widely associate it with severe disruption and loss of confidence in the domestic currency.

Under Kim Jong Un, economic management evolved further. Enterprises, farms and local units received varying degrees of practical discretion while markets continued. Foreign currency became important in pricing and saving. Private capital often operated through formally state-owned enterprises or partnerships with officials rather than through a legally independent private corporate sector.

This produced a dual or hybrid economy: formal state plans and priority allocation coexist with markets that perform price discovery, retail distribution, household income generation and some enterprise financing.

## Re-centralization does not mean de-marketization

The post-2020 period appears to have shifted the balance toward greater state control. Pandemic border closure dramatically reduced trade, movement and informal cross-border channels. Authorities strengthened rules governing trade, grain distribution, foreign currency and market activity. External analysts report tighter control over wholesale commerce and attempts to route more trade through state institutions.

The crucial point is that three decades of marketization cannot be removed simply by administrative decision. Households still require markets where state distribution is incomplete. Enterprises still face shortages and need mechanisms to obtain inputs. Foreign currencies remain economically important because they connect domestic actors to imported goods and preserve value when confidence in the won falls.

KDI's 2025/2026 assessments describe a paradox: formal-sector recovery and stronger official trade coexist with market exchange-rate depreciation and inflation. That is consistent with a state capturing a larger share of foreign exchange and imported inputs while household markets face tighter liquidity or supply conditions.

The transition should therefore be modeled as:

```mindmap
North Korea's hybrid political economy
- State allocation
  - strategic industry
  - defense
  - construction
  - public distribution
  - large infrastructure
- Enterprise adaptation
  - plan targets
  - retained earnings
  - informal procurement
  - foreign-currency settlement
- Household markets
  - food and consumer goods
  - small services
  - price discovery
  - household income
- External channels
  - China trade
  - Russia cooperation
  - sanctions-constrained finance
  - maritime and land logistics
```

## The pandemic closure created a natural experiment in external dependence

North Korea responded to COVID-19 with one of the world's strictest border-control regimes. Conventional trade with China collapsed. Diplomatic and international humanitarian presence shrank. The UN Resident Coordinator and country team left in early 2021 and, as of the UN's August 2025 FAQ, had not been invited back to Pyongyang.

BOK estimates real GDP contracted 4.5 percent in 2020 and then remained slightly negative in 2021 and 2022. These figures are model estimates, but the directional story is corroborated by customs data showing the trade collapse and by visible reductions in cross-border activity.

The closure demonstrated that self-reliance did not eliminate external input dependence. A modern industrial economy requires machinery, replacement parts, petroleum products, chemicals, fertilizer inputs, specialty materials and selected consumer goods. When formal trade was compressed, both production and markets faced stress.

Border reopening after 2022 therefore generated a mechanical recovery channel even without fundamental reform.

## The 2023–2025 recovery is real in some observable dimensions but difficult to generalize

BOK estimates three consecutive years of real GDP growth above 3 percent from 2023 through 2025. In 2025 it estimated:

|---|---:|---:|
| Agriculture, forestry and fishing | 3.6% | 21.2% |

The pattern is revealing. Manufacturing and construction grew more rapidly than utilities, suggesting that priority allocation, external inputs and targeted state programs can raise selected output even while the energy system remains constrained.

## External trade has recovered but remains structurally narrow

BOK estimates DPRK merchandise trade, excluding the ROK, at US$3.13 billion in 2025: US$470 million exports and US$2.66 billion imports. The trade deficit is therefore large in recorded merchandise flows.

```chart

2025 imports | 2.66
2025 total trade | 3.13
```

The series does not fully capture military transfers, services, unrecorded transactions, sanctioned trade or transfers associated with military cooperation.

China remains the dominant conventional partner. KDI estimates China accounts for roughly 95 percent of recorded trade. Chinese customs reported in the first eight months of 2025 exports of approximately US$1.37 billion to the DPRK and imports of about US$279 million from the DPRK, for total recorded bilateral merchandise trade of about US$1.65 billion.

That dependence is structural because China supplies many categories North Korea cannot efficiently produce at required quality or scale. It also purchases labor-intensive processed goods and other products permitted under or routed around the constraints of the sanctions environment.

The asymmetry produces leverage but not simple control. China has interests in border stability, non-proliferation, avoiding war, maintaining economic ties and limiting destabilizing pressure. North Korea has repeatedly demonstrated policy autonomy even when economically dependent on China.

## Russia changes the composition of external dependence

The Russia relationship differs from the China relationship. China is the dominant conventional commercial partner; Russia has become a more important security, military-industrial and strategic partner.

Russia and the DPRK signed a Comprehensive Strategic Partnership Treaty in June 2024 and Russia ratified it in November. Russian official statements later acknowledged DPRK military personnel participating in operations in the Kursk region. By 2026, Russia continued publicly praising DPRK military participation and cooperation.

The economic implications are larger than merchandise customs statistics alone. Military cooperation can generate foreign currency, commodity flows, logistics, training and potential technology transfers. The exact value and composition are difficult to verify independently.

UN Security Council sanctions prohibit arms exports from the DPRK and various forms of military cooperation. Russia and the DPRK describe their partnership as lawful strategic cooperation and invoke their treaty and security interests. The legal conflict is not merely rhetorical: the sanctions resolutions remain in force even though the UN Panel of Experts monitoring them lost its mandate in April 2024 after the Security Council failed to renew it because of a Russian veto.

A group of states subsequently created the Multilateral Sanctions Monitoring Team. Its reports are not reports of a UN expert panel; they are evidence produced by participating governments and must be attributed as such.

## Sanctions constrain the economy, but sanctions are not a complete theory of North Korean outcomes

UN sanctions expanded substantially after the DPRK's nuclear tests and ballistic-missile activity. The current regime includes restrictions on arms, coal and other mineral exports, textiles, seafood and other sectors; caps or limits on petroleum products; financial restrictions; limits on joint ventures; and requirements relating to overseas workers. The 1718 Committee also operates humanitarian exemption procedures.

Sanctions matter through several mechanisms:

- reduced legal export earnings;
- higher transaction and insurance costs;
- restricted access to international banking;
- constraints on machinery, transport equipment and dual-use technology;
- risk premiums for counterparties;
- incentives for evasion and opaque intermediaries.

But economic outcomes cannot be attributed to sanctions alone. Domestic allocation, military spending, agricultural policy, exchange-rate management, border closures, investment choices, natural disasters and institutional incentives also matter. The pandemic period is especially important because border controls were imposed by the DPRK itself and generated economic effects separate from international sanctions.

Conversely, the fact that the economy has resumed growth under sanctions does not establish that sanctions have no effect. Growth can occur from a low base, through substitution, evasion, state prioritization and new partners while potential output remains below a counterfactual path.

## The 2026 five-year plan points toward managed modernization rather than liberalization

The Ninth WPK Congress, held 19–25 February 2026, launched a new five-year plan for 2026–2030. A United Nations summary of the Congress describes priorities as stabilization, consolidation and gradual qualitative development, with focus on metallurgy, chemicals, electricity, coal, machinery, agriculture, construction, regional development, information technology and artificial intelligence.

The Regional Development 20×10 Policy aims to build modern industrial facilities in 20 cities or counties each year over a decade and expand hospitals and other facilities outside Pyongyang. DPRK official reporting presents the program as a means of reducing regional disparities and raising living standards.

External analysts offer a more cautious interpretation. 38 North argues that the new plan retains stronger centralized governance while still relying selectively on market mechanisms. KDI emphasizes that formal-sector recovery has not necessarily spread to household markets.

The plan therefore does not resemble conventional market liberalization. Its logic is closer to state-led upgrading: concentrate resources in basic industry, modernize equipment, direct construction, use military and party mobilization where needed, and attempt to improve local production without surrendering political control over allocation.

## Regional Development 20×10 is also a state-capacity experiment

The significance of 20×10 is not only the number of factories. It tests whether the central state can convert cement, steel, labor and local inputs into repeatable local industrial capacity.

The program has potential advantages under North Korean conditions. Construction can rely on domestic materials and mobilized labor. Facilities create visible outputs. Decentralized consumer-goods production can reduce transport burdens. Hospitals and public facilities can address geographic inequality.

The constraints are equally structural. A factory building is not productive capacity unless it receives reliable electricity, raw materials, spare parts, skilled labor, transport and demand. If the state can construct facilities faster than it can supply operating inputs, physical investment may create underutilized capital.

The correct indicators are therefore operating rates, output, product quality, energy consumption and local household access—not completed buildings alone.

## Energy remains one of the hardest physical constraints

North Korea's industrial model is energy-intensive. Mining, metals, chemicals, rail transport and military manufacturing all require dependable electricity and fuel. Yet BOK estimates electricity, gas and water output contracted 0.4 percent in 2025 despite overall GDP growth.

Hydropower provides an important domestic source but exposes generation to rainfall variability and winter conditions. Coal supports thermal generation but requires mines, rail and plant maintenance. Imported petroleum remains constrained by sanctions and foreign-exchange availability.

The grid itself is a bottleneck. Transmission losses, aging equipment and uneven distribution can make nominal generation capacity less useful than nameplate figures suggest. Priority facilities can receive power while households or lower-priority enterprises face shortages.

Night-light satellite imagery is useful for broad spatial patterns but should not be treated as a direct GDP measure. Low light intensity can reflect grid scarcity, conservation, urban morphology and policy decisions; bright military or industrial sites can also distort interpretation.

## Agriculture improved in 2026 weather conditions, but food security cannot be measured from weather alone

FAO's 4 September 2026 GIEWS brief found generally favorable weather for main-season crops. Rice and maize dominate the main season, along with smaller quantities of soybeans, potatoes, millet and sorghum. Remote sensing through mid-August indicated generally good vegetation conditions, while localized flooding affected some western lowlands.

This is evidence about crop conditions, not a complete food-security survey. The absence of international UN staff inside the DPRK since early 2021 limits current household-level assessment.

Food availability depends on:

```flow
Weather + arable land + fertilizer + fuel + irrigation + seed → harvest
Harvest + imports + storage + transport + state procurement → national availability
Availability + public distribution + market supply + household income + geographic access → actual consumption
```

A good harvest can coexist with local shortages if distribution and purchasing power are weak. Conversely, imports and markets can cushion a poor harvest.

## The 1990s legacy makes the public distribution system politically and economically important

Before the 1990s crisis, the Public Distribution System was a central channel for urban food access. FAO/WFP missions documented its severe weakening during the famine period as national supplies fell.

The PDS never disappeared completely, but market purchases became increasingly important. More recent policies seeking greater grain procurement and state commercial control can be understood partly as attempts to recover distribution capacity and reduce dependence on uncontrolled market prices.

The policy trade-off is complex. Stronger procurement can improve state reserves and priority distribution. If procurement prices or quotas reduce farm incentives or crowd out local trade, they can also lower market supply. Without current, independently observed nationwide household data, the net effect cannot be asserted with high confidence.

## Industry: the strategic core is broader than weapons production

North Korean industry includes mining, metals, machinery, chemicals, construction materials, light manufacturing, textiles, processed consumer goods and defense production. The official development strategy emphasizes "self-reliant" basic industry because dependence on imported coking coal, petroleum, chemicals and equipment creates vulnerabilities.

KDI analysis of the 2021–2025 plan notes that some official targets remain incomplete or opaque. For example, authorities continued to emphasize domestic steelmaking and C1 chemistry in the new plan, suggesting technological bottlenecks remain.

The military-industrial sector receives strong political priority and may benefit from Russian demand. But measuring its scale is especially difficult because output is not transparently reported and many inputs overlap with civilian heavy industry.

A rise in heavy-industry production can therefore have multiple explanations: construction, civilian machinery, metals, energy projects, military production or inventory rebuilding. BOK's estimated 7.8 percent growth in heavy and chemical industry in 2025 does not by itself identify the final user.

## Nuclear weapons changed the strategic state but exact capability remains uncertain

North Korea conducted nuclear tests in 2006, 2009, 2013, twice in 2016 and in 2017. The UN Security Council responded with progressively stronger sanctions. The DPRK later incorporated nuclear status and nuclear-force policy more explicitly into domestic law and constitutional arrangements.

The IAEA has not had inspectors in the country since April 2009. Its current assessments rely on commercial satellite imagery, open-source information and DPRK disclosures.

The IAEA's 2025 safeguards report identified indications that the Yongbyon uranium-enrichment facility continued operating, observed continued activity at the Kangson enrichment facility, and documented additional construction. It also reported activity associated with reactors and the radiochemical laboratory. The IAEA explicitly states that, without access, it cannot implement verification measures or draw standard safeguards conclusions.

This creates an important epistemic boundary. It is reasonable to conclude that the DPRK maintains and develops a nuclear fuel-cycle and weapons-related infrastructure. It is not reasonable to present a precise current warhead count as directly verified fact.

## Nuclear doctrine should be analyzed as both capability and signaling

The DPRK presents its nuclear arsenal as a deterrent against perceived external military threats, especially the United States and U.S.-ROK alliance. U.S., ROK and Japanese governments describe the program as a threat to regional and international security and emphasize its violation of Security Council resolutions.

Both positions are strategic claims. The empirical questions are narrower:

- what delivery systems have been demonstrated;
- what nuclear-material production infrastructure is observable;
- what command arrangements are codified;
- what exercises and deployments are visible;
- what crisis scenarios official doctrine discusses;
- how adversaries change their own deployments and exercises in response.

The feedback loop matters:

```flow
DPRK nuclear/missile development → U.S.-ROK-Japan deterrence and defense measures → DPRK perception of stronger external threat → further force development and exercises → higher crisis complexity and miscalculation risk
```

This does not imply moral or causal equivalence. It identifies a strategic interaction in which each side's actions become inputs into the other's threat assessment.

## Conventional forces remain economically consequential

The Korean People's Army is not only a security institution. It absorbs labor, industrial output, construction capacity, logistics and fiscal resources. Military units also participate in civilian construction and disaster response, which blurs the boundary between defense and economic mobilization.

North Korea maintains large conventional forces relative to population. Exact readiness varies by unit and is difficult to observe. Raw personnel or equipment counts therefore overstate what can be inferred about usable combat capability.

Recent overseas operations with Russia create a new variable. Russian official statements confirm DPRK personnel participated in operations in the Kursk region. This may generate combat experience, organizational learning and external income. The scale of technology transfer back to the DPRK is more uncertain and should not be assumed without evidence.

## The inter-Korean framework has changed materially

For decades, both Korean states officially maintained versions of a future-unification framework, even when relations were hostile. From late 2023 onward, DPRK policy shifted toward treating the ROK as a separate hostile state rather than a partner in national reunification.

The 2026 WPK Congress revised party rules, and external analysis of texts released by South Korean institutions indicates removal of previous peaceful-unification language. A 2026 constitutional text released by the ROK government also reportedly formalizes territorial and state distinctions.

The material consequence is greater than rhetoric. Institutions, propaganda, border infrastructure, military planning and diplomacy can all be reorganized around a more permanent two-state assumption.

The change does not mean war is inevitable. It means the cost of restoring earlier inter-Korean institutions rises as legal and organizational structures are dismantled or repurposed.

## The ROK is simultaneously adversary, comparison economy and information source

South Korea has three analytically distinct roles.

First, it is a military and political counterpart tied to the United States.

Second, it is an extraordinarily high-income comparison economy sharing the same peninsula and cultural history. The divergence is useful for institutional and development analysis but can easily become politicized.

Third, ROK agencies are among the largest producers of North Korea data. BOK, KDI, the Ministry of Unification, KINU and intelligence-linked institutions generate estimates and documents unavailable elsewhere.

This creates a source problem. South Korean data should neither be rejected because of political rivalry nor treated as neutral direct measurement. The correct method is to use transparent methodologies, cross-check against Chinese customs, satellite observations, DPRK reporting and independent scholarship, and label uncertainty.

## China prefers influence without assuming complete responsibility for North Korean choices

China is North Korea's most important conventional economic partner and a treaty neighbor. It has supported UN sanctions resolutions in the past while also opposing policies it views as destabilizing or excessively coercive.

China's interests are not reducible to "supporting North Korea." They include preventing war and uncontrolled instability on its border, limiting refugee flows, opposing nuclear proliferation, preserving strategic space on the peninsula, maintaining trade and avoiding a geopolitical outcome dominated by the United States.

North Korea, for its part, has historically resisted subordination to any patron. The relationship therefore contains both dependence and autonomy.

A useful indicator is not simply bilateral meeting frequency but the composition of trade: whether North Korea's imports become more technologically sophisticated, whether export earnings broaden, and whether transport infrastructure deepens.

## Russia provides strategic diversification but can also create new dependence

The post-2023 Russia relationship gives Pyongyang options that were unavailable during the period when China dominated nearly all external commerce.

For the DPRK, Russia can provide demand, logistics, fuel-related supplies, food, foreign currency, diplomatic backing and security cooperation. For Russia, North Korea offers military-industrial production, personnel, geographic access in Northeast Asia and a partner willing to oppose Western-led pressure.

This diversification reduces dependence on China at the margin, but it can produce a different form of concentration if Russian demand becomes a large share of foreign-currency earnings.

The durability of the relationship should be tested against post-war conditions in Ukraine, treaty implementation, non-military trade and whether cooperation persists if Russia's immediate military demand changes.

## Japan remains relevant through missiles, sanctions, abductees and regional security

Japan has no normal diplomatic relations with the DPRK. Bilateral relations are shaped by North Korean missile activity, nuclear policy, sanctions and the unresolved abduction issue involving Japanese citizens.

The abduction issue is not merely historical diplomacy; it constrains normalization and public opinion in Japan. DPRK and Japanese governments disagree over whether the issue has been fully resolved.

Japan also participates in U.S.-ROK-Japan security cooperation, missile warning and sanctions enforcement. This makes its relationship with the DPRK partly bilateral and partly embedded in a wider regional deterrence system.

## The United States remains the central external military reference point

The United States is the DPRK's principal stated external security adversary, maintains a military alliance with the ROK and stations forces on the peninsula. The two governments have also negotiated directly at several historical moments.

The 1994 Agreed Framework froze key plutonium facilities under IAEA monitoring for a period in exchange for an energy and normalization process. The arrangement later collapsed amid disputes over compliance, uranium-enrichment allegations and implementation.

The Six-Party Talks in the 2000s produced agreements but did not yield permanent denuclearization.

In 2018, Kim Jong Un and U.S. President Donald Trump met in Singapore and issued a joint statement including commitments related to new relations, a peace regime and denuclearization of the Korean Peninsula. The 2019 Hanoi summit ended without agreement on sanctions relief and nuclear dismantlement sequencing.

The recurring bargaining problem is sequencing and verification: one side seeks credible limits or dismantlement before large-scale normalization, while the DPRK seeks security guarantees, sanctions relief and recognition on terms that do not expose it to unilateral vulnerability.

## The 2026 external posture is less diplomacy-centered than in 2018–2019

The DPRK's current policy gives greater weight to strategic alignment with Russia, continued ties with China and institutional separation from the ROK. This reduces the relative importance of inter-Korean economic projects and U.S.-DPRK summit diplomacy in the short term.

However, it would be analytically incorrect to infer that negotiation is impossible. DPRK policy has changed rapidly at previous strategic junctures. Formal doctrine can establish bargaining baselines rather than eliminate diplomacy.

The useful question is what conditions would alter expected returns to negotiation. These could include sanctions pressure, Russian demand, Chinese preferences, domestic economic conditions, U.S. force posture and the credibility of phased verification arrangements.

## Society cannot be reconstructed reliably from elite politics alone

Much external discussion of North Korea treats society as passive. The marketization experience suggests otherwise. Households adapt to state shortages, prices and policy changes; traders rearrange supply networks; enterprises improvise input procurement; families use informal finance and foreign currency; local differences matter.

At the same time, the state retains extensive mechanisms of political organization, workplace assignment, neighborhood administration, schooling, information control and security enforcement.

The balance between these systems varies by class, geography, workplace and access to foreign currency. Pyongyang residents, border traders, farmers, miners, military families and residents of remote provinces do not experience the same political economy.

This is why a single label—"planned economy," "market economy," "total control" or "informal capitalism"—is analytically inadequate.

## Human-rights evidence is substantial but access limitations must be visible

The OHCHR's 2025 ten-year assessment used 314 interviews with victims and witnesses who had left the DPRK during the reporting period, plus UN, civil-society and other corroborating sources. It reported persistent restrictions on movement, expression, information access, commercial activity and other rights, and described increased surveillance.

The DPRK rejects the country-specific human-rights mandate and disputes the legitimacy and findings of such reports. It argues that external criticism is politicized and emphasizes constitutional rights, social welfare, education, healthcare and sovereignty.

The country has nevertheless participated in four Universal Periodic Review cycles. In the 2024 cycle it received 294 recommendations from 86 countries and, according to the 2026 Special Rapporteur report, accepted 143 and partially accepted seven. It has also engaged with the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities.

These facts should be held together. International engagement does not validate all DPRK domestic claims, and rejection of the OHCHR mandate does not invalidate interview evidence. The appropriate approach is claim-by-claim evaluation.

## Information control and surveillance have economic effects

Controls on outside media, communications and movement are usually discussed as political issues, but they also affect economic behavior.

Information restrictions reduce price and market transparency, make business planning harder and increase the value of personal networks. Surveillance raises transaction risk in informal trade. Border controls change arbitrage between Chinese and domestic prices. Restrictions on mobile communications affect logistics and payments.

Conversely, the spread of mobile phones, local digital services and state information systems can improve administrative coordination while increasing monitoring capacity.

Technology therefore has dual effects: higher state capacity and potentially more efficient commerce on one side, greater surveillance capability on the other.

## Demography is a slower but important constraint

BOK estimates the DPRK population at about 25.9 million in 2025. Long-term demographic trends point toward lower fertility and gradual aging, although precision is limited by the absence of frequent transparent census data.

Aging affects military manpower, labor supply, healthcare and household dependency. Migration is tightly controlled, so the country does not have the same large-scale labor-import option available to some aging economies.

Human capital remains a structural asset. North Korea has widespread basic education and technical training, including engineering and scientific institutions. The economic return to that human capital depends on equipment, power, access to scientific information, incentives and ability to deploy skills outside priority state sectors.

## Science and technology are now explicit economic and security priorities

The 2026 five-year plan emphasizes information technology and artificial intelligence alongside basic industry. Official statements also prioritize space, unmanned systems and advanced defense technology.

This produces a two-speed technology system. Selected strategic institutions can receive advanced training and equipment while the broader civilian economy faces older capital stock and limited imports.

The model can produce concentrated capability in narrow fields without economy-wide productivity convergence. Nuclear engineering, missiles, cyber operations or AI-enabled military systems do not imply equivalent technology adoption in ordinary factories, farms or hospitals.

## Cyber activity is economically relevant but attribution requires caution

U.S., ROK and other governments, cybersecurity firms and previous UN Panel reports have attributed cryptocurrency theft, cyberespionage and other operations to DPRK-linked groups and argued that proceeds support state programs. The DPRK generally rejects such accusations.

Cyber attribution is probabilistic: it uses malware signatures, infrastructure overlap, operator behavior, money flows and intelligence. It is stronger when multiple independent technical sources converge, but it is rarely equivalent to a publicly verifiable court-standard chain of evidence.

A deep economic assessment can therefore state that multiple governments and technical investigators consistently attribute significant cyber operations to DPRK-linked actors, while avoiding treating every incident or dollar estimate as certain.

## Household welfare is the least measurable major variable

There is no current independently administered nationwide household expenditure survey available to outside researchers. UN agencies lack normal in-country access. Market-price networks are partial. Official wages have limited meaning where households depend on markets, foreign currency, informal work and in-kind distribution.

Several proxies can be used:

- staple-food market prices;
- exchange rates;
- crop conditions;
- state rationing announcements;
- trade in consumer goods;
- satellite-observed construction;
- reports from border regions;
- nutrition and health data when available;
- household testimony from recent arrivals abroad.

No proxy should be converted mechanically into a national poverty rate.

This is why the article does not claim that 3.5 percent estimated GDP growth means living standards rose by 3.5 percent.

## Construction has become a visible political-economic instrument

Housing projects in Pyongyang, rural housing and regional industrial construction are recurring components of Kim Jong Un-era policy.

Construction serves several functions simultaneously:

1. adds physical capital;
2. provides visible state outputs;
3. mobilizes military and civilian labor;
4. generates demand for cement, steel, glass and transport;
5. redistributes resources geographically;
6. signals administrative capability.

The output is real when buildings are physically completed. But welfare value depends on occupancy, services, power, transport and allocation. Satellite confirmation of construction is therefore stronger evidence than official claims of social effect.

## Scenario 1: Managed re-centralization with market coexistence

The state continues to capture foreign exchange and strategic imports, prioritizes heavy industry, defense, housing and regional development, and expands Russian cooperation while conventional trade with China remains large. Household markets survive but operate under tighter regulation. Growth continues at moderate rates from a low base.

**Mechanism:** external inputs + priority allocation + construction + defense demand → formal-sector output → tax/revenue and political capacity, while markets absorb household shortages.

The core risk is distribution: if state capture of foreign exchange tightens household supply too far, market prices and exchange rates become destabilizing.

## Scenario 2: Broader civilian normalization

External relations improve enough to increase legal trade, banking access, humanitarian presence and civilian investment without requiring immediate wholesale political change. Energy, agriculture and light industry receive more imported inputs. The state retains strategic control but permits more enterprise autonomy.

**Mechanism:** lower transaction costs + larger input availability + market incentives → higher civilian productivity → greater household supply → less dependence on military-linked foreign exchange.

Evidence for this pathway is limited at present.

## Scenario 3: Militarized growth with narrow civilian spillover

Russian military demand and domestic defense investment remain major growth engines. Heavy industry, chemicals, machine building, logistics and selected technology sectors expand. Civilian output benefits indirectly from infrastructure and some industrial learning, but resources remain concentrated in defense and strategic projects.

**Mechanism:** military demand → priority industrial capacity → foreign exchange and selected technology → stronger strategic state capacity, with uncertain household transmission.

This scenario is compatible with several current observations but cannot be quantified precisely because military flows are opaque.

## Scenario 4: External reversal and renewed compression

Russian demand declines sharply, China tightens enforcement or trade, sanctions evasion becomes more difficult, or a security crisis closes borders. Foreign exchange and imports fall. The state responds with tighter allocation and controls.

**Mechanism:** external shock → input scarcity → industrial and market contraction → exchange-rate/price pressure → stronger administrative rationing → household welfare deterioration.

The pandemic demonstrated that such a mechanism is physically possible.

## What would change the assessment

The current assessment would become more favorable to a broad civilian-development interpretation if several independent indicators move together: sustained consumer-goods output, stable market exchange rates, rising food availability and household purchasing power, stronger electricity supply, more non-military trade, greater international operational access and evidence that regional factories operate rather than merely open.

It would shift toward a stronger militarized-recentralization interpretation if heavy industry and construction remain strong while market prices deteriorate, Russia-linked foreign earnings rise, military production receives visible priority and civilian trade composition remains narrow.

It would shift toward systemic fragility if multiple external channels contract simultaneously, harvests weaken, the won depreciates persistently, formal trade falls and the state increases emergency distribution controls.

A major strategic reassessment would be required if the DPRK accepts renewed on-site IAEA verification and a negotiated nuclear freeze or rollback, or conversely if verified fissile-material and delivery-system expansion accelerates substantially.

## Indicators worth monitoring

- Chinese customs exports to and imports from the DPRK by product.
- Russia-DPRK rail, port and non-military trade flows.
- BOK estimated sector growth, with methodology caveat.
- Market won/USD and won/RMB exchange rates from multiple independent networks.
- Rice and maize market prices and the maize/rice relative price.
- FAO satellite crop conditions and any renewed field access.
- Electricity generation proxies, reservoir conditions and industrial operating signs.
- Coal, fertilizer, machinery and petroleum-related import volumes.
- 20×10 factory completion **and operating evidence**.
- Housing completion, occupancy and utility connection.
- IAEA-observed signatures at Yongbyon and Kangson.
- Missile and space-launch activity classified by demonstrated capability rather than announced objective.
- UN humanitarian access and return of the UN Country Team to Pyongyang.
- UPR implementation indicators and disability-rights engagement.
- Inter-Korean legal/institutional changes and border infrastructure.
- Treaty implementation and military cooperation with Russia.
- Changes in UN sanctions implementation and humanitarian exemptions.

## Limitations and confidence

**Overall confidence: medium.**

Confidence is **high** on the existence of a highly centralized party-state, continued nuclear-program activity, sanctions architecture, China-dominant observable trade, the 2024 Russia treaty, Russian acknowledgment of DPRK personnel in Kursk and the broad direction of post-pandemic trade recovery.

Confidence is **medium** on the magnitude of GDP growth, sectoral output, market-control effectiveness and the distribution of recent economic gains. These rely heavily on external estimates and indirect observation.

Confidence is **low to medium** on current nationwide household welfare, exact military-industrial output, exact nuclear stockpile size, hidden financial flows, the value of Russia-related compensation and local enforcement variation.

The largest research limitation is access. The UN Country Team has not operated normally from Pyongyang since early 2021. IAEA inspectors have not been present since 2009. DPRK national statistics are selective. Many external accounts rely on defectors or border information whose samples are not nationally representative. Chinese customs measure declared trade, not the totality of economic exchange.

These limitations do not make analysis impossible. They make **source classification and uncertainty part of the result**.
