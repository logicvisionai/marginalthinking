# Global Macro Daily — September 17, 2026

## Executive assessment

**Regime:** a supply-driven inflation shock is still tightening global financial conditions, while Saudi alternative export routes have reduced — but not removed — the immediate crude-supply tail risk. The Federal Reserve raised the federal-funds target range by 25 basis points to 3.75%–4.00%. Brent traded around $104–105 and the 10-year Treasury yield moved back just below 5%.

**Observed fact.** The Fed unanimously raised rates on September 16 and said inflation remains elevated while activity, domestic spending and capital investment remain resilient. Its September projections raised the median 2026 real-GDP growth forecast to 2.3% from 2.2% in June. Early Thursday trading put the 2-year Treasury near 4.71% and the 10-year near 4.99%; the latest official H.15 observation available for the 30-year was 5.36% on September 15. Saudi Arabia offered additional crude cargoes through Sohar, Oman.

**Marginal Thinking inference.** The market is assigning less weight to an immediate loss of Saudi export volumes and more weight to the interest-rate response required to contain the inflation consequences of the energy shock. This is less disorderly than an outright supply seizure, but remains restrictive for long-duration assets, leveraged borrowers and energy-importing economies.

**Confidence:** high on the monetary-policy mechanism; medium on the energy-relief mechanism. Ship-tracking evidence confirms abnormally low Hormuz activity, but successive preliminary Kpler/Reuters snapshots are not directly comparable enough to support a precise day-over-day throughput claim.

## What changed since yesterday

1. **The Fed converted market expectation into policy.** The target range rose to 3.75%–4.00%. Goldman Sachs subsequently changed its public research forecast to another 25-bp hike in October. This is a `research thesis`, not evidence of proprietary portfolio positioning.
2. **Crude-supply tail risk eased, but physical shipping remains impaired.** Saudi Arabia is redirecting barrels through Oman and work on the East-West pipeline is expected to restore capacity. Brent fell toward $104–105. Kpler data reported by Reuters at 0445 GMT on September 17 counted three commodity-vessel transits through Hormuz on Wednesday, versus a 10-day average of about 17, excluding AIS-dark traffic. A Reuters/Kpler snapshot published a day earlier had counted four Tuesday transits versus a 10-day average of 18; the later September 17 report retrospectively cited 12 for Tuesday. Because the snapshots differ by cutoff and/or classification, this report does not use them as a continuous day-over-day series.
3. **The rates shock became more orderly.** The 10-year Treasury slipped back below 5% after briefly exceeding that level, while rate volatility eased.
4. **Asian market differentiation increased.** China and Hong Kong weakened while Japan advanced modestly and the yen traded around 155–156 per dollar. South Korean equities were resilient, but foreign investors remained net sellers.
5. **Brazil begins trading a narrower interest differential.** Copom cut Selic to 13.75% after Wednesday’s close while the Fed moved in the opposite direction.

The important signal is selective relief rather than a synchronized global rally. Airlines and transport operators benefit directly if fuel costs fall, although tight diesel and jet-fuel markets limit the pass-through.

U.S. index futures rose roughly 0.7%–1.0% before the cash open. Lower oil and a more orderly Treasury market explain the relief better than a broad easing in financial conditions: a 10-year yield around 5% still raises required returns across equities and credit.

## Global Opportunity Radar

### European transport and energy-intensive industries

**What changed:** crude fell for a second session as Saudi export alternatives improved. **Potential dislocation:** if physical supply improves faster than expected, airlines, transport, chemicals and selected manufacturers can recover part of the margin compression created by the oil shock. **Catalyst:** verified restoration of Saudi export capacity and sustained easing in refined-product prices. **Risk:** Hormuz activity remains abnormally low and diesel is tight. **Invalidation:** renewed verified infrastructure damage or a sustained return of crude and refined products to recent stress highs. **Horizon:** days to several weeks. **Consensus relation:** second-order observation, not evidence of institutional allocation.

### South African assets

The rand strengthened about 0.6% to roughly ZAR16.31 per dollar, the JSE Top-40 gained around 0.6%, and the 2035 sovereign yield fell about 3 bps to 8.71%. Gold and platinum strength is offsetting part of the Fed tightening impulse. The research question is whether commodity income can continue to support the currency and sovereign curve despite weak domestic growth. Invalidation would be simultaneous commodity weakness, renewed dollar strength and rising domestic yields.

### Hong Kong versus mainland China

Higher U.S. rates transmit more directly into Hong Kong through the currency-board system. Mainland China retains greater monetary autonomy, although capital-flow and currency constraints remain. The evidence to watch is HIBOR, HKMA liquidity operations, southbound flows and relative earnings revisions; current evidence is insufficient for a directional conclusion.

## Institutional consensus and divergence

The only institutional change documented strongly enough for publication today is Goldman Sachs’s September 17 public forecast for another 25-bp Fed hike in October following the September decision. It is classified as a **research thesis** and does not establish portfolio positioning.

**Consensus state: Unclear.** The Fed’s projections and market pricing are important policy and market evidence, but they are not institutional research signals under the Marginal Thinking classification. Public bank forecasts also differ on the timing of the next hike. Without multiple comparable, attributable house-view changes with dates and signal classes, there is insufficient evidence to label the institutional state `Broadening`.

What would clarify the state: additional named institutions changing their published Fed paths in the same direction, with identifiable dates and prior views. What would invalidate the tightening narrative more broadly: sustained declines in inflation expectations and energy prices together with material deterioration in labor demand.

## Global rates, credit and financial conditions

Early Thursday trading put the 2-year Treasury near 4.71% and the 10-year near 4.99%; official H.15 data showed the 30-year at 5.36% and the 10-year real yield at 2.62% on September 15. High real yields raise the hurdle rate for growth equities, private assets and leveraged credit.

## Commodities and energy

Brent traded around $104–105 and WTI around $101. The decline reflects Saudi export alternatives and expectations of East-West pipeline restoration. Physical evidence should be read cautiously: Reuters/Kpler reported **three commodity-vessel transits on Wednesday as of 0445 GMT Thursday, versus a 10-day average near 17, excluding AIS-dark vessels**. A contemporaneous report published Wednesday had counted **four Tuesday transits versus a 10-day average of 18**, while Thursday’s later retrospective comparison cited 12 for Tuesday. These observations are preliminary and appear to use different cutoffs and/or classification states; they establish severely depressed visible traffic, but not a reliable 12-to-3 daily collapse.

This revision lowers confidence in the *magnitude of the latest daily deterioration*, not in the broader conclusion that Hormuz shipping remains impaired. The energy-relief thesis therefore rests primarily on Saudi alternative loadings, expected pipeline restoration and observed crude-price easing — not on a reconstructed daily Hormuz series.

Refined products remain a separate risk. Diesel markets are tight because Middle Eastern disruption coincides with pressure on Russian refining. Lower crude does not automatically produce equivalent relief in diesel, freight or industrial fuel costs.

Gold rebounded above $4,300/oz and silver rose more than 1% in early trade; copper was comparatively stable. Agricultural markets showed large individual moves, but contract-month and exchange-timing differences were too material to support a unified macro inflation signal today.

## Conflict, security and political transmission

The Gulf conflict remains material because it affects physical energy logistics. The robust observation is that visible commodity-vessel traffic through Hormuz is far below recent averages, not that a precise daily count fell from 12 to three. Restricted maritime and pipeline capacity affects available crude and refined-product supply; energy prices affect inflation; inflation alters central-bank reaction functions and financing costs.

Claims about military intent or imminent diplomatic resolution are not treated as verified outcomes. Market-relevant evidence is physical throughput with explicit definitions and cutoffs, verified infrastructure condition, freight and insurance costs, and observed energy prices.

## Systemic risks and transmission

**Energy relapse:** renewed verified disruption to Saudi export infrastructure or Hormuz shipping would pressure crude, refined products and inflation compensation.

**Rates-to-credit transmission:** the absence of acute financing stress does not eliminate refinancing risk around a 5% Treasury benchmark.

**Asian FX pressure:** further Fed tightening with slower Asian policy adjustment can increase intervention needs and imported-energy costs simultaneously.

## Conditional scenarios

**Base — restrictive but orderly.** Saudi alternative export capacity continues to recover, Brent remains below recent stress highs, the Fed retains a tightening bias and credit spreads stay contained. Hormuz traffic remains impaired, so confidence in physical normalization is only moderate.

**Pro-risk — verified energy normalization.** Saudi pipeline/export capacity improves and consistently defined Hormuz throughput rises across successive observations; refined-product spreads ease, breakevens decline and long yields soften. The trigger is comparable physical data, not diplomatic headlines or inconsistent preliminary snapshots.

**Risk-off — renewed supply disruption plus monetary tightening.** Verified infrastructure damage or further deterioration in consistently measured shipping coincides with tighter refined products, renewed inflation pressure and wider credit spreads.

## Conclusion

**Thesis:** the market has moved from pricing an uncontrolled energy-supply tail risk toward a restrictive inflation-and-rates problem. Saudi alternative logistics have reduced the immediate crude-shortage risk while the Fed has raised the cost of money. The evidence does **not** justify claiming a clean day-over-day recovery or deterioration in Hormuz throughput because preliminary Kpler snapshots are not fully comparable.

**Main contrary evidence:** visible Hormuz traffic remains severely depressed and refined-product markets remain tight.

**Potentially underestimated risk:** cumulative refinancing pressure as more borrowers roll debt at rates materially above earlier vintages.

**Three variables to monitor:** (1) consistently defined Saudi/Hormuz physical throughput and diesel spreads; (2) U.S. 2-year/10-year yields and inflation compensation; (3) cross-border flow confirmation in Asian and emerging-market assets.
