# Europe–Russia–Ukraine security system

**Internal research memory. Not a public source. Current facts must be reverified externally before reuse.**

## Structural assessment

The European security order moved from an attempted post-Cold War accommodation toward a durable system organized increasingly around war, deterrence, rearmament and long-horizon defence capacity. The mechanism is path-dependent: force structure, industrial capacity, logistics, sanctions adaptation, fiscal commitments and alliance planning create capabilities and constituencies that can persist beyond a ceasefire or a single diplomatic episode.

Legal responsibility for the use of force and causal explanation of the preceding security order are separate analytical questions. Future research should preserve that distinction while examining the interaction among Ukrainian agency, NATO's open-door architecture, Russian security and status preferences, and divergent threat perceptions within Europe.

## Historical and institutional anchors

- **1991:** Ukraine re-established independence and began formal relations with NATO, becoming an autonomous security actor rather than a Soviet administrative unit.
- **1994:** Partnership for Peace and the Budapest Memorandum linked Ukrainian nuclear disarmament to the post-Soviet security settlement.
- **1997:** the NATO–Russia Founding Act institutionalized cooperation while preserving independent decision-making and sovereign alignment choice; it did not grant Russia a veto over NATO membership decisions.
- **2008:** NATO's Bucharest declaration said Ukraine and Georgia would become members without establishing an operational timetable.
- **2010:** Ukraine's non-bloc policy showed that alignment remained politically reversible while NATO's open-door principle remained in place.
- **2014:** Russia's annexation of Crimea and the war in Donbas converted institutional disagreement into territorial and military conflict.
- **2022:** Russia's full-scale invasion transformed European security, Ukrainian identity, military planning, fiscal priorities and industrial policy.
- **2025–2026:** higher long-run NATO and European defence commitments embedded the conflict in fiscal, industrial and infrastructure planning.

The durable institutional tension is between sovereign alignment choice and attempts to construct a security order acceptable to a major neighbouring power that assigns high strategic importance to the orientation of nearby states.

## Actors, agency and constraints

**Ukraine:** alignment choices cannot be reduced to Washington–Moscow competition. Domestic politics, coercion, war, territorial loss and changing assessments of security guarantees materially changed the perceived costs of neutrality. Ukrainian state and population should not be treated as interchangeable analytical objects.

**Russia:** security concerns, great-power status, military geography and influence over neighbouring alignments overlap but are not identical mechanisms. Future work should distinguish protection against military threats from attempts to shape the external orientation of neighbouring states.

**NATO and European states:** there is no unitary Western preference. Poland and the Baltic region face different historical and geographic exposure from Germany or France; the United States continues to provide capabilities that Europe cannot reproduce quickly. These differences affect sanctions, energy policy, procurement, force posture, fiscal policy and the durability of support for Ukraine.

## Economy, production and society

War has become part of European political economy rather than a separate foreign-policy variable. Higher defence commitments transmit through procurement, infrastructure, R&D, skilled labour, metals, electronics, energy, transport capacity and public finance. The economic effect depends on domestic versus imported production, financing method and whether new capacity produces civilian spillovers.

Ukrainian reconstruction and displacement are structural European variables. Destruction and insecurity transmit into reconstruction finance, labour supply, housing, schools, municipal budgets, integration policy, remittances, demographics and the future conditions of return. Germany and Poland are particularly relevant host-country systems and should be examined as part of this transmission rather than only as diplomatic actors.

## Escalation and de-escalation mechanisms

Do not compress escalation into a precise probability without a defensible model. Track observable mechanisms separately: conventional posture and geographical expansion, alliance consultation and readiness, nuclear signalling, diplomatic and military communication, arms-control arrangements, defence-industrial mobilisation, sanctions/economic coercion, cyber/space/critical-infrastructure incidents, and public expectations where methodologically credible.

Additional military capacity can strengthen deterrence while simultaneously increasing military proximity and opportunities for incidents. The net effect depends on doctrine, deployments, communication and crisis-management mechanisms.

A ceasefire can reduce combat without restoring the cooperative institutional conditions of the 1990s. A more durable de-escalation would require some combination of credible implementation and monitoring, lower operational contact, restored military communication, arms-control/risk-reduction mechanisms and changed long-horizon force-planning assumptions.

## Competing explanations to preserve

Future research should keep several explanation families separate rather than synthesize them into one verdict:

- sovereignty and legal order;
- security dilemma and military geography;
- sphere-of-influence and status politics;
- institutional/path-dependent breakdown of post-Cold War accommodation;
- Ukrainian state-building and domestic agency;
- national identity, collective memory and social adaptation;
- material constraints such as finance, demographics, logistics, ammunition and industrial capacity.

The relative causal weight of these explanations remains contested and requires external historical and empirical verification.

## Open questions

- Which post-war or ceasefire security arrangements could reduce operational contact without recreating the unresolved alignment problem?
- How persistent are European defence-industrial and fiscal commitments under different conflict outcomes?
- How do Ukrainian and Russian public attitudes change as war duration, casualties, economic conditions and settlement proposals evolve?
- Which capabilities remain most difficult for European states to substitute for U.S. support?
- How do reconstruction, refugee integration and possible return migration alter Ukrainian and host-country demographics and labour markets over a longer horizon?
