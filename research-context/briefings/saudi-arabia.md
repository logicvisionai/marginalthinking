# Saudi Arabia: state-led diversification, fiscal dependence and labour transition

## Structural assessment

Saudi Arabia combines hydrocarbon export income, a large state investment apparatus, a dollar-pegged monetary regime and an effort to broaden non-oil production and private employment. The durable analytical question is not whether the economy is simply moving “away from oil,” but whether oil-funded public investment can create commercially viable private activity, export capacity and productivity that remain durable as state capital becomes more selective.

Oil dependence and diversification can coexist. Non-oil activity may expand while fiscal capacity still depends heavily on hydrocarbon income. Future research should therefore separate non-oil GDP growth from the non-oil fiscal balance, private investment, non-oil exports, project cash flows and the composition of state financing.

## Political and institutional structure

The diversification model remains state-led. The Public Investment Fund (PIF) is a major mechanism for allocating state capital into infrastructure, tourism, industry and services. Its scale can create infrastructure and anchor demand, but asset size alone does not establish productive additionality or commercially sustainable returns.

A durable institutional test is whether project selection becomes more selective while private firms, financing and exports deepen. Public-investment management, non-oil revenue, subsidy design, capital-market development and bank exposure to large projects therefore matter alongside headline Vision 2030 project execution.

The riyal's U.S.-dollar peg provides a nominal anchor but also imports a substantial part of U.S. monetary conditions. When the domestic oil and investment cycle differs from the U.S. cycle, more adjustment falls on fiscal choices, bank regulation and liquidity management.

## Economic and production structure

Hydrocarbon income continues to support fiscal resources and state balance sheets that finance diversification. The reusable mechanism is:

`oil income → fiscal/state balance sheets → PIF and public investment → infrastructure/industry/services → private suppliers and employment → potential broader tax and export base`

The constraint loop runs in the opposite direction: weaker oil income or weak project returns can tighten fiscal choices, force project reprioritization and increase the need for private capital and productivity.

Saudi export infrastructure provides partial logistical redundancy. The East-West pipeline and Red Sea ports can reduce exposure to impaired Hormuz traffic, but they do not make Gulf logistics irrelevant. Effective substitution depends on pipeline and terminal availability, product mix, shipping, insurance, refining and realized throughput. Keep this mechanism linked to `gulf-energy-logistics.md` rather than duplicating route detail here.

## Society, labour and demography

Labour reform is part of the production model, not a separate social appendix. Saudi female labour-force participation has risen materially over the past decade, expanding domestic labour supply and the household income base. At the same time, the economy remains segmented between Saudi nationals and a large expatriate workforce, while technical, digital and capital-intensive sectors increase demand for specialized skills.

Future work should examine whether national participation, skills formation and private employment broaden the distribution of non-oil growth. Housing costs, skill mismatches and national/expatriate segmentation can affect both household outcomes and firm competitiveness. Short-term quarterly labour changes should not be treated as a monotonic measure of reform success.

## Relationships and dependencies

- Saudi Arabia ↔ global oil market: export prices and volumes affect fiscal capacity and the current account.
- Saudi state balance sheet → PIF/public projects → private suppliers, banks, labour and urban demand.
- Hormuz/Gulf security → route utilization, freight and insurance → export receipts and global energy transmission.
- East-West pipeline/Red Sea ports → partial route redundancy, not full immunity from Gulf disruption.
- U.S. monetary policy → dollar peg → Saudi financing conditions.
- Labour-market reform → national participation/skills → private-sector productivity and distribution of diversification gains.

## Open questions

- Can non-oil exports, private investment and productivity deepen while the non-oil fiscal deficit narrows?
- Does more selective PIF allocation increase private financing and commercially sustainable project cash flows, or mainly reduce the pace of state-led investment?
- How does bank exposure to large projects evolve relative to underlying cash flows and capital buffers?
- Which skill, housing and labour-segmentation constraints most affect private-sector competitiveness?
- How much effective Saudi export redundancy remains available under prolonged regional disruption after operational constraints are included?
