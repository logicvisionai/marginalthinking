# Iraq — state capacity, hydrocarbon federalism and productive conversion

**Object:** Iraq as a durable political-economy and state-capacity system.  
**Use:** Internal orientation only; re-verify all current claims externally before public use.

## Structural assessment

Iraq's core constraint is not resource scarcity but conversion capacity. Hydrocarbon rents provide the fiscal and external base of the state, while electricity reliability, domestic gas capture, water, finance, labour absorption, federal coordination and security governance determine whether that income becomes durable non-oil productive capacity. This creates a strong transmission channel from oil export conditions into treasury liquidity, public payrolls and transfers, contractors, household demand, bank deposits and investment.

Large reserves, a young population, a Gulf-to-Türkiye land position and major infrastructure ambitions give Iraq more capacity than a simple fragility frame implies. The analytical question is whether institutions can reduce the economy's dependence on the oil cycle rather than whether the state possesses resources.

## Historical and institutional anchors

- The 2005 Constitution established a federal parliamentary republic. Post-2003 government formation has relied heavily on coalition bargaining across Shi'a, Sunni and Kurdish parties and blocs; this political practice should not be treated as identical to a formal constitutional quota system.
- The territorial defeat of ISIS removed the earlier condition of large areas outside federal control, but left a layered security architecture involving federal forces, police, the Counter Terrorism Service, Popular Mobilization structures and the Kurdistan Region's Peshmerga.
- UNAMI ended its mandate on 31 December 2025. The shift from a special political mission toward the UN Country Team increased the relative burden on Iraqi constitutional and political institutions for dispute resolution.
- The international coalition's military mission has been transitioning toward bilateral security arrangements. Future research should verify the current stage and operating terms rather than assuming the 2026 transition timetable remains unchanged.

## Federalism and hydrocarbon bargaining

Federal-Kurdistan relations are a recurring state-capacity mechanism, not only an oil dispute. They combine constitutional ambiguity, fiscal transfers, public-sector salaries, field contracts, pipeline access and competing interpretations of hydrocarbon authority. Repeated bargaining affects budget predictability, contractor payments, investment incentives and the credibility of federal-regional rules.

The shutdown of the Iraq-Türkiye Pipeline after 2023 reduced formal northern export options. Subsequent budget amendments and federal-KRG arrangements showed that operational compromise is possible, but repeated renegotiation means announcements should not be treated as durable settlements without evidence of implementation, transfers and sustained flows.

## Economy, production and finance

Oil remains the dominant fiscal and external anchor. The durable mechanism is fiscal procyclicality: a large recurrent spending base can sustain demand during strong oil years but raises sensitivity to price, production and export-route shocks. Southern Gulf terminals concentrate export capacity, making maritime access through the Gulf and Strait of Hormuz a macroeconomic variable. Northern routes matter partly because they provide potential diversification of export geography.

Electricity illustrates the gap between resource ownership and productive services. Iraq has large hydrocarbon resources yet has faced gas gathering, processing, grid reliability and import-dependence constraints. Domestic gas capture, interconnections and generation diversification matter because unreliable power acts as a cost on households and firms.

Banking intermediation is another conversion bottleneck. Oil revenue is centralized while productive opportunities are dispersed. If deposits and public liquidity are not transformed into risk-priced private credit, diversification remains dependent on budget allocations, state-linked finance, cash and foreign investors.

## Society, labour and demography

A young population expands potential labour supply and domestic demand but also increases the cost of weak job creation. Public employment preference creates a feedback loop: oil revenue supports payroll expansion; expectations of state employment raise political pressure for hiring; recurrent expenditure rises; and fiscal space for productivity-enhancing investment can narrow.

Future work should distinguish labour-force participation, unemployment, public employment, private formal employment and informal activity. Labour-market claims are time-sensitive and should be refreshed from the latest nationally representative survey.

## Water, infrastructure and geography

Water scarcity links climate, agriculture, food imports, rural employment, internal migration and provincial politics. Short-term rainfall or storage improvements should not be mistaken for resolution of structural aridity, evaporation, water-quality problems or transboundary dependence on the Tigris-Euphrates system.

Grand Faw Port and the Development Road are attempts to monetize geography beyond crude exports by linking Gulf shipping to rail and road corridors toward Türkiye and Europe. Treat these as productive capacity only when operational evidence appears: completed links, throughput, border-processing performance, private logistics investment and industrial activity. Announced project scale alone is insufficient.

## External relationships

Iraq's external position is structurally plural rather than reducible to one alignment. The United States matters for security cooperation, finance and international economic connectivity; Iran for energy, trade, religious and political networks; Türkiye for water, northern security, commerce and the Ceyhan corridor; Gulf states for electricity interconnection, capital and logistics; and China as a major crude buyer and infrastructure/energy investor. Different partners map to different domestic bottlenecks, which encourages constraint-management across relationships.

## Durable indicators for future research

Re-verify whether non-oil revenue is rising relative to recurrent spending; whether domestic gas capture is reducing imported-energy dependence; whether electricity reliability improves without proportionate fiscal support; whether private credit and formal non-state employment deepen; whether federal-KRG transfer and hydrocarbon rules become more predictable; whether water productivity improves across variable rainfall; and whether Development Road/Faw generate measurable commercial throughput.

## Open questions

- Can federal-KRG hydrocarbon and transfer arrangements become rule-bound enough to lower investment and fiscal uncertainty?
- Can electricity and gas reforms turn hydrocarbon abundance into reliable productive energy rather than continued import dependence and private backup generation?
- Will logistics corridors create tradable-service and industrial capacity beyond construction spending?
- Can the financial system allocate capital to private firms at sufficient scale to weaken the payroll-and-oil cycle?
- How will water stress alter agricultural employment, migration, provincial bargaining and infrastructure priorities?
- Can security coordination remain sufficiently unified as external coalition arrangements recede?
