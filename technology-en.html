RESEARCH PROGRAM · TECHNOLOGY, POWER & TRANSFORMATION

Technology, power and transformation.

We assess emerging technologies by what has been demonstrated, what is already deployed and what still depends on engineering, scale, capital and time. The purpose is not spectacular forecasting; it is to identify when technical change begins to alter productivity, industrial capacity, security, markets and the distribution of power.

PrincipleEvidenceStructureAgenda

From laboratory result to economic impact

Every assessment follows an explicit chain: science → engineering → demonstrated capability → deployment → scale → economics → power. A laboratory result is not treated as a product; a product is not treated as an industry at scale; a roadmap is not treated as delivered capability.

Claimed

A target, announcement, roadmap or vendor statement. Evidence of intent, not delivery.

Demonstrated

A capability observed in a verifiable experiment, prototype or test, with conditions and limitations stated.

Deployed / Scaled

Operational use followed by enough industrial and economic repeatability to change real decisions.

Source hierarchy and uncertainty

Central technical conclusions prioritize peer-reviewed literature and primary documentation from institutions such as NIST, NASA, DOE, DARPA, DoD, ESA, CERN, National Academies, GAO, national laboratories, regulators and budget or procurement records. Nature, Science, IEEE, universities and research organizations such as RAND, IISS and RUSI complement the evidence when appropriate. Company materials are useful for hardware, investment and roadmaps, but statements about their own products remain explicitly vendor-reported.

Horizons are separated into 0–2, 3–5, 5–10, 10–20 and, only when useful, 20+ years. Confidence falls as technical and economic dependencies accumulate. Every assessment contains contrary evidence, limitations and a “What would change our assessment?” section.

Canonical structure

Reports cover the state of the art, maturity, physical and engineering constraints, supply chains, industrial capacity, costs, who funds and who pays, labor and productivity, macroeconomic and geoeconomic effects, security and defense where relevant, second-order effects, scenarios and observable indicators.

Military and political technology receives the same treatment: programs, funding and intent are not confused with demonstration, deployment or operational capability.

Initial research agenda

The order can change when new evidence makes another topic more important. The program is not required to produce a positive thesis about every technology.
