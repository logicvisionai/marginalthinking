# Global Macro — Lula reelected as Brazil enters a high-dollar-cost market opening

**26 October 2026 — early edition, information cutoff 01:44 Brasília time.** Brazil's presidential runoff was counted on Sunday, 25 October. The last completed liquid session for Brazilian and major US markets was Friday, 23 October. No Monday opening prices are included. Fund data released during the previous week retain their own observation dates and should not be interpreted as a post-election reaction.

President **Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva was reelected**, receiving **64,256,232 votes and 51.79% of valid ballots**, against **59,826,041 votes and 48.21%** for Flávio Bolsonaro. The Superior Electoral Court confirmed the outcome after Sunday's counting and issued an institutional statement early Monday. The margin was **4,430,191 votes**, or **3.58 percentage points** of valid votes rather than of the entire registered electorate. Approximately 128.2 million voters participated and abstention was roughly 19.2%. The political event is now observed; the response of the Brazilian real, interest-rate futures, equities and credit has not yet formed during the information cutoff. [1]

International financing conditions remain demanding. On **23 October, the US ten-year Treasury yield closed at 5.00%**, versus **4.96% on 22 October**; the two-year yield rose from **4.65% to 4.69%**, while the thirty-year yield reached **5.33%**, from **5.29%**. The ten-year minus two-year slope was **31 basis points**, and the ten-year inflation-protected Treasury real yield was approximately **2.66%**. A positively sloped curve does not imply cheap financing when longer-term discount rates remain elevated. [2][3]

Energy risk remains another material transmission channel. The Brent and WTI futures benchmarks closed Friday at approximately **US$116.72** and **US$103.27 per barrel**, based on contract histories identified in the prior daily edition. These are last-session prices, not quotations after Brazil's election or proof of a physical loss of crude output. Differences between Brent and WTI cannot be treated as a direct measurement of the Strait of Hormuz risk premium. [4][5]

## The election result is new information, not yet a new asset price

The analytical change from the Sunday edition is the transition from **electoral uncertainty** to **a confirmed electoral outcome**. The previous publication could assess who might win only as a conditional political event; the Monday edition has a known winner and must distinguish the implications of his reelection from prices that have not yet been observed. Required yields on Brazilian assets will depend on expectations for revenue, primary spending, inflation, monetary policy, institutions and external financing. Those variables cannot be calculated mechanically from the voting margin.

An election determines the executive office but does not enact a budget, set the future fiscal rule or guarantee congressional votes. Public-spending proposals require a legislative and financing process. Monetary policy operates through separate institutions. Investors may price prospective policies well ahead of their adoption, but a market quotation remains a statement about expected outcomes and risk preferences, not evidence that an announced programme has already been implemented.

The opening-session tests should compare the **BRL against similar emerging-market currencies**, **local DI yields against US Treasury and real yields**, **Brazilian exporters against domestic-demand equities**, and **sector-specific corporate credit**. A real depreciation accompanying broad declines across emerging currencies cannot be assigned exclusively to the election without further evidence. A divergence concentrated in Brazilian fiscal-duration exposures would make a local political explanation more plausible, subject to volume and positioning analysis.

The voting margin is an institutional fact that eliminates alternative winner scenarios. The magnitude of asset repricing depends on how far the actual result differs from probabilities already embedded in market prices, which cannot be directly read from the electoral count. Investors may have accumulated asymmetric positions, but claims about a particular portfolio require position and liquidation records.

## Dollar capital costs constrain Brazil's domestic fiscal choices

The US ten-year yield matters as a global discount-rate reference because international assets are often valued relative to dollar rates, credit premia and currency risk. Higher yields can lower the present value of long-duration cash flows and raise financing pressure for emerging issuers. The Federal Reserve does not need to change its policy rate immediately for the long end of the Treasury curve to alter financing conditions.

The US Treasury's official daily table reported **4.69% on the two-year note, 5.00% on ten years and 5.33% on thirty years** on 23 October. The **31-basis-point** ten-minus-two-year spread is a measure of curve slope, not a complete breakdown of expected inflation, real rates and term premia. Inflation-protected securities may assist that decomposition, but nominal-real differences also contain liquidity effects. [2][3]

Brazilian public finances face domestic borrowing in local currency and exposure through international investment, trade and exchange rates. Expensive dollar financing can increase risk premia, while higher oil prices can support exporters and harm transportation and food supply chains. The net effect depends on sectoral composition, taxation and policy, rather than a single market indicator.

## Oil-price risk begins in transport and expectations as well as production

Energy shocks transmit through at least five separate mechanisms: actual output, insurance premiums, freight rates, vessel availability and expectations embedded in forward prices. Futures may rise on the threat of interruption before measured supply declines. Refiners and importers can face high transport costs even when wells and pipelines remain technically operational.

Brazil combines crude exports with extensive domestic dependence on road transportation and fuel-sensitive costs. A higher benchmark oil price can improve revenue for some producers while raising costs for households, agriculture and logistics. Net energy-importing economies face potential deterioration in their external balances. Foreign-exchange reserves, fiscal credibility and exposure to shipping lanes determine the degree of resilience.

The International Energy Agency's October oil market report provides a benchmark for earlier global supply, inventory and consumption data. It cannot verify a disruption that occurred after its statistical cutoff. Shipping and production claims require separately dated operational evidence. [5]

Inflation effects can spread beyond fuel stations to agricultural distribution, machinery, building materials and retail prices with a lag. Their magnitude depends on the shock's duration, currency behavior and firms' pricing power. An increase in both energy costs and nominal yields does not directly reveal how much each product's consumer price will rise.

## Global growth resilience now coexists with persistent price pressure

The IMF's **October 2026 World Economic Outlook** projected global expansion of **3.1% in 2026** and **3.4% in 2027**, with projected world headline inflation at **4.7% in 2026** and **4.1% in 2027**. These are outlook figures based on an earlier statistical cutoff, not estimates revised after Brazil's 25 October vote. [6]

The US flash purchasing-managers survey released on 23 October suggested continuing economic activity alongside intense cost pressure, particularly in manufacturing. Respondents cited oil-related inputs, fuel, shipping and several industrial metals. Purchasing-manager indexes describe the breadth and direction of surveyed business conditions; they do not directly measure realized national GDP or producer-price inflation. [7]

This global environment complicates early monetary easing in both advanced and emerging economies. In a separate private projection dated 19 October, S&P Global anticipated further policy tightening at the Federal Reserve and European Central Bank. Such forecasts are not official decisions. Announcements should be verified against the relevant central-bank calendars and statements. [8]

## Demand for liquid assets remains large, but fund flows diverge

The latest Investment Company Institute publication recorded an increase of **US$21.49 billion** in US money-market fund assets for the week ended 21 October, bringing the total to approximately **US$7.94 trillion**. Government money-market funds accounted for most of the rise. This is a change in fund assets under a defined statistical population, not proof of a worldwide investor flight into cash and not evidence of post-election Brazilian asset trading. [9]

A different ICI data set showed unusually large estimated outflows from American long-term funds for the week ended 14 October. The association itself emphasized collective institutional investment trust activity behind much of the domestic equity outflow. The separate weekly wealth publication examines why the distinction between fund-vehicle changes, sales of underlying securities and ownership shifts is essential. [10]

The IMF's October **Global Financial Stability Report** identified leverage in nonbank finance, possible corrections in AI-linked assets, sovereign-rate shocks and cyber risks as channels that might amplify market stress. The warnings describe vulnerabilities and conditional mechanisms, not confirmation of an unfolding global crisis during Monday's early hours. [11]

## Transmission map and falsifiable tests for the week

The relevant interactions have separate clocks. The Brazilian political event is complete, but congressional and budget developments will occur over weeks and months. Dollar rates may change within minutes, while freight and physical oil supply are observed through shipping and industrial data with lags. The distinction should remain explicit in each causal argument.

```flow
Official Brazilian election outcome → revised fiscal and regulatory expectations → DI, BRL and equity valuations → actual policies only after legal decisions
US Treasury ten-year at 5.00% → higher global discount rate → possible repricing of credit and long-duration stocks → emerging-market transmission
Oil transport and insurance risks → futures and freight costs → delayed input-price effects → inflation and monetary decisions
```

The conditional interpretation can be tested. **If** Brazilian prices move differently from peer markets after controlling for dollar and energy factors, an election-specific component becomes more plausible. **If** dollar and oil shocks dominate across countries, an exclusively Brazilian explanation weakens. Lula's reelection resolves political uncertainty over who won, not the fiscal, financing and real-economy constraints ahead.
