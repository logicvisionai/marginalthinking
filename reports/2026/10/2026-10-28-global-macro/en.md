# Global Macro — AI memory contracts deepen the earnings–financing divide as Pacific central banks confront inflation

**28 October 2026, early Brasília edition. Information cutoff: 05:00 BRT (08:00 UTC); US and Brazilian market observations refer to the completed 27 October session, while Asian prices are intraday and Australian, New Zealand and Korean releases are dated 28 October in their local time zones. The Federal Reserve decision is still ahead.**

The most consequential change since yesterday is not another turn in Brazil's post-election volatility. It is the arrival of measurable evidence that the artificial-intelligence infrastructure cycle can generate exceptional profits at a critical supplier even as its customers and financiers face expensive dollar funding. SK hynix reported third-quarter revenue of **KRW 104.6155 trillion** and operating profit of **KRW 83.3338 trillion**, with signed long-term customer agreements covering **more than half of its 2027–28 production capacity**. That is stronger evidence of committed demand than an equity-market rally, but neither contracted capacity nor supplier profit demonstrates that the entire downstream AI investment chain earns adequate returns. [1]

At the same time, the Reserve Bank of New Zealand unexpectedly held its policy rate at **2.75%** on a **4–3 vote**, while Australian headline inflation accelerated from **4.0% in August to 4.3% in September**. These decisions and data illustrate the fragmentation of monetary responses to a common energy and dollar-rate environment. The United States enters its policy announcement with the ten-year Treasury at **4.99%** and the thirty-year at **5.30%**, according to the 27 October official curve. High discount rates coexist with strong technology earnings and a fragile domestic-demand recovery in parts of the Pacific. [2][3][4][5][6]

This divergence has a clearer transmission path than a generic risk-on/risk-off label. Semiconductor supply contracts concentrate expected future purchases, while the ultimate buyers still must finance data centres, electricity, equipment and software adoption. Energy prices and exchange rates meanwhile affect household purchasing power and central-bank reaction functions. The research question is whether productive revenue can broaden faster than financing and input costs constrain the rest of the chain. [1][2][7]

## The memory supplier has signed demand; the AI customer still has to earn its return

SK hynix's 28 October release shows third-quarter sales rising **32%** from **KRW 79.3187 trillion** in the second quarter and operating profit increasing **38%** from **KRW 60.5426 trillion**. Its reported operating margin reached **80%**, while net income was **KRW 39.7756 trillion**. These are preliminary company figures under Korean accounting standards, not independently audited results, and operating profit must not be added to revenue or net income. The company attributes the performance to advanced server memory, HBM4 shipments and higher product prices. [1]

The structural development is the procurement horizon. Management says agreements with **14 major customers** cover more than half of aggregate 2027 and 2028 output, with pricing mechanisms linked to market conditions. This does not disclose every buyer's contracted volume, prepayment, delivery schedule or ultimate utilisation. It nevertheless changes the supplier's revenue visibility and may reduce the amount of advanced memory available on the spot market. For cloud operators, the same arrangement can reduce procurement uncertainty while limiting flexibility if demand forecasts change. The two perspectives are not interchangeable. [1]

The company's plan to increase HBM4 production at M15X by year-end and its statement that Yongin Phase 1 will enter full-scale operation next year are different stages: one describes a current ramp, the other future operation. Management's expectation that supply will remain tight through 2030 is a corporate forecast, not a measured market-wide shortage through that date. Contract concentration, fabrication yield, advanced packaging, power availability and customer concentration are all potential sources of downside to the apparent visibility. [1]

A second distinction concerns cash allocation. The company announced a shareholder-return programme of approximately **KRW 100 trillion** for 2026, combining dividends and repurchases, and said a previously announced KRW 40 trillion repurchase had been completed. A programme, a board resolution, a completed repurchase and cash actually distributed are not identical flows. The company also discontinued consideration of a Solidigm IPO, citing changed market conditions. Retaining financing inside the group can support capacity expansion, but does not prove that outside financing is universally inaccessible. [1]

## The same imported fuel shock produces different monetary choices

Australia's statistical office reported **4.3% annual CPI inflation** for September, up from **4.0% in August**. The trimmed-mean measure was **3.6%**, versus a revised **3.5%** in August. Housing prices rose **5.2%** over the year, transport **8.7%**, and automotive fuel **9.4% in September alone**. These observations locate the shock in both energy-sensitive expenditure and domestic cost categories; the revised trimmed-mean comparison must not be quoted using the unrevised August value. [3]

New Zealand's September-quarter inflation moved in the opposite direction, from **4.1% in June to 3.7% in September**. Its central bank nevertheless expects a temporary rise to **4.4% in December**, conditional on current fuel futures, and identifies weaker household spending as a reason to avoid premature tightening. Four policymakers voted to hold and three favoured a 25-basis-point rise. All agreed that tighter policy could become necessary; the split was over timing and the weight given to inflation persistence versus domestic activity. The central bank is not promising unchanged rates for the rest of the year. [2]

The Reserve Bank's minutes also flag a less visible interaction: expensive energy and global long-term yields coexist with opaque AI-related financing and the risk that strong export sectors fail to lift domestic employment and consumption. Its discussion of those global vulnerabilities is a central-bank risk assessment, not evidence that a particular borrower has defaulted. For New Zealand, agricultural export receipts can improve while fuel, imported equipment and consumer costs worsen; for Australia, stronger energy inflation can make domestic rate expectations more sensitive to the next data release. [2][3]

The comparison does not rank the quality of central banks. Different labour markets, currency exposures, price baskets and financial structures can rationally produce different choices even when imported fuel is a shared disturbance. The next policy statements must be read against each institution's own target and evidence, rather than interpreted as a coordinated global turn. [2][3]

## High Treasury yields have not stopped equity gains, but equity gains are not financing flows

The US Treasury's official par curve places the two-year yield at **4.70%**, the ten-year at **4.99%** and the thirty-year at **5.30%** on 27 October, compared with **4.68%, 5.00% and 5.31%** on 26 October. The ten-minus-two-year spread therefore narrowed from **32 to 29 basis points**. The official ten-year inflation-protected real yield declined from **2.66% to 2.64%**; subtracting it from the nominal yield gives an approximate **2.35-percentage-point** nominal-real difference, not a directly observed forecast of future inflation. Treasury curves use indicative market quotes and interpolation. [4][5]

US equities nevertheless advanced on 27 October: the **S&P 500 gained 0.60%**, the **Nasdaq 1.18%** and the **Russell 2000 1.2%**, according to Reuters' closing market account. The STOXX 600 gained **0.47%**. On the Asian morning of 28 October, Reuters reported the Korean Kospi more than **6%** higher intraday, Japan's Nikkei up **1.1%** and China's CSI 300 up **0.7%**. These observations span different trading sessions and should not be combined into a single synchronous global close. [7][8]

There are at least three competing interpretations. Strong expected earnings may offset higher discount rates for selected firms. Investors may be repositioning within equities without increasing their overall equity allocation. Or price appreciation may partly reflect expectations about AI financing announcements rather than realised returns. None is established by index direction alone. In particular, a rally in suppliers can coexist with tighter credit terms for less profitable data-centre developers. Disclosed debt issuance, credit spreads, customer prepayments and capital expenditure converted into operating revenue would discriminate between these explanations. [1][7][8]

The Investment Company Institute's latest available weekly release put US money-market fund assets at **USD 7.94 trillion** for 21 October, up **USD 21.49 billion** on the previous week. That is a dated change in reported fund assets, not a transaction-level record of capital rotating out of AI equities or Brazilian bonds. Its stock and valuation effects cannot be netted mechanically against equity-index changes. [9]

## Oil's retreat relieves one margin while leaving transport risk unresolved

Brent settled at **USD 113.88** and WTI at **USD 99.90** on 27 October, both down nearly two dollars as traders weighed diplomatic contacts in the Middle East. Those are futures settlement prices. Negotiations, political statements and reported attacks do not independently establish that a shipping corridor is safe or that refinery output has been restored. The physical test requires vessel movements, cargo loadings, insurance terms, refinery utilisation and delivered-product availability. [10]

The cross-asset implications differ by balance sheet. Lower crude can improve transport and petrochemical margins, and eventually household real incomes, if freight, exchange rates and taxes do not absorb the decline. It can reduce revenue expectations for some producers. Import-dependent economies such as India, Japan and New Zealand are exposed to the dollar price and shipping costs, not just the benchmark futures price. Higher oil can support some export receipts without ensuring a gain in aggregate welfare when fuel and fertilizer costs rise. [2][10]

```flow
Gulf energy and shipping disruption → crude and refined-fuel costs → imported inflation and real incomes → divergent central-bank decisions → FX, sovereign yields and credit
```

## Brazil's second session confirms a local adjustment, not global contagion

Brazil remains a material cross-asset case but no longer supplies the principal new mechanism. B3's own 27 October market report records the Ibovespa at **175,710.05**, down **0.51%**, with trading value of **BRL 71.2 billion**. The commercial dollar closed around **BRL 5.35**, down **0.97%** in the same report. The index declined even as some domestic names rebounded, because oil and iron-ore-linked constituents weighed on it. This is sector dispersion following Monday's much larger shock, not evidence of a broad and complete recovery. [11]

Reuters separately reported an easing in Brazilian rate futures and official comments reaffirming fiscal discipline. Statements by officials describe intended policy and institutional responses, not enacted spending measures or a verified improvement in debt sustainability. A bond buyback or auction cancellation can change immediate market liquidity and issuance composition without demonstrating a lasting shift in fiscal expectations. Foreign investor net flows, derivative margin changes and the depth of the local sovereign market remain necessary to distinguish temporary position unwinding from persistent capital withdrawal. [12]

The Brazilian episode is also a useful negative control for the global thesis. US and European equities gained while Brazil's benchmark remained weak, and the US ten-year yield barely moved. A country-specific shock cannot be relabelled as worldwide liquidation merely because several Brazilian assets fell together. Conversely, a calm US Treasury close does not eliminate the risk that high dollar yields will eventually constrain investment elsewhere. [4][7][11]

## A concentrated supplier boom faces its test in customer economics

The most consequential asymmetry is between **a supplier's contracted output** and **a customer's unproven long-run return on capital**. SK hynix has provided an observable earnings and procurement milestone, but investors cannot infer from it that every AI project will be connected to power on time, achieve high utilisation or generate enough revenue to cover financing costs. The other asymmetry is monetary: Australia faces rising headline and underlying inflation while New Zealand held rates narrowly despite the prospect of a renewed fuel-driven rise. Neither divergence requires a global recession or a universal equity selloff. [1][2][3]

Over the next several quarters, contracted memory volumes could protect the supplier's utilisation while squeezing buyers that cannot pass through computing costs. Alternatively, growing customer revenue could validate the capital expenditure and allow earnings to broaden beyond semiconductor vendors. The first case would show up in weaker customer free cash flow, credit terms or delayed projects; the second in realised service revenues, capacity utilisation and manageable debt service. Limited visibility into private financing structures makes this an open empirical test, not an investable certainty. [1][2][7]

The Federal Reserve's **27–28 October meeting** ends later today; the policy announcement and press conference are not yet observed at this cutoff. A less restrictive message could ease the financing side without resolving power and fabrication constraints. A more persistent inflation signal could preserve high real discount rates even while supplier earnings rise. The ECB and Bank of Japan decisions later in the week will provide further evidence of whether energy and domestic demand are producing differentiated policy responses. [6][7]

The strongest contrary evidence to a generalized funding squeeze is the combination of exceptional reported memory profits, rising US equities and nearly stable Treasury yields. The underappreciated risk is that long-term contracts shift rather than eliminate financial exposure: buyers secure components but may retain electricity, construction, customer-adoption and refinancing risks. The most informative next observations are **customer free cash flow versus AI capex, credit spreads and issuance terms, verified energy transport costs, and the next central-bank policy language**. None of the documented price moves alone proves a durable reallocation of global capital.
