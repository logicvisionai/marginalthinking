# Global Macro — A Fed pause, a Canadian hike and a steeper Treasury curve expose the cost of persistent energy inflation

**29 October 2026 — early Brasília edition. Information cutoff: 05:00 BRT (08:00 UTC).** The Federal Reserve and Bank of Canada decisions, US Treasury closing yields and US equity closes are from 28 October. The European Central Bank and Bank of Japan decisions and the US third-quarter GDP and September personal-income/inflation releases had not occurred at this cutoff; no result from those releases is assumed.

The new information is a split between the price of overnight money and the price of financing for years. On 28 October the Federal Reserve left its target range at **3.75–4.00%**, by a **10–2 vote**, while two members sought a quarter-point increase. Canada instead raised its policy rate by **25 basis points to 2.50%**, its first increase since July 2023. Meanwhile the US two-year Treasury yield declined **3 basis points** from 27 October, but the ten-year rose **6 basis points** and the thirty-year **7 basis points**. A policy pause has not prevented long-duration funding from becoming more expensive. [1][2][3]

That divergence matters more than another description of expensive capital. Canada's central bank explicitly linked its decision to the risk that persistent fuel costs spread to other prices; the Fed emphasized resilient activity and elevated inflation but chose to wait for more evidence. Oil inventory data show a tightening crude and distillate cushion alongside a build in gasoline stocks. Fund-flow estimates reveal a further distinction: net ETF issuance was positive while long-term mutual funds suffered redemptions in a *prior* reporting week. These are different signals of policy, physical supply and investor transactions, not one synchronized global retreat. [2][4][5][6]

The question for markets is whether energy-driven inflation forces additional short-rate increases while government and private investment demand keep long yields high, even if equity earnings remain resilient. Today's evidence supports that risk, not its inevitability. The European and Japanese decisions, and the US GDP and personal-consumption inflation releases, are still ahead. [1][3][7][8]

## The Fed's divided hold does not amount to easier financing

The FOMC's **10–2** decision kept the federal-funds target at **3.75–4.00%**. Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack preferred a **25-basis-point increase**. The official statement describes economic activity as expanding strongly, spending as resilient and inflation as elevated. It also maintains the framework of ample banking-system reserves. These are the committee's stated assessments, not independently measured new output or inflation statistics. The September decision had raised the target by 25 basis points; October's hold is therefore a change in the pace of tightening rather than a reversal. [1][9]

The implementation note sets interest on reserve balances at **3.90%**, the standing overnight repo rate at **4.00%**, and the reverse-repo offering rate at **3.75%**, effective 29 October. It also permits Treasury-bill purchases when appropriate to maintain ample reserves. Such operational liquidity management should not be mislabeled a new quantitative-easing programme or a promise to lower the policy rate. A change in reserve composition and an easing of credit conditions are separate propositions. [9]

The more revealing market observation is the Treasury curve. The US Treasury's daily par yields on **27 October** were **4.70% at two years**, **4.99% at ten years** and **5.30% at thirty years**. On **28 October** they were **4.67%**, **5.05%** and **5.37%**, respectively. The ten-year-minus-two-year spread widened from **29 to 38 basis points**. That is a **9-basis-point steepening** calculated from the two official daily prints. These are end-of-day par yields, not executable quotes at one simultaneous intraday instant. [3]

The move is consistent with investors demanding greater compensation for longer exposure to inflation, supply, fiscal uncertainty or duration, but this three-point series cannot separate those channels or establish a rise in the term premium. It is equally inappropriate to infer a large forced bond liquidation from a six-basis-point daily yield increase. The relevant asymmetry is observable: a short-rate hold and a softer two-year yield coexist with more expensive long-maturity finance. Mortgages, long-lived infrastructure and heavily financed data centres are more sensitive to the latter than to the overnight target alone. [3]

## Canada's first hike in three years turns imported energy into a domestic policy test

The Bank of Canada increased its overnight target from **2.25% to 2.50%**. Its 28 October statement estimates Canadian second-quarter growth at **3.3%**, projects around **2%** for the third quarter and expects inflation near **3.5%** by year-end. The bank's revised forecast puts average 2027 inflation at **3%**, a full percentage point above its July projection, with a return to **2% around mid-2028**. These are official forecasts, not realized outcomes. [2]

The policy transmission is unusually explicit. The bank says energy and refinery costs are raising firms' transport and input bills; some companies have absorbed costs in margins, but its surveys suggest wider pass-through next year. It also sees improved activity and business investment, especially in energy and machinery, while noting weak housing and unemployment near **6.5%**. It is responding to the *persistence and diffusion* of an external shock, not claiming it can manufacture more oil by raising rates. [2][10]

Canada's move also contrasts with New Zealand's **4–3 hold at 2.75%** on the same day. New Zealand's monetary authority judged that weak domestic spending and relatively anchored expectations justified waiting, despite fuel pressure. Australia's latest **September CPI of 4.3% year on year**, versus **4.0% in August**, and trimmed-mean inflation of **3.6%**, add another Pacific test of inflation persistence. The Australian monthly and quarterly underlying measures use different conventions; these observations cannot be spliced into a synthetic series. [4][11]

This is a policy divergence, not evidence that central banks have abandoned inflation targets. The appropriate comparison is the interaction of imported prices, domestic demand and the credibility of inflation expectations. Commodity-exporting regions may experience improved revenues while households face fuel bills; a common world oil price can therefore produce different domestic interest-rate decisions. [2][4][11]

## Oil stocks offer a narrower cushion than the gasoline headline suggests

For the US week ending **23 October**, the EIA's weekly data, as reported contemporaneously, showed commercial crude inventories down **1.9 million barrels to 410.6 million**, distillates down **1.5 million to 102.8 million**, and gasoline up **3.9 million to 209.2 million**. These are three separate stock categories; a gasoline build is not equivalent to a crude supply recovery. The reported crude-export increase and conflicting vessel-tracking estimates make a single weekly flow figure a weak basis for claims about permanently reopened routes. The EIA weekly release is the primary statistical programme; the figures were cross-checked against contemporary reporting because the current live EIA page did not expose the 28 October vintage consistently. [5][12]

The difference matters for prices paid by businesses. Distillate is relevant to freight, industrial activity and heating. Lower inventories do not by themselves prove a refinery outage or quantify the geopolitical premium in Brent. But they reduce the immediately available buffer if a physical interruption occurs. Reuters reported Brent settling around **US$115.32 per barrel** and WTI around **US$102.78** on 28 October, amid renewed concern about Middle Eastern supply. Those futures settlements are not direct measures of barrels lost or the volume of shipping through Hormuz. [5][13]

```flow
Middle East supply and shipping uncertainty → crude and refined-fuel availability → freight and input costs → firm margins or consumer prices → inflation expectations and central-bank choices
Higher policy-rate expectations + fiscal and private financing demand → sovereign yield curve → mortgage and infrastructure discount rates → investment timing and household demand
```

Canada's tightening makes the second-round channel observable as a policy response, even though the size of actual future pass-through is not yet measured. New Zealand's hold demonstrates a competing response: allow time for weak domestic demand to contain the shock. Neither response is a proof that the oil shock will last indefinitely. [2][11]

## A modest equity gain and ETF issuance do not prove that financing stress has disappeared

US stocks ended 28 October modestly higher: the **Dow +0.19%**, **S&P 500 +0.28%** and **Nasdaq +0.27%**, according to the contemporaneous Reuters close. European equities also registered a small positive move. The immediate equity response to the Fed hold therefore did not resemble a broad liquidation. But strong supplier earnings and AI-related capital spending can coexist with rising long-term discount rates and weak cash-flow economics for some downstream projects. Equity index performance alone cannot identify how many new data-centre projects were financed, on what terms, or at what future utilization. [13][14]

The Investment Company Institute's **28 October** publication adds genuine transaction evidence with a crucial lag. For the week ended **21 October**, estimated **long-term mutual-fund outflows were US$33.50 billion** and **ETF net issuance was US$45.82 billion**, yielding **US$12.32 billion of combined net inflows**. The two components are different fund structures and should not be called purchases by the institutions administering the funds. These figures predate the 28 October Fed decision and cannot explain that day's equity returns. [6]

The apparent contradiction between mutual-fund redemptions and ETF issuance is analytically useful: product migration, investor preference and market exposure can differ, and issuance is not proof that the fund sponsor made a proprietary directional bet. The ICI estimates cover a defined fund universe; they are neither all global capital flows nor real-time trading positions. Without issuer-level financing terms, primary credit spreads and complete ownership records, it would be unjustified to claim either a generalized credit seizure or that the AI investment cycle is fully funded. [6]

For emerging markets, the immediate risk is uneven. A high US ten-year yield can raise required returns for dollar borrowers and complicate local debt issuance, but the size depends on currency mismatches, maturity structure, reserve buffers and domestic policy. Brazil's earlier post-election repricing remains relevant background, not the central new mechanism on 29 October; no new Brazil-specific price or flow is used here to infer renewed capital flight. Smaller commodity importers may face simultaneous fuel-cost and dollar-financing pressure, while some exporters receive a terms-of-trade offset. [3][13]

## The next releases can distinguish resilience from an inflation-financing squeeze

The European Central Bank's **29 October** meeting and the Bank of Japan's scheduled decision had not been released at the information cutoff. The ECB deposit rate in force was **2.50%**; that is a prior setting, not a prediction of today's vote. The US Bureau of Economic Analysis schedules both the **third-quarter GDP advance estimate** and **September personal income and outlays** for **08:30 US Eastern time on 29 October**, after this edition. Any claim about those results would be premature. [7][8]

The first possibility is a relatively benign separation: economic growth and productivity remain resilient, energy prices stabilize and long yields reflect stronger real investment demand rather than worsening inflation expectations. A less benign path is persistent fuel and freight costs feeding broader prices just as public debt issuance and AI infrastructure absorb long-term savings. In that case the Fed could hold its short-rate target while households and investment projects already face tighter finance. The opposite risk is a sudden growth slowdown that weakens earnings, reduces financing demand and changes the case for additional tightening. [1][2][3][11]

The main conclusion is not that all central banks are tightening together. **Canada has acted, the Fed has paused with dissents, New Zealand has paused narrowly, and the US long end has repriced upward.** Modest equity gains and positive combined fund inflows are important contrary evidence to an indiscriminate risk-off thesis. The underappreciated risk is that a persistent energy-cost shock and large long-duration financing needs tighten household and corporate conditions without an additional Fed hike. The next tests are the **US GDP/PCE releases**, **ECB and BOJ decisions**, **the ten-year versus two-year Treasury spread**, and **distillate inventories and fuel pass-through**. No asset-price or policy path is treated as certain.
