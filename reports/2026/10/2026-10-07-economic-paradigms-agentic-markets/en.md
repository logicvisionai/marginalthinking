# From algorithmic markets to agentic markets: when software begins to act as an economic participant

Financial automation is moving from tools that rank, predict or execute instructions toward systems that can plan multi-step actions, use external tools and adapt with limited human intervention. That shift does not make established economics obsolete. It changes where agency sits inside the market.

The relevant economic question is no longer only whether an algorithm can estimate a price or execute an order faster than a person. It is whether autonomous systems can interpret information, choose actions, interact with other agents and initiate transactions in ways that alter market adjustment, delegation, accountability and systemic correlation.

Evidence in 2026 places this mechanism between experimentation and early institutional deployment. The Bank of England reports that trading firms still use more autonomous AI mainly for research, coding, surveillance and other lower-risk functions rather than fully autonomous trading. At the same time, the BIS, Bank of England and Bundesbank are building Project Logos specifically to study LLM-based portfolio managers in simulated markets. Supervisors in France describe financial firms as moving from AI that assists humans toward agents capable of initiative. The FSB and Basel Committee are developing governance and supervisory responses.

The economic paradigm is therefore not "AI replaces markets". It is a shift from human organizations using software to organizations delegating bounded economic action to software agents.

## What changed in the economic mechanism

| Earlier automation | Agentic system | Economic implication |
|---|---|---|
| Executes predefined trading rules | Can interpret context and select multi-step actions | Strategy becomes less fully specified ex ante |
| Produces forecasts for a human | Can combine forecast, tool use and transaction initiation | Decision and execution can converge |
| Firm-level model risk | Shared foundation models and infrastructure | Common dependencies can create correlated behavior |
| Human authorization at each step | Delegated authorization within limits | Principal-agent and liability boundaries become central |
| Slow institutional feedback | Machine-speed interaction among agents | Collective adjustment may outrun human supervision |

The mechanism can be described with familiar theory: delegation, principal-agent problems, information asymmetry, transaction costs, strategic interaction and externalities. What is new is the speed, scale and opacity at which these mechanisms may operate when agents share models, data providers or objectives.

## Markets may become more correlated before they become fully autonomous

The Bank of England's July 2026 Financial Stability Report notes that fully autonomous trading is not yet the dominant use case. That limitation is important. It prevents a speculative future scenario from being described as a current market structure.

But the Bank also identifies the system-level risk: if agents respond similarly to information or prompts, widespread adoption could increase correlated behavior and accelerate adjustment in stressed markets. Project Logos is designed around precisely that question.

```flow
Common models, data and prompts -> similar interpretation of new information -> similar portfolio actions -> faster correlated order flow -> larger short-term price and liquidity adjustment
Model diversity, constraints and human intervention -> heterogeneous responses -> damped correlation -> lower probability of synchronized agent behavior
```

This channel resembles older concerns about algorithmic herding, but generative agents add a different source of correlation. Traditional algorithms are often explicitly coded around narrow signals. LLM-based agents can share broad pretrained representations, common toolchains and similar reasoning patterns even when their exact instructions differ.

## The AI financing boom makes the question macro-financial, not only technical

The Bank of England estimates that AI-related companies represented around half of the S&P 500 by 2026, up from roughly one quarter in 2022. The exact classification is institution-specific, but the direction shows how concentrated AI exposure has become in major equity benchmarks.

This concentration matters because agentic adoption is developing inside a financial system already exposed to the capital cycle of AI infrastructure. BIS research describes the AI build-out as one of the largest technology investment booms in U.S. history and models a winner-take-most investment race in which contest externalities can induce overinvestment. Debt and circular financing can amplify fragility if expected AI revenues disappoint.

Agentic finance and AI financing are different mechanisms, but they can interact. A revaluation of AI assets can change the data and signals fed into automated systems; those systems can then accelerate portfolio adjustment. The result is a feedback loop between the financing of AI and AI-mediated financial decisions.

## Payments turn autonomous action into an authorization problem

Payments provide a clearer institutional boundary. Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller has argued that agentic payments raise questions that differ from conventional payment automation. If an AI agent can select a merchant, negotiate terms or initiate payment, institutions need rules for identity, authorization, spending limits, dispute resolution and liability.

This is a principal-agent problem in literal form. The human or firm is the principal; the software system acts within delegated authority. The cost advantage comes from reducing search, coordination and execution effort. The risk is that the action may be economically valid in a technical sense while still violating the principal's preferences, constraints or legal obligations.

The theory of the firm therefore becomes relevant alongside payments engineering. Transaction costs may fall when agents can search and negotiate continuously, but governance costs can rise because organizations must define what an agent may do, how its actions are audited and when a human must intervene.

## Supervisors are moving from model governance to system behavior

The FSB's 2026 consultation on responsible AI adoption focuses on governance across the AI lifecycle. The Basel Committee is discussing AI alongside growing leverage and interconnected financing around the AI ecosystem. French supervisors emphasize customer protection, cyber risk and the economic consequences of agents that can initiate actions autonomously.

The Bank of England pushes the question further by asking whether authorities can observe collective behavior, not merely validate individual models.

The distinction is important. A model can be safe in isolation while many similar models interacting in the same market produce unstable aggregate behavior. Financial regulation has confronted this type of composition problem before: individually rational liquidity management can become collectively destabilizing during runs or fire sales.

## Existing economic theory still explains much of the problem

Principal-agent theory explains delegation and misaligned objectives. Transaction-cost economics explains why firms automate search, contracting and execution. Market microstructure explains how faster responses affect liquidity and price formation. Game theory explains strategic interaction among adaptive agents. Financial-stability theory explains why individually rational actions can create collective externalities.

What changes is the empirical object. Human traders and firms are no longer the only entities directly implementing economic decisions. Software can become a delegated actor with persistent objectives, memory, tools and transaction access.

That requires models to represent the governance boundary between human principal and artificial agent, as well as common technological dependencies across agents.

## Evidence stages

| Mechanism | 2026 evidence stage | What is established | What remains uncertain |
|---|---|---|---|
| AI support in finance | operating scale | Widespread use in analytics, fraud, coding and decision support | Productivity and risk effects by function |
| Agentic payment workflows | early deployment / design | Institutions are defining authorization and governance problems | Scale, liability and consumer behavior |
| LLM portfolio managers | controlled experiment | Central banks are building simulation environments | Live-market behavior and systemic effects |
| Fully autonomous core trading | limited | Some experimentation and lower-risk uses | Whether it becomes material at market scale |
| AI financial-stability governance | implementation | FSB/Basel/central banks are adapting frameworks | Whether oversight can keep pace with capability |

## What would constitute a genuine paradigm change

A genuine shift would require autonomous agents to become material participants in allocation, pricing or contracting and to produce persistent outcomes that models centered on human decision-makers cannot explain without new assumptions.

If agents remain mostly productivity tools under tight human control, the result is better described as another layer of automation. If they acquire broad delegated authority and interact at scale, then the unit of economic agency changes enough to require new empirical and theoretical treatment.

The dividing line is observable: transaction share, degree of delegated authority, response correlation, intervention frequency, operational losses and effects on liquidity or pricing.

## What would weaken the thesis

The agentic-market thesis would weaken if progress in autonomy stalls, firms keep agents away from core financial decisions, or governance costs outweigh transaction-cost savings. It would also weaken if diverse models and controls prevent meaningful correlated behavior.

Conversely, evidence of common-agent behavior during stress, widespread autonomous transaction initiation or measurable changes in liquidity and price dynamics would strengthen it.
