# Italy: manufacturing depth, high public debt and the demographic limits of the euro-era growth model

Italy in 2026 is neither a simple stagnation story nor a straightforward export success. It combines one of Europe's deepest manufacturing systems, a persistent non-energy trade surplus, globally competitive machinery, pharmaceuticals, food, luxury goods, aerospace, shipbuilding and industrial districts with very high public debt, weak labour-productivity growth, rapid ageing, low labour-force participation and material dependence on imported energy.

The economy grew by 0.5% in 2025. In the second quarter of 2026 real GDP increased by 0.2% quarter on quarter and 1.0% year on year; Istat's acquired growth for the year stood at 0.8%. The IMF and European Commission nevertheless maintained more cautious full-year baselines of around 0.5%, reflecting energy-price pressure, external uncertainty and Italy's weak medium-term potential growth.

The central structural question is whether Italy can use the unusually large National Recovery and Resilience Plan to raise productivity before demographic contraction and interest costs absorb a growing share of the fiscal space. The plan is not only a temporary demand stimulus. Its more important function is institutional: justice reform, public-administration digitalisation, infrastructure, energy, skills and investment are intended to raise the economy's productive ceiling. The risk is that expenditure is completed while the reform mechanism does not persist after the programme ends.

## Political and institutional architecture

Italy is a parliamentary republic. Executive authority is exercised by the Council of Ministers, while the president of the Republic performs constitutional, representative and guarantor functions. Parliament is bicameral, with the Chamber of Deputies and Senate both participating in legislation and confidence in the government.

Giorgia Meloni has served as President of the Council of Ministers since 22 October 2022 and remained in office at the October 2026 evidence cutoff. Her government operates within a political system in which coalition durability matters for the implementation of multi-year fiscal and reform programmes.

The constitutional system also has a strong territorial dimension. Regions possess important responsibilities in healthcare, transport and administration, while municipalities and other local bodies implement much of the physical public-investment pipeline. National reform therefore depends on subnational execution capacity, which is uneven across the country.

## Unification produced a national state before it produced a fully integrated economy

Political unification in the nineteenth century joined territories with markedly different institutions, infrastructure, land systems, literacy, industrial structure and administrative capacity. Northern and central areas entered industrialisation with stronger commercial networks and, in some regions, closer links to continental European markets. Large parts of the South remained more agrarian and institutionally distinct.

The resulting North–South divide was not a single event frozen in 1861. It was reproduced through cumulative differences in infrastructure, education, industrial concentration, migration, public administration and private capital. Later policy narrowed some gaps but did not eliminate them.

This history matters because current regional inequality is not simply a distributional problem. It affects national labour supply, investment efficiency, population mobility and the ability to absorb EU funds.

## Post-war reconstruction created the modern industrial economy

After the Second World War, Italy moved through rapid reconstruction, industrialisation and urbanisation. The post-war "economic miracle" transformed a still heavily agrarian economy into a major industrial power.

The industrial triangle around Milan, Turin and Genoa became associated with large-scale manufacturing, finance and transport. At the same time, a second model developed across parts of the North-East and Centre: networks of small and medium-sized firms specialised in machinery, components, furniture, textiles, ceramics, food and other tradable goods.

These industrial districts became one of Italy's distinctive institutional forms. Dense supplier networks allowed small firms to share specialised labour, tacit knowledge and flexible subcontracting. The model generated export competitiveness without requiring every company to become a large vertically integrated corporation.

Its weakness is the other side of the same structure. Small firms can adapt quickly but often invest less in management, research, digitalisation and international scaling.

## European integration changed both market access and macroeconomic discipline

Italy was a founding participant in European integration and later adopted the euro. Membership gave firms access to the single market and eliminated exchange-rate risk with the country's largest trading partners.

The euro also changed the macroeconomic adjustment mechanism. Italy no longer has a national currency that can depreciate independently. Losses in competitiveness must therefore be addressed through productivity, wages, margins, taxation, energy costs or sectoral reallocation.

This makes structural productivity unusually important. A low-productivity economy inside a common currency can remain solvent and export successfully in specialised sectors, but aggregate income convergence becomes difficult if productivity does not diffuse beyond those sectors.

## The public-debt burden is large but its transmission is more complex than the headline ratio

The IMF estimates gross public debt at 137.1% of GDP in 2025 and around 138.2% in 2026 under its baseline. The European Commission's spring forecast was similar, at 138.5% for 2026 and 139.2% in 2027.

The ratio alone does not describe immediate financing pressure. Italy has a deep domestic financial system, substantial household financial wealth, euro-area central-bank architecture and an average residual maturity of government securities of roughly seven years in early 2026. A maturity structure of that length slows the pass-through of higher market yields into the average cost of the entire debt stock.

The constraint is cumulative rather than instantaneous. If nominal growth remains weak while interest costs rise, a larger primary surplus is required merely to stabilise the ratio. This narrows the room for infrastructure, education, defence and demographic expenditure.

## Fiscal consolidation improved credibility but does not remove the debt-growth arithmetic

The general-government deficit narrowed to 3.1% of GDP in 2025 and the primary balance moved to a surplus of 0.8% of GDP. The IMF baseline put the 2026 deficit at about 2.9% and the primary surplus at around 1.2%.

This improved market confidence and helped compress Italian sovereign spreads before renewed geopolitical volatility in 2026.

However, primary surpluses alone do not guarantee rapid debt reduction. The interest-growth differential matters. When nominal growth is low and refinancing costs rise, even a positive primary balance can coexist with a rising debt ratio.

A second complication is the delayed cash effect of past housing-renovation tax credits, including the Superbonus. Part of the debt increase in 2025–2027 reflects stock-flow adjustments from measures whose deficit impact was recognised earlier but whose financing cash cost materialises later.

## The National Recovery and Resilience Plan is a once-in-a-generation capital programme

Italy's Recovery and Resilience Plan is valued at €194.4 billion, including approximately €71.8 billion in grants and €122.6 billion in loans. It is equivalent to more than 9% of GDP and is the largest national plan under the EU Recovery and Resilience Facility.

As of 4 May 2026, €153.2 billion had been disbursed after fulfilment of 366 milestones and targets. IMF monitoring reported €116.9 billion spent by the end of March 2026. The gap between disbursement and realised expenditure demonstrates why absorption capacity matters as much as formal milestone completion.

The programme includes transport, digital infrastructure, public administration, justice, energy, education, health and industrial transition. Its macroeconomic value depends on whether completed projects generate durable productivity gains rather than only a temporary investment peak.

## The post-PNRR transition is becoming the most important medium-term policy problem

RRF milestones and targets were designed around a deadline in 2026. That creates a discontinuity: public investment is currently supported by an external European programme with explicit milestones, monitoring and performance-linked disbursement.

After that framework fades, Italy must preserve the institutional practices that improved execution. Without a post-PNRR mechanism, ministries and local administrations may revert to fragmented project selection, slower procurement and weaker evaluation.

The risk is therefore not simply a fiscal cliff. It is a governance cliff.

```flow
Large EU financing
  -> milestones + reforms + public investment
  -> higher construction, digital and infrastructure activity
  -> potential productivity gain

Programme completion
  -> lower exceptional EU-financed investment
  -> need for domestic prioritisation + project evaluation + fiscal space

If institutions persist
  -> productivity effects remain

If institutions fade
  -> investment impulse ends without equivalent potential-growth gain
```

## Productivity is the core constraint behind Italy's debt, wages and demographics

The IMF estimates medium-term potential growth at around 0.6%. Weak productivity is the main reason a country with advanced infrastructure, high household wealth and a large export sector can still experience slow aggregate income growth.

The problem is broad rather than confined to one region. Industrial and construction productivity improved in some periods, but economy-wide gains remain weak. Employment growth has recently outpaced output growth, especially in labour-intensive services.

The structural mechanisms include small firm size, limited managerial capacity, shortages of high-skilled labour, insufficient risk capital, regulatory complexity and slow diffusion of digital technologies.

Higher productivity would affect multiple constraints simultaneously: wages could rise without equivalent loss of competitiveness; tax revenue would expand; the debt ratio would be easier to stabilise; ageing costs would be easier to finance; and skilled emigration would become less attractive.

## Small firms are a source of specialisation and a barrier to scaling

Italy's production system is unusually dense with small and medium-sized enterprises. This allows niche specialisation and flexible production but can limit fixed investment in research, software, management systems and global distribution.

Some tax and regulatory provisions have historically changed at firm-size thresholds. This can create incentives to remain small even when expansion would raise productivity. The IMF has therefore emphasised reducing size-contingent distortions and improving access to risk capital.

The policy challenge is not to replace small firms with large firms. It is to allow productive firms to scale when expansion is economically efficient.

## Manufacturing remains a strategic national asset

Italy retains one of Europe's largest manufacturing bases. Machinery, metal products, vehicles and components, pharmaceuticals, chemicals, food, fashion, furniture, ceramics, aerospace and shipbuilding all contribute to exports.

Industrial production nevertheless remains cyclical and exposed to weak European demand and energy prices. In June 2026 industrial output was 0.6% below a year earlier, although the second quarter as a whole grew 0.4% relative to the first quarter and July subsequently rebounded.

The structural distinction is between manufacturing capacity and short-term factory output. A temporary industrial contraction does not erase the supplier networks, engineering skills and export relationships accumulated over decades. Conversely, historical manufacturing depth does not guarantee competitiveness if energy, capital and labour costs become persistently unfavourable.

## Industrial districts remain economically relevant even as technology changes their form

Traditional industrial districts were built around proximity: suppliers, specialised workshops, machinery producers, designers and exporters located near one another.

Digital supply chains, advanced automation and global sourcing reduce the importance of some local relationships but increase the value of others. Tacit engineering knowledge, rapid prototyping and supplier trust can remain difficult to replicate remotely.

The districts therefore face a transformation rather than simple disappearance. Their long-run viability depends on whether small firms can adopt automation, AI, energy-efficiency technologies and digital sales while preserving specialised production advantages.

## Exports demonstrate competitive depth but energy imports absorb part of the surplus

In June 2026 Italy recorded a goods trade surplus of €4.2 billion. Excluding energy, the surplus was €9.2 billion. In the first half of 2026 exports rose 4.5% year on year and imports 4.3%, leaving a surplus of roughly €24.5 billion.

This illustrates a central feature of the Italian external account. Manufacturing and other tradable sectors generate substantial foreign-exchange earnings, while imported energy reduces the net surplus.

The energy transition therefore has a competitiveness mechanism beyond emissions. Every unit of energy demand shifted toward domestically produced electricity can reduce exposure to external fuel-price shocks, subject to the cost of grids, storage and backup capacity.

## Germany remains a production partner as much as an export market

Italy's industrial relationship with Germany is embedded in European value chains. Italian firms supply machinery, components, chemicals, metals, food and specialised intermediate goods into German and wider European production networks.

This makes Germany's industrial cycle a transmission channel into Italian manufacturing. German investment and demand can support Italian suppliers; German industrial weakness can reduce orders even when Italian firms remain internationally competitive.

The same relationship creates an opportunity. German rearmament, infrastructure investment and industrial renewal can generate demand for Italian machinery, electronics, aerospace, transport and defence suppliers.

## Tourism is an export sector with unusually broad regional transmission

International tourism brings external demand directly into accommodation, food, transport, retail, cultural services and property markets. Unlike manufacturing exports, tourism revenue is often distributed across many small firms and municipalities.

The sector therefore supports employment in areas with limited industrial activity. It can also create congestion, seasonal work and housing pressure in high-demand cities and coastal areas.

Tourism should not be treated as a substitute for productivity-intensive industry. Its strategic value is diversification: it converts cultural and natural assets into export revenue while supporting local service economies.

## Food and agriculture operate through value added rather than commodity scale

Italy is not a global agricultural power by land area. Its advantage lies in processing, quality differentiation, geographic indications, branded food, wine and integration between agriculture, manufacturing and tourism.

This model increases value per unit of raw material but makes firms sensitive to water availability, climate risk, energy, packaging and logistics.

Agriculture also illustrates the regional character of the economy. Northern intensive agriculture, central wine and food clusters, and southern fruit, vegetables and specialty crops face different infrastructure and climate constraints.

## The automotive transition is an industrial-system problem, not a single-company problem

Italy's vehicle industry includes assembly, design, components, machinery, motorsport, commercial vehicles and specialised suppliers. Electrification changes the value chain because batteries, power electronics and software replace part of the traditional engine and transmission content.

The transition creates risk for suppliers specialised in combustion technologies. It also creates opportunities in industrial automation, charging equipment, power electronics and high-performance engineering.

The strategic question is whether the supplier network can migrate into new technologies quickly enough. Protecting final assembly without upgrading suppliers would not preserve the full industrial system.

## Pharmaceuticals, aerospace and shipbuilding broaden the industrial base

Pharmaceutical production has become an important high-value export activity. Aerospace and defence companies participate in European and transatlantic programmes, while Italian shipyards occupy strong positions in cruise vessels, naval construction and specialised maritime platforms.

These sectors matter because they are capital- and engineering-intensive and less exposed to the same cost structure as lower-value manufacturing.

They also connect civilian and defence industrial policy. Aerospace, sensors, electronics, engines and shipbuilding can serve both markets, allowing public defence demand to support industrial capabilities with civilian spillovers.

## Financial stability is stronger than the sovereign-debt headline suggests

Italy's banking system has improved markedly from the period following the euro-area sovereign crisis. The 2026 Financial Sector Assessment Program concluded that the system is broadly sound and resilient, supported by strong capital, liquidity and profitability.

Non-performing loan ratios remain low by recent historical standards. Bank credit to the private sector has begun to improve, while the countercyclical capital buffer remained at zero in the fourth quarter of 2026 because the credit-to-GDP gap was still negative.

The main macrofinancial vulnerability is the sovereign-bank connection. Italian banks still hold government bonds, even though the IMF notes that banks have reduced exposure while households and non-residents increased their holdings.

A rise in sovereign risk can therefore still affect bank funding and asset values, but the transmission is less concentrated than during the earlier euro crisis.

## Household financial wealth is an underappreciated stabiliser

Italian households historically maintain substantial deposits, bonds, insurance assets and other financial wealth. Household purchases of government securities have also increased.

This creates a domestic investor base for public debt and reduces exclusive dependence on foreign portfolio flows.

It does not make debt costless. Government bonds held by residents still represent future tax and interest obligations within the economy. But ownership structure affects rollover risk and the sensitivity of spreads to external capital flight.

## The euro removes currency risk but imports ECB monetary conditions

Italy cannot set an independent policy rate. Monetary conditions are determined by the European Central Bank for the euro area as a whole.

This has two effects. It eliminates the lira-era risk of recurrent national currency depreciation, but it means Italy cannot independently lower rates in response to weaker domestic growth.

High public debt therefore increases sensitivity to the euro-area rate cycle. A common increase in policy rates can raise Italy's refinancing cost more than that of lower-debt members even when the policy decision was driven by inflation elsewhere in the currency union.

## Energy dependence is one of the most direct external vulnerabilities

The IMF estimates that more than 70% of Italy's energy consumption is generated from imported fossil fuels, a higher dependence than the euro-area average. This makes oil and gas prices a direct channel from geopolitical shocks into inflation, household purchasing power and industrial competitiveness.

The 2026 Middle East conflict demonstrated this mechanism. Energy-price increases pushed headline inflation higher even as domestic demand remained modest.

Reducing fossil-fuel dependence therefore improves both climate performance and macroeconomic resilience.

## The electricity system is changing faster than total energy dependence

Italy consumed about 312.2 TWh of electricity in 2025. Domestic generation covered about 85% of demand and net imports the remaining 15%.

Renewable installed capacity reached 81.7 GW, or 56% of total installed generation capacity, by the end of 2025. Solar alone reached 43.7 GW and generated more than 45 TWh during the year.

Actual annual electricity supply remains more mixed. Thermal generation still represents a large share, and natural gas generated 126.9 TWh in 2025, roughly four-fifths of thermoelectric output.

## Storage and grid expansion determine how much renewable capacity becomes usable energy

By May 2026 renewable installed capacity had risen to about 86.4 GW, including 46.1 GW of solar and 13.9 GW of wind. Energy-storage installations totalled roughly 19.2 GWh of capacity.

Capacity additions alone do not measure system transformation. Solar output is concentrated in daylight hours, while hydro varies with weather and wind is intermittent.

Transmission, interconnection, flexible gas generation, demand response and storage determine how much renewable electricity can be integrated without curtailment or reliability problems.

The north-south structure of the grid is especially important because renewable resources, industry and demand are not distributed identically across the country.

## Ports give Italy a geographic advantage in Mediterranean logistics

Italy sits between continental Europe, the central Mediterranean, North Africa and the Suez route. Ports such as Genoa, Trieste, Gioia Tauro and others connect industrial regions with maritime trade.

This geography creates an opportunity to serve as a logistics and energy gateway into Europe. It also exposes the economy to disruption in the Red Sea, Suez Canal and Mediterranean security environment.

Port competitiveness depends on more than quay capacity. Rail links, customs efficiency, inland terminals and connection to northern European production centres determine whether geographic position becomes economic advantage.

## Rail and public infrastructure are productivity policy

High-speed rail changed the effective economic distance among several major cities, while freight corridors connect northern industry to European networks.

Infrastructure gaps remain more pronounced in parts of the South and islands. PNRR spending attempts to reduce some of these differences.

The productivity mechanism is straightforward: faster and more reliable transport expands labour markets, reduces inventory and freight costs, and raises the number of economically viable locations for firms.

Infrastructure therefore matters not only through construction demand but through the lower operating costs it creates after completion.

## The Mezzogiorno remains a national growth constraint and an underused capacity reserve

The South and islands have lower employment, weaker infrastructure and lower productivity on average than northern regions. Yet 2025 data showed slightly faster GDP and employment growth in the South than in the Centre-North.

That short-term outperformance does not mean the structural gap has closed. It does show that the region is not mechanically condemned to stagnation.

The Mezzogiorno is economically important because Italy cannot offset demographic ageing while leaving a large share of its working-age population underemployed. Raising participation and firm productivity in the South would expand national output without requiring equivalent population growth.

## Employment has reached historic highs while participation remains weak

Employment reached around 24.4 million in mid-2026. In August the employment rate was 63.0% and unemployment 6.2%, with the number of employed still 1.2% higher than a year earlier.

Low unemployment can therefore coexist with labour-supply weakness. Italy's employment rate remains below that of several northern European peers because a large share of the working-age population is outside the labour force.

This distinction matters for ageing. The potential supply of labour depends on participation, not only unemployment.

## Female and youth participation are large untapped labour reserves

The IMF repeatedly identifies low participation among women and young people as a structural constraint.

Childcare availability, tax treatment of second earners, wage levels, regional job opportunities and school-to-work transitions all influence participation.

The effect is macroeconomic. Higher female employment raises household income and tax revenue, reduces the dependency burden and increases the number of skilled workers available to firms.

For youth, the issue is also migration. Weak early-career wages and limited high-skill opportunities can turn education investment into human-capital exports when graduates leave the country.

## Demography is moving from a social issue to a binding production constraint

At 30 June 2026 Italy had approximately 58.94 million residents. In 2025 only about 355,000 births were recorded against roughly 652,000 deaths. Total fertility fell to 1.14 children per woman.

Positive migration largely offset the natural population decrease. Net international migration in 2025 was about +296,000.

The long-run constraint is the working-age population. Fewer workers must finance pensions, healthcare, debt service and public investment unless participation or productivity rises substantially.

## Ageing makes pension arithmetic central to fiscal sustainability

The IMF estimates pension expenditure at about 15.2% of GDP in 2025 and projects it to peak around 17% near 2040 under current demographic trends.

Italy has already implemented major pension reforms over several decades. Later retirement ages and contribution-based formulas reduce some long-run liabilities, but political pressure to soften earlier reforms periodically returns.

Pension sustainability cannot be separated from productivity and labour participation. A larger contribution base makes the same benefit promises easier to finance.

## Migration helps demography but does not automatically solve the skills problem

Italy receives substantial immigration while also experiencing emigration of some young and highly educated Italians.

These flows are not symmetric. Incoming migrants may initially be concentrated in lower-wage services, agriculture, logistics, construction and care work, while emigrants can include university graduates and skilled professionals.

Migration can therefore stabilise population while the skill composition of the labour force still deteriorates.

Integration, credential recognition, language acquisition, vocational education and pathways into higher-productivity employment determine whether migration raises long-run potential output.

## Education and skills are the transmission channel from technology to productivity

Adoption of artificial intelligence, automation and digital platforms will not produce equal gains across firms. Large companies can purchase technology and hire specialised staff more easily than micro-enterprises.

Italy therefore faces a diffusion problem. The aggregate productivity payoff depends on whether medium and small firms can reorganise processes, not merely buy software.

STEM education, vocational training, managerial quality and lifelong learning are complementary capital. Without them, technology investment can remain concentrated in a narrow group of firms.

## Defence spending is increasing, but measurement depends on the accounting definition

NATO estimated Italian core defence expenditure at 2.10% of GDP in 2026, approximately €48.3 billion under the Alliance's harmonised definition. Around 24.5% of the total was allocated to major equipment and related R&D.

Italian parliamentary material also reports a much narrower COFOG defence figure of about €26.2 billion for 2026 under domestic functional budget classification.

These figures are not contradictory once the perimeter is specified. NATO methodology includes categories not identical to the national COFOG budget line.

```flow
National budget classification
  -> narrower COFOG defence perimeter
  -> about EUR 26.2bn in 2026

NATO harmonised methodology
  -> broader eligible defence expenditure
  -> about EUR 48.3bn / 2.10% of GDP in 2026

Do not add the two figures:
they measure overlapping expenditure under different definitions.
```

Italy therefore illustrates why defence-spending comparisons require methodological discipline.

## Rearmament creates an industrial opportunity and a fiscal trade-off

Italy has substantial aerospace, shipbuilding, electronics, helicopter, radar, missile and vehicle capabilities. Higher European defence spending can increase orders and support R&D.

The fiscal opportunity cost remains real. Italy is simultaneously trying to reduce debt, sustain pensions, complete infrastructure investment and meet NATO commitments.

Domestic production changes the macroeconomic effect. A euro spent on imported equipment has a different impact from a euro spent on domestic production that creates wages, exports and intellectual property.

The relevant indicator is therefore not only the defence-spending ratio but also domestic value added and the persistence of civilian spillovers.

## Italy's Mediterranean position is both strategic asset and exposure

Italy's geography gives it direct interests in North Africa, the Balkans, the central Mediterranean, maritime trade and energy routes.

Gas pipelines and LNG infrastructure connect Italy to Algeria, Libya and global suppliers. Migration routes across the Mediterranean affect domestic politics and European burden-sharing. Naval and coast-guard capacity therefore has both security and economic functions.

Italy can act as a bridge between continental European markets and Mediterranean energy and logistics. That role depends on political stability in neighbouring regions and on infrastructure capable of moving energy and goods northward.

## The EU constrains policy and amplifies capacity at the same time

European fiscal rules constrain national expenditure growth and debt dynamics. State-aid and competition rules limit some industrial-policy instruments.

The same European system gives Italy access to the single market, ECB monetary architecture, common trade policy and exceptionally large recovery financing.

This is not a simple loss-of-sovereignty story. Italy exchanges some national discretion for shared institutions that lower currency risk and increase financing and market scale.

The effectiveness of that exchange depends on domestic implementation capacity.

## Institutional stability matters because reforms require long execution horizons

Italy historically experienced frequent government turnover even when administrative and parliamentary continuity persisted.

The Meloni government has lasted longer than many recent Italian administrations, increasing continuity during the final PNRR implementation phase.

Political duration alone does not guarantee reform quality. What matters is whether changes in justice, public administration, taxation, permitting and competition survive beyond a single cabinet and become ordinary institutional practice.

## Baseline scenarios

### Scenario 1: PNRR conversion into durable productivity gains

Justice, administrative and infrastructure reforms remain operational after programme completion. Private investment follows public investment, digital adoption spreads through SMEs, labour participation rises and energy dependence falls. Potential growth moves materially above the current low baseline, making debt reduction easier.

### Scenario 2: stable low-growth equilibrium

Italy completes most projects and maintains fiscal credibility but productivity remains weak. Employment stays high, migration prevents rapid population decline and debt stabilises only slowly. The economy remains wealthy and export-capable but continues losing relative income against faster-growing peers.

### Scenario 3: post-PNRR investment and reform cliff

Exceptional EU-financed investment falls faster than private investment rises. Project-management disciplines weaken, productivity gains disappoint and public debt remains near 140% of GDP. Higher defence and ageing expenditure further compress civilian investment.

### Scenario 4: energy and geopolitical shock

A renewed external energy shock raises gas and oil prices, weakening household purchasing power and energy-intensive manufacturing. Inflation rises while growth falls. Sovereign spreads widen and fiscal consolidation becomes harder, exposing the interaction between energy dependence and high debt.

## What would strengthen the structural baseline

- sustained labour-productivity growth above the pre-2026 trend;
- continuation of PNRR governance practices after EU disbursement ends;
- faster civil-justice and insolvency resolution;
- higher private R&D and risk-capital investment;
- productive SMEs scaling without losing export specialisation;
- higher female and youth participation;
- successful integration of migrants into higher-productivity sectors;
- sustained reduction in the energy import bill;
- grid, storage and renewable capacity translating into lower industrial energy costs;
- higher South-to-North convergence in employment and productivity;
- stable primary surpluses without cutting productive investment;
- defence expansion with high domestic value added and export potential.

## What would weaken the structural baseline

- productivity returning to near-zero growth despite high investment;
- sharp decline in public investment after PNRR completion;
- debt remaining near 140% of GDP while refinancing costs rise;
- renewed widening of sovereign spreads and bank-sovereign exposure;
- persistent emigration of skilled young workers;
- fertility decline combined with weaker net migration;
- low participation among women and youth remaining unchanged;
- energy shocks repeatedly raising industrial costs and inflation;
- insufficient transmission and storage delaying renewable integration;
- deterioration in Germany and core European manufacturing demand;
- defence commitments financed mainly by reduced civilian investment;
- widening regional divergence after the current southern employment improvement.

## Evidence limitations

GDP, employment, trade and demographic figures for 2026 are provisional and subject to revision. Monthly and quarterly movements should not be interpreted as structural trends without longer series.

Public-debt forecasts differ between the IMF, European Commission and national authorities because of different forecast dates, growth assumptions and treatment of stock-flow adjustments. This dossier preserves the source-specific baselines instead of presenting one number as certain.

PNRR disbursement is not equivalent to final expenditure or completed productive capital. Milestones, cash spending and economic impact measure different stages of implementation.

Defence expenditure is particularly sensitive to accounting perimeter. NATO's harmonised definition and Italy's COFOG national-budget classification are not directly comparable and must not be added together.

Electricity capacity is not equivalent to annual generation. Installed solar and wind capacity can increase faster than usable renewable output if transmission, storage or balancing capacity lags.

Regional categories conceal substantial variation within the North, Centre, South and islands. A national dossier cannot replace region-specific analysis where local institutions and industrial structures are material.
