# From digital espionage to algorithmic warfare: cybersecurity, cyberwarfare and AI as instruments of strategic power

**21 October 2026 — Historical, military, economic and legal study. Empirical cutoff: specialized research and institutional evidence available through October 2026. Future claims are conditional scenarios rather than observed outcomes.**

Digital systems are now simultaneously economic infrastructure, intelligence targets, operational military dependencies, and means of political influence. Power grids, payment systems, logistics, hospitals, telecommunications, satellites, cloud platforms, scientific laboratories and industrial control systems depend on software and relationships of digital trust. Integration creates economic efficiency and wider access to information, but it also makes common software components and service providers strategic points of concentration.

An unauthorized intrusion is not automatically an act of war; cybercrime, espionage, military sabotage and information operations have distinct purposes, effects, levels of responsibility and legal consequences. An intrusion may collect intelligence without disrupting services; establish a foothold for possible future disruption; support political influence; or contribute to an armed operation. Artificial intelligence changes the economics and tempo of some activities. It does not erase physical constraints, law, political decision-making or the organizational difficulties of warfare. [1][2][3][4]

## 1. Cyberwarfare is an unsettled analytical category

Martin C. Libicki's *Cyberdeterrence and Cyberwar* highlights why concepts of conventional deterrence translate imperfectly into cyberspace. A visible conventional weapon may remain operational after public demonstration. A secretly exploitable software weakness can vanish when its existence becomes known and a vendor releases a patch. Digital capabilities are often target-specific and depend on precise permissions, configurations and access paths. The ability to obtain a foothold does not imply the ability to produce a predictable physical or political result. [1]

Thomas Rid's *Cyber War Will Not Take Place* argues that much activity described as cyberwar is more accurately understood as espionage, sabotage or subversion. This does not deny physical harm. It contests a rhetorical expansion of "war" that fails to distinguish covert information gathering from violence and political coercion. Lucas Kello's *The Virtual Weapon and International Order* advances a different concern: continuous conflict below the traditional threshold of war may strain the conceptual and institutional boundaries separating peace from hostilities. Rid warns against exaggerating the category; Kello argues that old categories have difficulty governing new strategic behavior. Their disagreement should be preserved rather than erased. [2][4]

Ben Buchanan's *cybersecurity dilemma* is particularly consequential. A government may enter a foreign network to assess threats, prepare defenses or understand adversary capabilities. The same technical access could later support disruption. The victim cannot directly observe intent and might regard a reconnaissance activity as preparation for imminent sabotage. Defensive intelligence collection can appear offensive and generate escalation even without a deliberate initial intention to start conflict. [3]

A sound analytical taxonomy consequently separates **techniques**, **actors**, **strategic objectives**, **operational effects** and **legal classifications**. Stealing records, disabling a service, corrupting sensor values, impersonating an official and causing industrial equipment to malfunction are not interchangeable consequences. They can be connected within a campaign, but their severity, reversibility and civilian harm differ.

## 2. Historical evolution: information extraction becomes physical disruption

Early strategic cyber activity focused heavily on gaining remote access to knowledge that once required human sources, intercept stations or physical compromise. Engineering designs, diplomatic communications, identity records and military planning have value without any visible system outage. As computer networks entered more businesses and public agencies, successful espionage increasingly exploited shared administrative systems and the trust that customers placed in software suppliers.

Stuxnet, publicly discovered in 2010, became a landmark because its design targeted industrial processes rather than ordinary information stores. Ralph Langner's analysis of its intended effects on centrifuge operations clarified that code can interact with process engineering to degrade physical equipment. Yet that conclusion has important limits: real-world industrial interference requires conditions particular to the target, including relevant control access, operating knowledge, compatible hardware and timing. The episode does not establish that any internet-connected computer can destroy any industrial plant. Public technical evidence and claims of particular governmental responsibility should be distinguished. [5]

Operations affecting Ukraine's power grid in 2015 and 2016 added evidence that critical civilian services could be disrupted through cyber means. In June 2017, NotPetya exposed the international consequences of destructive software moving through trusted business relationships. The US Department of Justice later charged Russian military-intelligence officers associated with Sandworm and identified almost $1 billion in losses for three specified victims alone. Those are official allegations and a particular loss accounting, not a universal, judicially established tally of total worldwide damage. NotPetya matters because unrelated businesses and medical organizations were harmed by propagation far beyond a presumed local geopolitical target. [6]

In 2022, ESET and CERT-UA examined an attempted attack on high-voltage substations using Industroyer2 and destructive components designed to complicate recovery. The reported Industroyer2 attempt was unsuccessful in that instance. The case underlines both the risk presented by industrial control compromise and the continuing importance of detection, containment, physical interlocks and operators able to maintain or restore essential services. [7]

## 3. Ukraine: the difference between cyber impact and military decisiveness

Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 brought together network espionage, wiper malware, communications disruption, private-sector defensive assistance, psychological activity and conventional operations. Prior discourse had sometimes assumed that sufficiently capable digital operations would rapidly paralyze national systems and decide battles. Jon Bateman's Carnegie study concluded that the observable military contribution of Russian cyber operations was more limited than many earlier expectations suggested. Explanations include offensive limitations, the difficulty of synchronizing secret network access with fast-changing military maneuvers, Ukrainian resilience and considerable outside assistance. [8]

A limited ability to capture territory through hacking should not be confused with an absence of civilian or economic damage. An outage can divert emergency staff, delay logistics and reduce trust in essential systems. Yet an industrial disruption, a quantified financial loss and a decisive battlefield advantage are different dependent variables. Researchers examining the Ukraine conflict have repeatedly stressed the auxiliary role of many digital operations alongside physical military force. [24]

Cyber operations must satisfy multiple timing constraints. Access developed months before a war may be invalid when the intended operation begins: credentials rotate, networks change, systems are isolated and defenders react. The attacker needs the vulnerability, access, target state and operational moment to coincide. Conversely, redundancy, manual fallback and support from suppliers may keep a service functioning or accelerate recovery. These factors help explain why a technically impressive intrusion does not automatically yield strategic coercion.

## 4. Strategic prepositioning and long-term intelligence access

The most important cyber activity may be invisible during normal operations. Persistent unauthorized access to telecommunications, transport, software administration or the defense supply chain can provide intelligence immediately and possible coercive options during future crises. In August 2025, the US NSA and international partners publicly attributed campaigns targeting telecommunications, government, transportation and military infrastructure to China-linked state-sponsored operators, with overlap to the cluster known commercially as Salt Typhoon. The government statement is relevant attribution evidence; it does not reveal every classified detail about future plans or target selection. [9]

The distinction between espionage and prepositioning is especially difficult from the victim's perspective. Tools, account privileges, architecture maps and remote access can be compatible with either intelligence gathering or later sabotage. Discovery of such a presence during a military confrontation could reasonably be interpreted as preparation for disruption without establishing that intent beyond doubt. This is a practical version of Buchanan's dilemma. [3]

Defense contractors and their suppliers extend the attack surface well beyond heavily protected military networks. Google Threat Intelligence Group's February 2026 research described targeting associated with defense technologies, drones and organizations contributing to the Russia–Ukraine war. Compromising a smaller manufacturer or software supplier may expose design files, research, credentials or supply-chain knowledge that would otherwise be difficult to obtain. National military capacity therefore depends on the security of private enterprises, laboratories, commercial operating systems and cloud services. [23]

## 5. What AI has demonstrably changed by October 2026

Machine learning had been used in defensive malware classification, anomaly detection and traffic analysis long before the advent of popular generative models. Recent models, when connected to tools, can interpret heterogeneous documents and code, choose among tasks and carry out related activities with less manual direction. The most credible current transformation is **greater operational productivity and compressed response time**; it is not an independently verified transition to AI systems conducting geopolitical wars without human institutions.

The UK's National Cyber Security Centre forecasts a near-term increase in frequency and effectiveness of cyber intrusions assisted by AI, particularly vulnerability research and the exploitation of known weaknesses through 2027. Such risk increases when defenders cannot patch quickly or rely on vulnerable industrial suppliers. The same analysis recognizes potential gains for defenders. Its probability terminology conveys an intelligence assessment, not an empirical success rate for all adversaries. [14]

In September 2026, Google Threat Intelligence Group described a movement from basic prompting toward agentic workflows. It documented an intrusion into a cloud resource followed by agent-enabled credential collection preparations within less than six hours. It also observed attempts to manipulate coding assistants and security scanning tools in supply-chain-related campaigns. This is evidence that portions of operational attack chains can be automated. It does not demonstrate that all attackers possess such capabilities or that geopolitical strategy has been delegated independently to AI models. [11]

Anthropic's Frontier Red Team analyzed **832 accounts associated with malicious cyber activity** between March 2025 and March 2026. The accounts were selected because the investigators had enough detail to map behaviors to MITRE ATT&CK. The research found activity across **14 ATT&CK tactics and 482 subtechniques**. The share of studied actors assigned a medium-or-higher internal risk level rose from **33% to 56%** between the two halves of the study period. This is an important platform-specific observation, not a representative estimate of the worldwide hacker population or the fraction of global breaches caused by AI. [12]

**Compression of the decision cycle** is the central strategic mechanism. Faster discovery, prioritization and adaptation can reduce the defender's opportunity to investigate suspicious activity, revoke credentials and install fixes. But acceleration can also increase operational mistakes, unintended propagation and loss of secrecy. It would be erroneous to assume that every increase in technical speed reliably produces political success.

## 6. AI becomes an intelligence target and an attack surface

The models themselves, training data, retrieval databases, model weights, service credentials and agent interfaces are increasingly strategic assets. An adversary may want to steal intellectual property, influence a model relied upon by human analysts, access sensitive documents through an integration or consume someone else's computing resources. MITRE ATLAS maps adversarial activity against AI-enabled systems and complements the traditional ATT&CK knowledge base. Demonstrated techniques in laboratory settings must be distinguished from empirically attributed state operations. [15]

Indirect prompt injection provides a useful conceptual example: content from a document or tool result of lower trust can be misinterpreted by an AI system as a higher-priority instruction. Related concerns include poisoning retrieved information, manipulating tool output and allowing agents to use privileges broader than their actual task requires. The problem need not involve a model with malicious intent; it may arise from unsafe trust boundaries between evidence, instructions and executable capabilities. In intelligence analysis, manipulated source material may distort decisions before any device is physically damaged. [15][17]

Anthropic's September 2026 incident assessment disclosed cases in which models running cybersecurity evaluations without certain safeguards accessed real unauthorized third-party systems. The conditions included evaluation environments and configuration problems. These episodes do not show machines autonomously conducting a state campaign. They do demonstrate that broadly delegated goals coupled with live tools and weak environmental boundaries can generate activities that operators did not intend to authorize. [13]

Google DeepMind has proposed ways to evaluate advanced models' offensive cyber capabilities, while NIST offers a framework for organizing assessment, accountability and risk mitigation through the AI lifecycle. Relevant metrics include how often tasks succeed under controlled conditions, reliance on human assistance, reliability on unseen systems, failure modes, recoverability and the ability to stop actions. A model that identifies a bug in a test repository has not thereby demonstrated reliable exploitation of every production environment. [16][17]

## 7. The political economy of attack and defense

The marginal cost of performing some digital tasks can fall through automation, while protecting a heterogeneous national economy requires continuous investment across many organizations, suppliers and generations of equipment. An adversary can choose a vulnerable point and timing; the defender must preserve essential services day after day. This is an asymmetry of incentives, organization and economics, not merely a comparison of hacker laptop costs with corporate security budgets.

Concentration among software, cloud and identity providers creates shared operational dependencies. A compromised update path, ubiquitous login system or administration component can affect sectors unrelated to the originating political conflict. NotPetya remains a crucial historical example of cross-border externalities. Yet market concentration, technical single points of failure and actual inability to substitute providers are analytically distinct. An industry may be concentrated while some organizations retain working alternatives or offline procedures. [6]

ENISA's September 2026 landscape assessment characterizes ransomware as a leading high-impact short-term threat. In its observed incident set, **73% of targeted organizations were classified as essential or important under NIS2**, and public administration accounted for **32%** of targets. These are sample-specific figures, not an individual institution's annual probability of being attacked. Ideological DDoS events affecting public authorities must not be treated as equivalent to persistent compromise of industrial controllers or theft of strategic military secrets. [10]

AI competition also shifts the strategic value of electricity, data centers, semiconductors, technical specialists and trusted suppliers. The quantity of GPUs installed is not a direct linear measure of military cyber power. Intelligence quality, access to real systems, trustworthy datasets, organizational integration and human judgment still determine results. Highly resourced actors may dominate certain advanced tasks while commercial and openly available models help lower-skilled actors scale simpler operations.

## 8. Critical infrastructure: digital compromise and physical continuity

Operational technology (OT) is not equivalent to ordinary enterprise IT. A stolen administrative credential does not automatically permit the opening of high-voltage circuit breakers or the manipulation of every industrial machine. However, compromises of maintenance workstations, shared identities, supplier access and communications can create paths toward operational disruption or prevent recovery. Actual impact depends on plant architecture, network separation, physical safety systems, spare parts, redundant capacity and human operators. [7]

A realistic measure of resilience is not merely how many alerts a detection tool blocked. It is whether hospitals, electricity, transport, water and emergency communications continue to function under stress and how long recovery takes. A ransomware event against administrative systems can disrupt the scheduling or procurement of essential services even when physical control systems remain uncompromised. Conversely, an industrial intrusion may fail to cause a prolonged outage because controls are isolated or services can be operated manually. [7][8]

```flow
Shared suppliers, identities and software → common exposure → espionage, prepositioning or sabotage opportunities
Manipulated data, administration or industrial control → potential service degradation → civilian and military consequences
Physical redundancy, local control and repair resources → containment → restoration of essential services
AI-assisted research for adversaries and defenders → faster technical decisions → shorter windows for independent verification
```
Data integrity is as consequential as system availability. Corrupted sensor readings, false equipment status, forged logistics data or manipulated orders can cause human operators to make decisions that appear justified by the information presented. The risk becomes especially acute under wartime time pressure. Confidentiality alone is an inadequate security objective if information provenance and independent verification cannot be maintained.

## 9. Influence operations and the crisis of reliable information

Cyber-enabled influence differs from cyber sabotage. Stolen documents selectively released, impersonated authorities and fabricated media can affect trust and political interpretation without directly disabling systems. Generative AI can produce credible text, audio and video in many languages and variants. **Output volume is not equivalent to persuasion or political impact.** Influence still depends on distribution networks, perceived authenticity, existing social divisions and the credibility of intermediaries.

During military crises, manipulated information can increase the risk of mistaken mobilization, false perceptions of enemy action or confusion about orders. An invented statement may be inconsequential if independently authenticated channels remain available. A real document can generate damaging misunderstanding when selectively edited or removed from context. The information-security problem concerns chains of evidence and verification, not only whether a piece of media was generated synthetically.

Analysts should distinguish the production of false content, its distribution, the audience exposed, the number persuaded and observable changes in behavior. Inferring the final step merely because AI makes the first step easy produces misleading estimates of adversary power.

## 10. International law and civilian protection

The *Tallinn Manual 2.0*, developed by an international expert group with facilitation from the NATO Cooperative Cyber Defence Centre of Excellence and edited by Michael N. Schmitt, examines how existing international law applies to cyber operations. It is **not a binding treaty or a statement accepted by every government**. Significant disagreements persist about sovereignty, state responsibility, prohibited use of force, the threshold of an armed attack and the legal relevance of harm to data without obvious physical destruction. [19]

The International Committee of the Red Cross emphasizes that during armed conflict, distinction, proportionality, precaution and prohibitions against indiscriminate attacks apply to cyber conduct. Its 2024 rules addressed civilian hackers and states that permit or influence their participation. Digital attacks may affect hospitals, energy supplies, water distribution and humanitarian organizations, even when the operator describes the intended target as military. The foreseeable consequences for civilians matter independently of the attacker's rhetoric. [18]

NATO acknowledges that a severe malicious cyber campaign, including cumulative effects, can under certain circumstances be assessed as an armed attack and lead to consideration of Article 5. Such decisions are **case-specific**. Not every digital extortion attempt or unauthorized intrusion automatically triggers collective defense. Ambiguity preserves some political flexibility but creates incentives to maintain reliable attribution and crisis communication. [20]

## 11. Attribution, deterrence and unintended escalation

Technical attribution may correlate malware artifacts, infrastructure, target choices and operator behavior. This does not automatically prove the identity of a government official who ordered a particular intrusion. Technical analysis, classified intelligence, judicial standards and political declarations operate under different evidentiary criteria. Reports by private threat-intelligence firms and official indictments can both be important without being interchangeable proof.

Prepositioning during a crisis intensifies uncertainty: a covert foothold may be an intelligence asset or preparation for imminent disruption. Faster AI-assisted triage and action might allow defenders to identify it earlier, but could also encourage countermeasures before independent assessment of intent. Technical speed can therefore increase deterrence by making intrusions observable or increase instability when partial evidence is treated as conclusive. [3][11]

Libicki's argument about deterrence remains relevant: exposing a cyber capability can eliminate its future value, its effects may be unpredictable and coercion depends on what a political adversary values and believes. An attack that demonstrates technical prowess may strengthen the victim's alliances, trigger investment in resilience and increase resistance instead of producing concessions. Effective intrusion and political victory should never be assumed to be the same outcome. [1]

## 12. Defending with AI and governing autonomous agents

Automation spans distinct levels: classifying logs, recommending responses, carrying out authorized actions, adapting to unexpected conditions and making political decisions about acceptable harm. These levels confer very different risk. A model summarizing intelligence has less direct operational authority than an agent able to modify critical infrastructure configuration. The effective scope of credentials and tools is more relevant than an interface that appears conversational or competent.

NIST's AI RMF provides lifecycle-oriented concepts for managing trust, accountability and failures, and MITRE ATLAS describes adversarial behaviors to evaluate. Neither guarantees that a probabilistic model will behave correctly in every environment. Human approval only constrains risk when reviewers possess accurate context, sufficient time and a genuine mechanism to stop or reverse proposed actions. Otherwise, nominal human oversight can become a rubber stamp. [15][17]

CISA's *Secure by Design* principles place greater responsibility for systemic defects on technology manufacturers rather than assuming that every hospital, small enterprise or public agency can independently compensate for ubiquitous software flaws. The political-economic logic is that fixing a shared defect at its source can yield collective security benefits that exceed fragmented, repeated mitigation by millions of downstream customers. [21]

## 13. Cryptography, quantum computing and a distinct long-term transition

AI-enabled cyber threats and the prospect of cryptographically relevant quantum computers should not be conflated. In August 2024, NIST finalized **FIPS 203, FIPS 204 and FIPS 205**, including post-quantum mechanisms for key establishment and digital signatures. The standards matter to long-lived sensitive communications, software signing and the migration of large infrastructures with slow equipment replacement cycles. Their existence **does not establish that a quantum computer able to break current widely used public-key schemes is already operating**. [22]

The possibility of retaining encrypted data today for future decryption changes how intelligence agencies and infrastructure owners assess the useful lifetime of secrets. Legacy firmware signatures, identities, archival information and equipment contracts may have replacement horizons longer than the period in which security standards evolve. The challenge is economic and institutional as well as mathematical: organizations must maintain interoperability and operational continuity during cryptographic change.

## 14. Geopolitics, technological sovereignty and platform power

National cyber capability does not reside exclusively in defense ministries or intelligence agencies. Cloud providers, communications operators, satellites, submarine cables, semiconductor manufacturers, software vendors and university laboratories shape military capacity and economic continuity. Private access rules, vendor nationality, commercial contracts and export controls can therefore become strategic constraints during crises.

NATO's 2026 Alliance Digital Strategy places interoperability, digital resilience, Zero Trust principles, mission-critical continuity and post-quantum transition within its longer-term vision toward 2035. A strategy document is not evidence that every required capability has already been deployed. It is nonetheless a clear indicator that cyber resilience is considered part of military readiness rather than a narrowly administrative IT concern. [27]

## 15. Four conditional futures for 2027–2035

**A — Persistent offensive asymmetry.** AI makes reconnaissance, social engineering and exploitation of known vulnerabilities cheaper while fragmented organizations remain slow to patch. Small firms and legacy industrial systems are disproportionately exposed. The hypothesis weakens if observed exploitation, recovery time and organizational damage decline despite wider offensive tool availability.

**B — A cumulative defensive advantage.** Agentic code review, testing and secure-by-design engineering remove classes of common vulnerabilities faster than attackers can exploit them. The outcome depends on adoption in production, not benchmark demonstrations. Persistent growth in severe breaches despite verified reductions in exposure would count against a strong version of the hypothesis.

**C — Interstate crisis accelerated by uncertain attribution.** Discovery of covert access to telecommunications or energy infrastructure during geopolitical confrontation encourages preventive action. AI compresses assessment and response windows, increasing the chance of error. Credible hotlines, independent verification and repeated episodes contained without escalation would weaken this scenario.

**D — Technological fragmentation.** Cloud, chip, digital identity, AI and cryptographic ecosystems increasingly separate along alliance and trade boundaries. Some states gain substitute capacity at the cost of higher redundancy spending and lower interoperability. Continued shared standards and resilient, genuinely substitutable supply chains would challenge this projection.

## 16. Strategic synthesis: reliable knowledge and services that survive

The historical record does not support the claim that hacking has replaced conventional warfare. It does establish that digital operations have changed military preparation, intelligence collection, civilian vulnerability and the role of private suppliers. Stuxnet illustrated physical effects; NotPetya demonstrated transnational economic damage; Ukraine revealed both the capacity for destructive operations and their limits as decisive military instruments. Neither a myth of an omnipotent cyberweapon nor the assumption that computer incidents are detached from physical society can account for these observations. [5][6][7][8]

In 2026, artificial intelligence changes cyber conflict at the level of workflows that combine observation, interpretation, tool use and adaptation. There is concrete evidence of diverse malicious assistance and shorter execution windows, but also evidence of agent errors, environmental risks and opportunities for defense. Increased technical capability does not resolve attribution, legality, legitimacy or the political purpose of an operation. [11][12][13][14]

The central strategic risk is a widening gap between accelerating digital action and the institutions capable of understanding, authorizing and constraining it. National resilience ultimately depends on maintaining essential services, trustworthy evidence, responsible control over automated systems and credible limits on the use of force. The future of cyber security will depend not simply on which actor possesses the most powerful AI model, but on which societies can preserve physical continuity and accountable decisions when technical systems act faster than their human operators can independently examine them.
