# Where the world economy actually moves: factories, commodities, corridors and power

Global trade is organized by the location of extraction, transformation, transport and demand. The country shown on a customs declaration is often only one of several economically decisive places.

## The world economy is a network of places, not a collection of country totals

A consumer in São Paulo may buy a device whose industrial history includes minerals mined in Africa or South America, chemicals made in Japan, chip design in the United States, wafer fabrication in East Asia, packaging in Southeast Asia, and final assembly somewhere else. The sequence is not a single verified itinerary for every device: it illustrates how final origin labels compress many distinct productive transactions. A shipment of soybeans raises a different set of questions. It may move from a farm through warehouses, trucks, barges, rail and a bulk terminal, or be crushed domestically into meal and oil before export. Oil can travel from an offshore platform to a terminal, across a chokepoint, to a refinery whose products then reach third markets. Measuring the world economy through the customs value of finished goods alone conceals these transformations.

Three questions must therefore be kept separate: where a commodity is physically created or extracted; where value is generated through processing, design, branding and services; and which routes make exchange possible at acceptable cost. The OECD's TiVA database estimates value-added origins using inter-country input-output tables, whereas customs trade records border-crossing transactions. TiVA's 2025 edition, revised in January 2026, covers 80 economies and 50 activities through 2022. Its lag is not a flaw to conceal: using a 2022 input-output estimate as proof of the exact 2026 factory network would be an analytical error. [6]

The distinction also changes the meaning of competitiveness. An exporter can ship large quantities but depend on imported machinery, shipping, insurance, credit and specialized refining. Another country may have little raw material but earn income through engineering and process control. A third may run a transshipment port with enormous throughput but modest local manufacturing of the goods passing through it. These are structurally different sources of income, bargaining power and vulnerability.

## Sea, land, river, pipeline and air: five economies of movement

Ocean shipping carries roughly four-fifths of international merchandise trade by volume according to UNCTAD, but the maritime system is not one market. Iron ore, grain and coal move in dry bulk vessels; crude oil and refined products in tankers; liquefied natural gas in specialized carriers; cars may move on roll-on/roll-off ships; manufactured goods use containers; high-value urgent components may travel by air. Physical characteristics, handling equipment, cargo density, insurance and delivery frequency determine transport choices. A twenty-foot equivalent unit (TEU), a tonne of crude oil and a dollar of exported medicine describe different magnitudes and must not be added or used interchangeably. [1]

Inland transport determines the actual economic geography of seaports. A large deepwater terminal can be uncompetitive for a factory if a rail connection is unreliable, a border adds several days, or the last road leg becomes expensive. Rail and waterways tend to offer economies of scale for predictable heavy volumes where infrastructure and operations support them; roads provide flexibility and door-to-door reach, but cost and congestion rise with distance and vehicle limitations. Pipelines suit specific compatible fluids and gases, with capital-intensive fixed networks. Aviation commands a price premium but preserves value when the cost of delay, weight or perishability is decisive.

Transport time generates a financial cost as well as a freight bill. A longer voyage ties up inventory, often requires more vessels to maintain the same service frequency and changes the timing of delivery. The importer may hold more safety stock; the exporter may have to finance the shipment for longer. That is why a port, strait or railway can affect inflation, capital requirements and factory utilization even without any material reduction in the annual mass transported.

## The 2024 rerouting shock reveals the hidden unit: tonne-miles

UNCTAD's Review of Maritime Transport 2025 records 2.2% growth in maritime trade volume in 2024, against a 5.9% increase in tonne-miles, a measure that multiplies weight by distance carried. The divergence is not evidence of a comparable increase in final consumption. Ships sailed further to deliver comparable cargo volumes. Red Sea rerouting and temporary restrictions at the Panama Canal were prominent reasons. With higher average voyage duration, effective vessel capacity tightens even if the number of ships does not change. [1]

This is the core economic mechanism behind chokepoint disruptions. A ship travelling around the Cape of Good Hope instead of through Suez spends additional time at sea. Operators pay for fuel, crews and vessel employment; shippers face altered schedules; receivers may carry higher inventories. Some costs can be passed through to prices, but the magnitude depends on freight contract terms, competition and the share of logistics in the landed cost of each product. A bulk commodity and a valuable electronic component will exhibit very different freight-to-value ratios.

The same source recorded Suez tonnage in May 2025 at about 70% below the 2023 level. That is a dated comparison, not a claim about today's traffic. Container routes can change again as security, insurance and service economics shift. An analyst who treats the most recent monthly voyage pattern as a permanent geographic law confuses a temporary routing equilibrium with infrastructure capacity.

## Energy chokepoints in 2026: corridor exposure can change abruptly

The U.S. Energy Information Administration's August 2026 energy-security analysis provides a particularly consequential time comparison. It estimated oil and petroleum-liquid flows through the Strait of Hormuz at 20.9 million barrels per day in the first quarter of 2025 and 4.9 million in the second quarter of 2026. Its estimates for the Strait of Malacca were 22.3 and 16.6 million barrels per day, respectively. Those are quarterly average flow estimates obtained using tanker-tracking and other inputs—not end-of-year realized customs cargo volumes. Both corridors were profoundly affected by the conflict and related disruptions during 2026, but the precise month and causal components require separate analysis. [3]

Hormuz connects Gulf producers and export terminals to the wider maritime network, while Malacca connects Indian Ocean and Pacific-facing shipping networks. They are not substitutes for one another, and their numerical flows should not be summed into world oil demand: the same barrel can pass through both passages. Nor is an alternative pipeline equivalent to complete maritime substitution. A pipe has a specific product, direction, capacity, connection and operating availability; a terminal outside the strait helps only for barrels that can physically reach it.

This distinction matters for countries far from the Gulf. Refining margins, shipping insurance, fuel procurement and import balances can change as crude grades, available vessels and destination markets adjust. A producer with a different export terminal may obtain a relative advantage, while a consumer depending on a narrowly specified crude grade may struggle to substitute quickly. The macroeconomic burden depends on domestic inventories, fuel taxes, subsidies, exchange rates and fiscal capacity. An energy chokepoint is a transmission mechanism, not a fixed multiplier applied to every country.

## Food: the field, the industrial plant and the import-dependent household

Food trade combines global routes with local biological constraints. Grain crops move in heavy bulk volumes, oils and meals follow separate processing chains, chilled meat requires sanitary controls and cold storage, while fruits and medicines share some refrigeration needs but have very different regulation. A tonne of soybeans is not a tonne of soybean meal: crushing creates distinct products with different customers, prices and handling requirements. Moving an oilseed abroad rather than crushing it domestically changes where processing employment, energy demand, tax receipts and industrial capabilities arise.

FAO's December 2025 statistical release, covering the period through 2024, found that the two largest exporters accounted for around 85% of global soybean exports and, separately, of palm-oil exports in 2024. This identifies concentration in specific products rather than a universal concentration of all agriculture. It also observed a widening Asian agrifood trade deficit and stronger cross-regional dependence outside Europe. These patterns expose importers to freight, foreign exchange, harvest conditions and fertilizer costs, but national inventories and domestic agriculture can provide substantial buffers. [5]

An agricultural export corridor begins before loading a vessel. Crop choice responds to expected selling prices, credit, soil, rainfall, fertilizer and land access. Harvest peaks strain trucks, silos and port windows. A road connecting a municipality to a river terminal may matter more for farmgate prices than a large container port several hundred kilometres away. For households in importing economies, the transmission runs through wholesalers, milling, domestic transport and retail competition. Measuring prices in the destination country without measuring the domestic distribution chain attributes too much of the final price to shipping.

## Ore is not metal: copper, iron and critical minerals

A mine is the beginning of an industrial chain, not proof that a country can manufacture its downstream equipment. Iron ore travels toward steel plants where grade, energy, coke, furnaces and local demand influence conversion economics. Copper ore can be concentrated near its mine, transported to a smelter and refinery in a different country, then converted into wire, motors or electronic components elsewhere. Lithium mining and conversion, nickel processing, battery materials and cell manufacturing are also distinct operations. Each stage requires different capital, skills, environmental controls and reliable utilities.

The IEA's Global Critical Minerals Outlook 2025 calculated that the average combined share of the three largest refining countries across selected key energy minerals rose from roughly 82% in 2020 to 86% in 2024. Mining is generally less concentrated than refining for those materials. The aggregate is not the market share of one mineral, and individual supply chains differ greatly. The significance lies in the concentration of processing capabilities that cannot be replicated immediately by opening another mine. [4]

USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026 offers a complementary, estimated 2025 production-and-reserves picture for more than 90 minerals and materials. Reserves estimate economically recoverable deposits under prevailing assumptions; mine output measures actual annual extraction; smelter capacity and refinery output represent further operations. Conflating these categories creates a false picture of independence. New deposits may be abundant while grid access, water, skills and financing delay the relevant processing equipment for years. [9]

Industrial policy faces a genuine trade-off. Domestic refining can deepen supplier networks, technical employment and taxation, but building a plant without competitive energy, water or environmental compliance can produce losses or damage. Exporting concentrate may be rational under some conditions, while developing downstream capacity can be valuable under others. The assessment depends on scale, prices, technology transfer, ownership, local content and the long-run cost of operating each stage.

## Semiconductors and machines: high value does not mean freedom from physical logistics

The semiconductor industry exposes the limits of identifying production with an export label. OECD analysis distinguishes design, wafer fabrication and assembly, testing and packaging. Design employs intellectual property, technical teams and specialized software. Fabrication requires exceptionally sophisticated equipment, chemicals, gases, substrates and reliable utilities. Back-end processes need skilled production, testing and packaging capacity. A finished electronic device can incorporate components with different geographic histories and large amounts of imported value added. [8]

Unlike iron ore, most chips are relatively light and high in value. Yet their supply is vulnerable to a small number of process-specific facilities, certified chemical inputs, tool suppliers and specialized logistics channels. A shipment delayed for several days can idle a factory when the missing component is not functionally substitutable. Diversifying import origins only helps if the alternative supplier can produce the same specification at adequate scale. A nearby plant with an incompatible process node or qualification standard is not ready-made redundancy.

This explains why industrial clusters matter more than individual factory announcements. The cost of an integrated operation reflects local engineering talent, packaging subcontractors, testing services, equipment maintenance, chemicals, water treatment, clean-room construction and electricity quality. A government may attract final assembly while most technological rents remain elsewhere. Conversely, supporting one specialized supplier can matter more for resilience than increasing the number of nominally independent assembly locations.

The growth of AI-related infrastructure has made this more visible in trade statistics. In its October 2026 outlook, the WTO raised its forecast for global merchandise trade volume growth in 2026 to 3.9% and projected 4.1% for 2027, explicitly identifying AI-enabling goods as an important contributor. These are projections dated 8 October 2026, not realized full-year growth. The hardware trade is not merely digital: it mobilizes foundries, servers, electrical transformers, cooling systems, metals and transport. [2]

## The logistics geography of Brazil: hinterlands rather than a single coast

Brazil supplies a useful test of the relationship between large export volumes and uneven internal integration. Farms in Mato Grosso and the wider Centre-West are linked to different terminal systems depending on location, road access, railway availability, river conditions and contractual choices. Santos and Paranaguá are major gateways in the south and southeast; northern river and coastal routes include terminals reached through the Tapajós and Amazon systems and ocean departures across Pará and Maranhão. There is no single 'northern arc route', and a port's annual total does not reveal which farms supplied its cargo.

The public ANTAQ aggregate for Brazilian waterborne cargo handled in 2025 was around 1.4 billion tonnes, 6.1% above 2024. Another ANTAQ study published in October 2026 estimated 374.6 billion useful tonne-kilometres of waterborne transport activity in 2025, with 114.59 billion tonne-kilometres in Brazilian waterways under the report's category definition. The indicators answer different questions: cargo handling counts tonnes, whereas transport production weights volume by distance. Neither by itself measures net domestic value added or the delivery price to a buyer. [11][12]

A statistical boundary is especially important at Santos. ANTAQ's account described 142.8 million tonnes for the public-port installation in 2025, while the Port of Santos authority reported 186.4 million tonnes for its broader port perimeter and 44.9 million tonnes for the soybean complex. Those values should not be presented as an inconsistency without reconciling installation boundaries, coverage and cargo definitions. The same port can be measured by different agencies and operational scopes. [12][13]

For a grain cargo, the economically relevant sequence is municipality of harvest, storage and grading, inland tariff, terminal access, vessel loading, trade finance, ocean freight and the buyer's downstream processing. The municipality may receive land-related revenues, service demand and some wages, while crushing, trading margins, shipping and financing revenues arise elsewhere. Whether new roads or a barge corridor improve local welfare depends on land concentration, employment, environmental effects, public revenue and alternatives—not only on falling export costs.

## Europe's entry ports and the difference between a hub and a factory

Rotterdam and Antwerp-Bruges demonstrate that a seaport is economically inseparable from its hinterland. The industrial and transport links toward the Rhine basin, Ruhr and continental logistics centres help explain why a shipment unloaded in the Netherlands may serve a customer in Germany or elsewhere. An increase in Dutch port throughput need not mean an equivalent increase in Dutch final consumption or manufacturing. Inland waterway capacity, rail services, road congestion, warehouse availability, emissions rules and customs systems all affect the port's competitiveness.

The Port of Rotterdam Authority's March 2026 account of its 2025 annual report stressed investment in energy and logistics infrastructure and the port's role in Europe's supply chains. That assertion is an institutional account from the operator, useful for assessing its strategy but insufficient on its own to quantify all regional economic benefits. A port authority naturally emphasizes its advantages. Separate industrial employment, customs, national accounts and transport data are needed before estimating the total benefit to surrounding cities. [14]

Comparable distinctions apply across the Mediterranean and northern Europe. A container transshipped in Algeciras or Piraeus may be carried toward another port; an energy cargo at a specialized terminal has a different supply-chain function; a vehicle crossing through a ro-ro port may have been manufactured hundreds of kilometres inland. The map should therefore distinguish the manufacturing location, the trade-contract location, the transshipment node and the final market. A single national flag beside a port is not an explanation of the production system.

## Africa and landlocked economies: roads, borders and industrial options

Many African production systems are organized around mineral basins, plantations, consumer cities and corridors leading toward ocean ports. The copper and cobalt belt across the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Zambia illustrates a broader challenge: a mine or processing plant may depend on power stability, road or rail links, border formalities, port scheduling and external customers in separate jurisdictions. Routes toward ports in Angola, southern Africa or the Indian Ocean are not automatically equivalent. Their comparative economics depend on cargo type, operating capacity, border delays, transit tariffs and political conditions.

Transport to or from a landlocked region adds more than kilometres. Customs guarantees, product inspections, paperwork, bridge capacity and negotiating arrangements with transit states may matter as much as maritime rates. When a border closure delays raw materials or equipment, factories reduce utilization and households face higher imported-food and fuel prices. Conversely, a functioning corridor can connect domestic suppliers to large markets and make new processing industries viable, particularly when complemented by electricity, technical training and trade facilitation. The presence of an exported mineral does not establish that the exporting country has the conditions required for profitable refining.

The analytical problem is distribution as well as growth. Mining may increase public revenue while wages, pollution, displacement and land ownership produce sharply uneven outcomes. A rail line dedicated to extraction may generate fewer linkages than an interoperable line serving agriculture, industry and passengers, but a corridor with multiple uses is not necessarily financially viable without public support. Evaluating projects requires disclosed investment, traffic, tariffs, environmental obligations and beneficiaries. Treating Africa as a single commodity exporter would conceal the diversity of manufacturing, logistics, energy and consumer economies.

## North America and Asia: industrial borders and cumulative specialization

The United States, Canada and Mexico form a network of production sites, parts suppliers, rail links, border crossings and consumer markets rather than three isolated exporters. In automotive manufacturing a component may cross national borders before final assembly; gross trade records multiple transactions even though the same intermediate input is incorporated into a single final product. Rules of origin, safety standards and labour requirements can change where suppliers choose to invest. Yet the economic effect of nearshoring is not established by the relocation of a company headquarters or announcement of a new industrial park: actual construction, equipment installation, staffing and production are decisive.

In East Asia, the specialization of coastal and inland industrial regions varies across products. Shenzhen and the Pearl River Delta form one kind of electronics and machinery ecosystem; the Yangtze Delta links manufacturing and port networks; semiconductor production connects specialized capabilities across Taiwan, South Korea, Japan, mainland China, Southeast Asia, the United States and Europe. Singapore is simultaneously a logistics, trading and industrial node, but a re-exported good must not be treated as manufactured there. Likewise, a shipment reaching a Vietnamese port need not be destined for Vietnamese households.

Industrial clusters can build on themselves. A dense supplier base reduces lead times, provides specialized labour and makes maintenance cheaper. But concentration also increases exposure to local grid failures, floods, restrictions or geopolitics. Diversification may raise short-term unit costs while reducing the economic damage of a rare outage. Whether that trade-off is desirable depends on probability of disruption, inventory policy, value of continuity and viable substitute capacity; blanket claims that all trade should be reshored cannot replace those calculations.

## Export totals cannot identify ownership, origin or national income

Customs statistics are essential but answer narrower questions than they appear to. Import values may be recorded CIF while exports are commonly expressed FOB; the former includes insurance and freight under relevant measurement conventions. Timing, product codes, confidentiality, valuation, re-exports and country-of-consignment treatment create asymmetries between partner reports. UN Comtrade's guidance explicitly distinguishes domestic exports, re-exports and country-of-origin treatment, and explains why intermediate markets can produce asymmetries between recorded partner flows. A route through a hub does not automatically change the good's economic origin. [15]

This is particularly important for landlocked producers and trading hubs. A shipment can be exported by a firm registered in one country, loaded on a vessel under another flag, insured in a third, transshipped through a fourth and physically consumed in a fifth. A listed multinational's headquarters, its ultimate shareholders, a terminal concession and a ship's operator provide different pieces of information. None can substitute for the others. Identifying 'who controls the chain' requires specifying the right being exercised: ownership of productive assets, allocation of shipping capacity, price setting, technology licensing or legal authority over a port.

National income requires another accounting step. The exported value of an assembled machine includes intermediate materials and services imported from abroad. Domestic value added is the contribution of domestic labour, capital and other factors, as recorded under national accounts conventions, not the entire sale price at the border. Likewise, a mining concession with large exports may remit profits abroad, while taxes, wages and supplier payments stay locally. Comparative regional studies should distinguish gross exports, government revenue, company profit and household income instead of presenting them as one 'wealth generated' figure.

## The cost of reaching the buyer and the macroeconomic chain of transmission

A shipment's landed cost includes the purchase price and whichever inland haulage, handling, freight, insurance, customs duties, non-recoverable taxes, final distribution, financing and expected loss costs are borne by the relevant buyer. Contract terms determine who pays each component. A CIF price already includes named costs that cannot be added twice; an FOB price omits later freight in the convention used for international statistics. The difference between FOB and CIF is not a profit estimate for the carrier, because it can include multiple costs and statistical adjustments.

The relationship with inflation follows a sequence. A disruption increases voyage time or reduces the effective supply of shipping capacity. Freight, insurance and inventory costs may rise. Firms' prices respond depending on competition, contract duration and available margins. The consumer price index only reflects the shock as products move through domestic production, wholesale and retail. Central banks may then respond depending on inflation expectations and broader conditions; fiscal authorities may face a larger bill for fuel or food support. Exchange-rate depreciation can amplify an import shock. None of these steps is automatic, and a cheaper alternative product or weaker demand can offset part of the pressure.

At the firm level, inventory in transit can be approximated as average daily movement times average transit duration, provided the measure is consistent. Financing cost depends on value, interest rate, currency and time, while service reliability depends on the distribution of delays rather than average transit time alone. These elementary accounting relationships are more informative than a vague 'logistics cost index' when no underlying data are published.

The World Bank's LPI 2.0 moves toward evidence from shipment-level maritime, air and postal tracking rather than relying only on the former perception survey. The redesigned indicators can help compare connectivity and reliability, but they cannot prove that a particular mine-to-port cargo moved on a specified railway without a matching shipment record. [7]

## Workers, cities and ecological limits of the corridors

A logistics corridor reorganizes the geography of employment. A modern port requires seafarers, pilots, terminal operators, maintenance specialists, truck drivers, rail crews, customs personnel, IT workers and security staff. Its economic reach extends to fuel supply, repair, warehousing, construction, restaurants and urban services. But mechanization can increase output per worker while reducing the labour required for a particular task. Evaluating employment therefore requires wages, skill levels, subcontracting conditions, accident rates, bargaining institutions and the evolution of productivity rather than head counts alone.

For towns near mines, processing plants and ports, export growth can finance schools, infrastructure and household consumption. It can also produce housing inflation, traffic, land concentration, air pollution, water pressure and displacement of existing activities. A local government may have little tax capacity even as the associated shipment value rises. Owners, transport companies and foreign creditors can receive substantial income outside the production region. These effects are not negated by national GDP growth; nor do local costs prove that trade is inherently undesirable. The balance depends on governance, compensation and practical alternatives.

Environmental accounting also requires the correct unit. A longer maritime detour can increase fuel use and emissions per delivered tonne, but the result depends on vessel type, speed, load factor and alternative routing. Shifting cargo to rail or barge may reduce certain emissions where networks operate efficiently; building new infrastructure has land-use, ecosystem and capital costs of its own. A demand to 'shorten all routes' does not substitute for lifecycle analysis and local assessments. The physical map and social map of the same chain can reveal different winners and losers.

## Who can interrupt the chain? Property and operational control are different

Ownership of a port concession does not automatically grant a state the legal power to stop every vessel calling at that port. A shipping company's fleet, the beneficial owner of a cargo, the bank financing its movement, the insurer, a customs authority and the owner of a refinery possess different forms of leverage. Sanctions and export controls can restrict transactions without physically blocking a channel; an accident or drought can reduce capacity without any coercive intention. When describing a dependency as strategic, the relevant test is who can exercise what right, at what cost, and with which available substitutes.

The usual indicators understate vulnerability when they aggregate products too broadly. Two sources of 'copper' may supply different chemical or metallurgical stages. Two chip factories may manufacture incompatible designs or process generations. Two ports may serve the same ocean coast but lack comparable rail access or terminal equipment. Even when shipping services can be rerouted, longer voyages can reduce available container capacity and increase costs for third parties. The network's risk sits in specific combinations of product, infrastructure, contract and timing.

A resilience strategy should compare diversified suppliers, inventories, domestic processing, engineering capability, insurance, contingency routing and diplomatic access. Maintaining domestic stockpiles can improve response time but imposes storage, working-capital and deterioration costs. Moving a plant for political reasons can reduce one exposure while creating dependence on a new electricity system or imported machinery. Resilience is therefore a decision about tolerated risk and explicit costs, not autarky by default.

## From map to policy: how territorial development is created or lost

The same export corridor can support different regional development paths. One economy ships crude ore and imports machines; another ships refined metal and creates employment in engineering and industrial maintenance; a third manufactures equipment, provides credit and owns intangible assets. The objective is not to rank these arrangements by an automatic 'stages are always better' hierarchy. Their viability varies with scale, electricity cost, technology, geology, labour availability, market access and externalities. Economic development emerges when complementary capabilities accumulate rather than when a customs code is simply moved to a more processed category.

Public investment should be judged by the economic cost of the corridor it actually improves. Dredging a port without fixing inland queues may relocate the bottleneck. Building rail without reliable terminal slots may produce underused track. Tax incentives for an assembly plant without local supplier development may increase imports and fiscal exposure without proportional learning. Conversely, lower border friction, stable electrical infrastructure or certification labs can unlock private investment at lower cost than a grand new corridor.

The counterargument deserves attention: dependence on foreign components can coexist with rapid growth, and specialization may allow consumers to obtain better goods at lower prices. A country can gain from trading raw materials if extraction is efficient and public institutions invest the revenues well. The relevant failure is not trade itself but the inability to retain gains, manage risks, develop skills or replace exhausted resources. Public policy should compare alternative expenditures and monitor productivity, household income, fiscal receipts, environmental outcomes and financial durability over years, rather than measuring success exclusively in tonnes loaded.

## Scenarios after the 2026 disruptions: fragmentation, adaptation and redundancy

The observed disruptions and 2026 trade outlook permit three conditional paths, not precise predictions. Under a gradual-normalization path, navigational security improves, transit and insurance become more predictable and shipping networks reduce expensive detours; some additional inventory and supplier diversification may nevertheless remain. Under a persistent-fragmentation path, restrictions and uncertainty keep routes longer, reduce effective capacity and shift trade toward regional supply chains. Under an adaptation path, firms reorganize suppliers and investments, specialized processing remains concentrated in some industries and different regions attract new terminals and manufacturing.

Evidence that would distinguish the paths includes recurrent actual vessel transits by passage, tonne-miles relative to tonnage, freight and insurance contracts, capacity utilization at refineries and ports, new factories entering production rather than being announced, bilateral flows by detailed product, inventories and local employment. A nominal increase in exports to a country is insufficient to prove full relocation if the new supplier continues importing components from the same origin. Equally, a high headline concentration ratio is not definitive evidence of fragility if production is easily substituted and ample stocks exist.

The starting point in November 2026 is neither a world economy that stopped trading nor one immune to shocks. The WTO's October forecast describes resilient aggregate merchandise volumes, while the EIA and UNCTAD documents show the very different conditions facing specific routes and cargoes. Aggregates can grow while one energy passage contracts sharply; consumers may benefit from competitive manufactured imports while an industrial district loses production. The distribution of effects is the central object of analysis.

## What ultimately determines the geography of economic power

A world map filled only with flags of principal exporters can describe merchandise trade but not the organization of production. A useful map must connect extraction sites, inland networks, processing plants, ports, maritime passages, manufacturing systems and households. It must distinguish the physical course of the good from the contractual and financial chain, and identify the institutions and working conditions that keep both systems operating. The interaction among these networks determines which regions can turn access to a resource into diversified productive capabilities and which remain dependent on a small set of external buyers or bottlenecks.

The contrast between 2024 tonne-miles and tonnage shows that physical trade can become more costly without shrinking proportionately. The 2026 energy passage data show how abruptly a corridor's throughput can change. Concentration of refining illustrates why a new mine does not immediately produce industrial independence. OECD's value-added accounting demonstrates why exports alone do not reveal the domestic share of the economic reward. The experiences of Brazilian agricultural corridors, European hinterlands, North American industrial borders and African transit economies show why the relevant territory is rarely identical to a national border.

The lasting structure of global trade is therefore neither a permanent hierarchy between countries nor a perfectly fluid marketplace. It is a changing set of material capacities, technical qualifications, contracts, public institutions and geographic constraints. A region's economic prospects depend on the terms under which it connects to that set: what it can produce, how reliably it can move the product, which decisions it controls, how the income is distributed and what alternatives remain when a critical link breaks.
