# Global Macro — AI repricing, an oil rebound and divergent central banks reverse the post-election relief

**6 November 2026 · Early-morning edition, Brasília time · Information cutoff: 00:07 BRT (03:07 UTC).** This issue uses completed 5 November trading sessions and the latest available official releases. The US October employment report scheduled for later on 6 November is not yet an observed outcome. [1][2][3]

The repricing after the US congressional midterms lasted less than a full session. On 5 November, the S&P 500 declined **4.36% to 7,353.73**, the Nasdaq lost **5.52% to 24,315**, and the Philadelphia semiconductor index fell approximately **12.2%**. At the same time Brent futures recovered **3.88% to US$98.29 per barrel** after Wednesday's 8.67% plunge, while the ten-year US Treasury benchmark yield fell **6 basis points to 4.84%**. A technology-specific valuation shock, a renewed physical energy risk and demand for government bonds occurred together; none is sufficient by itself to describe global demand. [1][2][3][4]

The monetary picture became less uniform. The Bank of England raised its policy rate **25 basis points to 4.00%**, citing persistent energy-led inflation, while Brazil's Copom had held the Selic at **13.75%** on 4 November. The Federal Reserve's last formal decision, on 28 October, was to maintain **3.75%–4.00%**. Euro-area headline inflation was **4.0% in October**, with energy up **20.8%** year on year; China's official manufacturing PMI had slipped to **49.3**. High energy costs coexist with uneven production and asset-price vulnerability. [5][6][7][8][9]

Prices, surveys and policy instruments have different observation periods and economic meanings. An equity price decline is not an observed fall in output; a PMI below 50 is not a percentage contraction; a policy rate does not move in lockstep with sovereign yields. These distinctions are essential in a session with simultaneously falling equities and Treasury yields. [1][2][3][5][7][9][10]

## 2. What changed in 24 hours: elections, AI investment and valuation

On 4 November investors initially treated the Democrats' victories in both chambers of the US Congress as a potential constraint on further executive policy escalation. Oil and Treasury yields fell, while major equity indices rallied. By the 5 November close, that broad relief trade had fractured. Nasdaq's 5.52% decline exceeded the S&P 500's 4.36% fall, and semiconductor shares dropped more sharply still. Qualcomm's weak smartphone-chip revenue contributed to the sector's reassessment, while investors debated whether a changed Congress could obstruct or regulate the expansion of data centers. [1][2][11]

The immediate transmission is financial. An investor's estimate of future AI-related earnings can decline when the assumed build-out pace, operating margins or financing terms worsen. A lower terminal earnings multiple then reduces today's share value even without evidence that construction, electricity purchases or semiconductor orders have already declined. The resulting mark-to-market loss affects portfolios, capital-raising costs and collateral values; it is not a dollar-for-dollar subtraction from measured GDP.

There are competing explanations for the scale of the sell-off. Political uncertainty around data-center approvals matters, but earlier high equity valuations and concentrated exposures made even ordinary earnings disappointments consequential. A pure regulatory explanation would require identifiable legislative proposals or adopted restrictions and evidence linking them to company cash-flow revisions. A pure macro-recession explanation would be difficult to reconcile with the absence, at this cutoff, of the official October payroll release and with October US manufacturing PMI at 55.5. The evidence supports a sharp repricing of uncertain future technology profits, not a verified cessation of the AI investment cycle. [1][11][12]

The distributional consequences extend beyond megacap shareholders. Utilities selling power into data-center projects, construction contractors and specialized equipment suppliers have different revenue exposures from semiconductor designers. A temporary equity decline can coexist with signed long-term contracts and capital expenditures already under way. A sustained correction would matter more if it forced cancellation of financing, contracts or plant construction. Those observable changes would distinguish a change in production from a change in market capitalization.

## 3. Oil: an abrupt rebound exposes the difference between negotiation and physical security

Brent futures ended 4 November at **US$94.62**, down 8.67%. The following session they settled at **US$98.29**, a **US$3.67** or **3.88%** increase; US WTI reached **US$90.52**, up **2.72%**. Wednesday's prospect of diplomatic de-escalation had not guaranteed Thursday's deliveries. Reuters reported renewed tanker incidents in Gulf waters and cited UK maritime authorities recording at least five incidents since Sunday. The Iranian Revolutionary Guard also made claims concerning vessels in the Strait of Hormuz; the claims and attribution require separation from independent vessel-level verification. [2][4]

This is not merely an oil-price story. Refineries buy specific grades under contract. Tanker operators face security, charter and insurance costs; insurers price incidents and exposure, while shipping routes affect transit times. A change in the futures price can precede or diverge from physical availability and freight conditions. The reopening of a maritime corridor, regular loaded-vessel passage and reduced insurance premia would offer better evidence of supply normalization than a single futures settlement.

```chart
type: bar
title: Brent futures settlement across two sessions
unit: US dollars per barrel; 4 and 5 November 2026
4 November | 94.62
5 November | 98.29
```

The two observations do not create a complete price series or prove that the physical volume reaching importers changed in proportion to the price move. The Wednesday fall and Thursday rebound reveal unstable expectations, not a lasting settlement of the Middle East conflict. [2]

Energy can generate apparently contradictory global effects. Importing households face higher heating and transport expenses, reducing discretionary consumption. Exporting governments may collect more oil revenue if output and shipments remain viable. Energy-intensive factories face cost increases that are not automatically passed on in full; margin compression, delayed investment and selective price rises are alternative responses. Persistent diesel and freight costs can matter even when headline Brent retreats.

```flow
Reported tanker incidents → Higher perceived shipping and insurance risk → More uncertain delivered energy costs
Delivered energy-cost pressure → Household bills and industrial input costs → Inflation expectations and real purchasing power
AI equity repricing → Cost of issuing risk capital and collateral values → Financing decisions and investment timing
```

Each link is conditional. Neither an attack allegation nor a price move establishes the size of the delivered-cost shock in each country. The financial pathway from equity valuations to real investment also depends on financing needs and contracts already signed.

## 4. England tightens while Treasuries rally: monetary divergence in practice

At its meeting ending on 4 November, the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee voted **8–1** to raise Bank Rate to **4.00%**; one member preferred a larger **50-basis-point** increase. The decision was announced on 5 November. The bank reported **3.5% UK consumer-price inflation in September** and projected inflation around **5% by mid-2027**, emphasizing the prolonged energy shock and potential second-round effects. It also acknowledged that direct evidence of broad wage-and-price propagation remained limited. The higher rate is a policy response to expected persistence, not proof that inflation has already reached its forecast peak. [5]

In the United States, the 28 October FOMC maintained a **3.75%–4.00%** range by a 10–2 vote, while two dissenters wanted a quarter-point increase. The 5 November Treasury market instead priced lower nominal yields: two-year 4.46%, ten-year 4.84%, thirty-year 5.25%. This movement can reflect demand for government securities, altered expected future policy or adjustments in term and liquidity premia. It is not consistent with treating every bond-market decline as a central-bank pivot. [3][7]

In the euro area, the **4.0%** October HICP flash estimate was up from **3.8%** in September. Energy inflation rose to **20.8%** from **18.9%**; core excluding energy, food, alcohol and tobacco stood at **2.5%**. Energy therefore explains an important part of the current headline pressure without making all underlying prices immune. A stronger-than-expected energy shock can force households to cut other spending while leaving central banks concerned about price expectations. [8]

## 5. Brazil: market contagion is not the same as a new domestic monetary decision

Brazil's Copom maintained Selic at **13.75%** on 4 November, halting a sequence of reductions. The B3 reported that the Ibovespa lost **0.82%** on 5 November to **166,191.45 points**, while the commercial dollar rose approximately **0.67%** to **R$5.48**. The Brazilian session took place after the Copom announcement, unlike the positive 4 November stock close that preceded the decision. The immediate local combination was external equity pressure, a stronger dollar and a still-restrictive domestic benchmark. [6][10]

Brazil faces three distinct transmission channels. First, a fall in US technology shares can reduce global appetite for risk, even where Brazilian listed firms have little direct AI revenue. Second, a stronger dollar raises the local-currency cost of imported machinery, fuels and dollar debt, although the pass-through depends on contracts, hedging and competition. Third, high Selic increases the cost of working capital and refinancing for households and businesses while supporting the return on local-currency fixed income.

The policy trade-off is asymmetric. An energy exporter may benefit from stronger dollar-denominated oil revenue while fuel-consuming companies and households face higher costs. Public finances can benefit from particular tax or royalty flows while debt service and inflation-linked expenditure increase through separate mechanisms. It would be inappropriate to infer a net fiscal windfall without production volumes, fiscal rules and detailed tax incidence.

After the national election, fiscal policy expectations also matter for the long yield curve. Actual budget provisions, spending execution and revenue measures must be distinguished from political statements. Monetary policy cannot repair a temporary geopolitical shipping interruption, but it can influence expectations and demand. Conversely, lowering rates prematurely into higher expected inflation can increase future financing premia. Brazil's eventual policy room depends on domestic inflation, activity, fiscal implementation and the external rate environment, not on one New York session.

## 6. China and Asia: demand weakness meets a concentrated technology supply chain

China's official manufacturing PMI fell from **50.1 in September to 49.3 in October**, with production **49.8**, new orders **49.4** and new export orders **48.7**. The large-enterprise PMI was **50.2**, while small and medium-sized enterprises remained below 50. These are diffusion responses about direction, not direct measures of industrial volume or export value. They reveal an uneven industrial climate with softer orders and smaller-business conditions than a single headline can show. [9]

The US semiconductor sell-off adds a separate channel for East Asian suppliers. Advanced components, foundry capacity, equipment, memory and assembly occupy different places in the value chain; their dependence on data-center capital spending is not identical. A falling US market valuation could foreshadow slower AI orders, but current production and cash revenue require company disclosures, export data and order cancellations. A downturn in Chinese general manufacturing does not establish a downturn in every high-end electronics producer elsewhere in Asia.

Oil-importing Asian economies face an energy bill and supply-security problem at the same time. Firms may respond through hedging, pricing, inventory or delayed investment. Exporters of energy or industrial inputs face different effects. Those differences are why a global risk-off session can coexist with locally divergent earnings, industrial activity and exchange rates.

```map
United States | AI-linked equity values fall sharply while Treasury yields decline | Asset repricing precedes any confirmed payroll and project cancellations
United Kingdom | Bank Rate rises to 4.00% amid an energy-led inflation projection | A forecast is not observed mid-2027 inflation
Euro area | October HICP flash inflation reaches 4.0%; energy 20.8% | Flash estimate; national exposure differs
Brazil | Selic remains 13.75%, Ibovespa falls and dollar strengthens | External shock and domestic monetary setting coexist
China and East Asia | China's October official factory PMI slips to 49.3 | PMI is not a percentage fall in production; tech supply-chain exposures differ
Gulf and shipping corridors | Tanker incidents undermine confidence in energy normalization | Incident reports do not quantify verified delivered barrels
```

These regional comparisons describe economic exposure rather than a geographic measure of hazard or a ranked list of national vulnerabilities.

## 7. Labor, prices and the next US information event

October US ISM manufacturing stood at **55.5**, with its prices-paid diffusion index at **75.2**. ISM services registered **54.5**, with a prices-paid index of **73.0**. Both surveys indicate a wide prevalence of reported price increases; a reading of 75.2 or 73.0 must never be described as a corresponding percentage rate of inflation. Neither survey reveals the exact increase in consumer prices. [12][13]

The separate ADP report indicated **86,000** additional private-sector jobs in October. It is not the official Bureau of Labor Statistics nonfarm-payroll survey: coverage, modeling and revisions differ. The BLS release scheduled for Friday, 6 November, is therefore a genuine event risk. Before its publication, any estimate of 65,000 jobs or a 4.2% jobless rate is only a forecast, not an observed report. [14][15]

A stronger official employment reading could reduce fears of an abrupt US demand slowdown yet revive expectations of tighter monetary policy if wage pressure remains persistent. A weaker reading could support Treasury prices while worsening earnings concerns. Neither case necessarily moves all assets in one direction: oil, bonds, technology equities and the dollar respond through different growth, inflation and risk channels. The joint interpretation of payrolls, hours, revisions, participation and hourly earnings matters more than a single headline deviation.

## 8. Competing scenarios and evidence that would change the assessment

| Conditional pathway | Confirmation | Evidence against |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Risk normalization and orderly energy disinflation | Sustained safer shipping, falling delivered freight/insurance costs, oil stabilizing with lower price pressure | Repeated tanker incidents, rising delivered costs, durable energy pass-through |
| Extended AI valuation reset without production collapse | Lower equity multiples with maintained capex commitments, supplier orders and power contracts | Canceled construction, falling real equipment orders, tightening credit access |
| Equity shock becomes broad financial tightening | Wider credit spreads, reduced primary issuance, investment postponements and weaker employment | Continued stable financing, resilient business investment and broad employment |
| Energy inflation forces more restrictive policy | Repeated core inflation persistence, wages/expectations broadening and further central-bank tightening | Declining core measures, normalized supply and easing expectations |

These are mechanisms and conditional tests, not assigned probabilities. A shock can follow more than one pathway at once. Technology equities can decline without a recession; expensive energy can delay disinflation while weak activity reduces demand.

## 9. What to monitor after the information cutoff

The **6 November US employment report** is the nearest scheduled test of whether demand remains resilient enough to withstand higher financing costs. Subsequent releases on price inflation, household expectations and credit spreads will be necessary to separate risk aversion from a durable change in monetary fundamentals. The Bank of England's next scheduled rate announcement is **17 December**. [5][15]

For energy, reports of attacks should be reconciled against maritime incident notices, actual tanker movement, cargo deliveries, insurance prices and refinery availability. For AI, company capex plans and verified data-center permitting or project cancellations are stronger evidence than generalized speculation about future regulation. For Brazil, the subsequent Focus survey, local inflation releases, fiscal legislation and domestic yield curve will establish whether the US shock transmits into a lasting change in funding conditions.

The observed regime at the cutoff is **simultaneous technology-equity deleveraging, renewed oil-supply anxiety, falling US Treasury yields and diverging national monetary decisions**. This combination raises volatility without establishing one uniform global recession or inflation trajectory.
