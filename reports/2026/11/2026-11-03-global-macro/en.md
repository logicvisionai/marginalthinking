# Global Macro — Oil's 7.6% decline exposes a split between industrial resilience and financing risk

**3 November 2026 · Brasília early edition · Information cutoff: 01:45 BRT (04:45 UTC).** Market closes and industrial releases refer to Monday, 2 November; the Brazilian stock exchange was closed on that date. The US congressional election of 3 November, the Brazilian Central Bank's 3–4 November meeting and the 4 November euro-area October inflation flash estimate remain future or unpublished events at this cutoff. Forecasts and market-implied possibilities are distinguished from outcomes. [1][5][11][12][14][15][17]

The first completed global trading session of November produced an unusual combination. **Brent crude lost 7.63% to $104.77 a barrel**, the largest apparent relief for global importers since the latest Middle East escalation; yet the United States reported an October industrial purchasing managers' index of **55.5** and an input-price index of **75.2**, which remained firmly in inflationary territory. Lower crude futures reduce the price paid for an expected future barrel, but they do not immediately replenish inventories, reverse supplier delivery delays or eliminate inflation already transmitted into contracts and household budgets. [1][5]

American equities did not behave as though cheaper oil had removed financial stress. The **S&P 500 fell 0.51%**, the **Nasdaq declined 1.07%**, while the Dow rose 0.41%. The dispersion reflected concern over the capital required for artificial-intelligence infrastructure, notably companies dependent on heavy investment, debt financing and revenue expectations many years into the future. The same Monday session also featured political positioning ahead of US congressional voting. [7][17]

The global macroeconomic regime is therefore not a uniform demand collapse. October manufacturing surveys showed expansion in the United States and the euro area while their operating costs increased; France contracted, Asian activity remained uneven, and Brazil awaited its first post-holiday local session after a sharp election-related repricing. The common question across these markets is whether lower energy futures will reach the real economy quickly enough to offset financing restrictions and already elevated costs. [1][3][4][12][16]

## 1. Oil: a peace premium reverses, but barrels do not appear immediately

Reuters reported that negotiations involving Iranian and Qatari officials contributed to renewed hopes of a diplomatic route out of the US–Israeli war with Iran. **Brent settled at $104.77, down $8.66 or 7.63%**, while **West Texas Intermediate finished at $96.46, down $7.66 or 7.36%**, on Monday 2 November. This is a completed futures settlement, not an intraday quote or a forecast for 3 November. The scale of the drop shows the sensitivity of prices to perceived geopolitical risk, although a negotiated peace, restoration of transit and normalisation of exports had not been confirmed at the information cutoff. [5]

**Price, flow and stock are separate economic objects.** The futures price expresses a market valuation for delivery under contract terms. Physical flow depends on maritime access, pipelines, terminals, refinery specifications, insurance and actual cargo movements. Inventories are accumulated barrels that buffer the gap between demand and immediate production. A price decline of more than seven percent can occur before a corresponding increase in accessible supply. It may instead reflect a change in the probability investors assign to a future de-escalation.

This distinction matters particularly because the Middle East conflict disrupted shipping and drew down buffers. Reuters quoted the head of ADNOC describing a cumulative drawdown approaching one billion barrels, but this is an attributed industry assessment, not a reconciled global stock census. Even if diplomacy succeeds, damaged infrastructure, risk premiums on marine insurance and unusual crude-quality constraints may persist. At this stage, the price response is better evidence of a shift in market expectations than of verified production recovery. [5]

On 1 November, seven OPEC+ producers—Saudi Arabia, Russia, Iraq, Kuwait, Kazakhstan, Algeria and Oman—announced that they would **maintain their September 2026 required production levels for December**. The organisation scheduled another review for 29 November. A production *requirement* is a policy commitment or target, not a record of the oil actually extracted, shipped or delivered. Neither the communiqué nor the futures settlement confirms that the physical supply network has recovered. [6]

```flow
Reported Iran–Qatar diplomatic contacts → Lower perceived conflict risk → Brent and WTI futures fall on November 2
Physical transit, terminal access and insurance → Actual cargo deliveries and inventory rebuilding → Delayed effect on refining costs, freight and consumer prices
Persistent energy costs already embedded in contracts → Factory input-price pressure → Corporate margins and central-bank decisions
```

For importers, a sustained decline in landed energy costs would ease external accounts and, with a lag, inflation. Exporting governments and producers face the opposite risk for current revenue, although a more stable transit network can increase physical sales. The pass-through depends on exchange rates, taxation, subsidies, refinery margins and the durability of the price movement. A single trading session cannot determine the net effect on the fiscal accounts of any particular producer or importer.

## 2. United States: factories expand while input prices restrict relief

The Institute for Supply Management's October report, released 2 November, showed the **US manufacturing PMI rising to 55.5 from 54.5** in September. The **production index climbed to 59.0 from 56.7**, new orders reached **55.5 from 55.3**, and employment remained in expansion at **52.4**, slightly below September's 52.7. Supplier deliveries stood at **59.1**, indicating slower deliveries; the index is inversely interpreted relative to more conventional measures. Customer inventories remained low at **41.6**. [1]

```chart

New orders | 55.5
```

The **prices-paid index eased to 75.2 from 77.9**, but remained in increasing territory, at an elevated level rather than evidence of falling factory input prices. A decrease in a diffusion index measures a smaller breadth of respondents reporting increases, not necessarily a negative monthly change in the price level. The survey also mentioned energy costs, metal products, transport, tariffs and supply-chain constraints. Respondents expressed considerably more negative than positive sentiment despite the high headline activity index. [1][2]

The combination of solid output and expensive inputs complicates the Federal Reserve's trade-off. On **28 October**, the Federal Open Market Committee kept the target range at **3.75%–4.00%**, in a **10–2** decision; two dissenting members preferred an increase. Its statement described strong activity, resilient spending, investment and inflation remaining elevated. The ISM October survey is not proof of a future interest-rate increase, but stronger production alongside costly inputs reduces the scope for assuming that cheaper crude will quickly deliver broad disinflation. [8]

US third-quarter real GDP growth was reported at an annualised rate of **3.4%**, a distinctly different statistical measure from the PMI: GDP is a flow of real production over the quarter, annualised from a quarter-on-quarter rate; the PMI is a survey diffusion reading for October. Strong national income data can coexist with financial stress in highly valued industries. [2]

The next decisive information will be whether later orders and prices indicate persistence, whether long-term Treasury yields remain under pressure after the oil fall, and whether firms reduce hiring or investment plans despite current output resilience. Changes in interest-rate futures are probabilities inferred from traded contracts and should not be reported as Federal Reserve decisions.

## 3. Equity markets: lower oil did not remove the AI-financing problem

The 2 November close was characterised by a divergence between broad equity benchmarks and firms carrying high AI-related expenditure expectations. Reuters reported **S&P 500 −0.51% to 7,541.40**, **Nasdaq −1.07% to 25,164.32**, and **Dow Jones +0.41% to 51,983.98**. The energy sector declined by roughly four percent. October had already brought a **5.3% decline in the Nasdaq**, as markets questioned the conversion of AI investment into near-term cash generation. [7]

```chart

unit: percent change on the day; negative values indicate declines

Nasdaq Composite | -1.07
Dow Jones Industrial Average | 0.41
```

Reuters described Oracle shares falling nearly **9%** after filings indicated a **$20 billion plan for debt and equity financing**, while chip-sector transactions and earnings expectations contributed to large moves in other technology names. These are company- or transaction-specific reactions, not evidence of a general withdrawal of finance from every AI project. A firm raising capital may be expanding a real profitable business, while equity investors may simultaneously demand a lower valuation because the financing structure or timing of returns has changed. [7]

**Capital expenditure, financing and equity valuation must remain distinct.** Spending on data centres, semiconductors and electricity networks is a flow of investment that creates capacity and costs. Debt issuance changes the liabilities and funding costs of a firm. The stock-market capitalisation is a quoted value of equity based on future expectations. A fall in the share price does not imply that the same amount of cash physically left the company, and a rise in revenues does not establish that new investments already have positive net present value.

At a high discount rate, the distant profits expected from infrastructure investment receive lower current valuations. If projects are financed by debt, interest expenses and refinancing requirements can be felt before contracted services generate sufficient revenue. Firms with stronger cash flow or ready customers may be less vulnerable than speculative suppliers. The reported large year-on-year increase in S&P 500 earnings, which Reuters said was concentrated among AI-related heavyweights, further illustrates why aggregate earnings and the risk borne by specific firms can move in different directions. [7]

The decline in long-end Treasury yields on 2 November provided some financing relief, according to market coverage, but did not reverse the equity concerns. Lower yields can help the discounted value of future profits while falling because markets fear weaker growth; the same price action can have opposing interpretations. The specific signal will be the joint movement of borrowing spreads, earnings guidance, capital-raising terms and investment commitments, not the level of one stock index. [18]

## 4. Europe: manufacturing resilience confronts energy inflation

Euro-area manufacturing activity remained in expansion in October, with S&P Global's final PMI at **52.7**, down from **52.9** in September but above the preliminary 52.4 estimate. Output growth slowed, new orders increased more modestly and input-cost pressure accelerated. Export orders rose at their fastest pace in more than four years, while suppliers' delivery times lengthened and producers resumed stockpiling raw materials. The surveys capture conditions *during October*, before the 2 November fall in Brent. [3]

France diverged. Its October manufacturing PMI fell to **49.5 from 50.6**, returning below the neutral level of 50 as new orders weakened and costs increased. The euro-area aggregate and the French reading come from related but different survey populations; their levels show differences in the breadth of factory responses, not comparable percentage growth rates in industrial output. [4]

Eurostat's preliminary GDP release showed **euro-area real GDP rising 0.4% quarter on quarter in Q3 2026**, compared with 0.6% in the previous quarter. The European Central Bank held its deposit rate at **2.50%** on 29 October and emphasised the risk that prolonged energy shocks would raise inflation while restraining growth. The latest completed euro-area inflation estimate available for the full monetary union was **3.8% for September**, up from 3.2% in August; October's euro-area flash estimate was scheduled for **4 November**, beyond the present cutoff. [9][10][11]

This is a potential cost-push trade-off: higher delivered energy and input prices squeeze real household income and profits even while some sectors expand. Import relief from lower crude futures will depend on how quickly suppliers adjust prices and whether physical deliveries, gas, electricity and freight normalise. Structural differences between countries make policy transmission uneven. Higher sovereign risk premiums can raise financing costs in a member state independently of the common ECB deposit rate.

For Europe, the immediate challenge is not to confuse an improving export-order survey with broad domestic recovery. Manufacturers may export more and rebuild input stocks while local households face weaker purchasing power. The adverse development would be simultaneous pressure on energy, credit and fiscal accounts; the resilience case would be sustained new orders alongside easing actual input costs.

## 5. The United States votes: congressional control becomes a fiscal and regulatory variable

The **US congressional midterm elections take place on Tuesday, 3 November**. At the report's early-morning Brazilian cutoff, no election result was available. The outcome will affect the balance of authority over legislation, fiscal negotiations, oversight and some regulatory priorities, but it will not by itself change the Federal Reserve's existing policy rate or mechanically determine the direction of equity markets. [17]

Analysts interviewed by Reuters identified several conditional outcomes. A change in control of the House could increase investigations of major technology companies and alter negotiating leverage on government spending or the debt ceiling. Retention of congressional control by Republicans could support other tax or defence-spending priorities. A divided Congress could make substantial new legislation more difficult while increasing the importance of deadlines, appropriations and debt-ceiling bargaining. These are scenarios, not predictions of which party will win. [17]

The economic transmission is mainly through expectations for fiscal deficits, Treasury issuance, tax policy, industrial subsidies and regulation. Bond investors assess not only the latest election but the expected future supply of debt and the credibility of fiscal agreements. Corporate investment committees may delay large commitments if legislation affecting incentives, energy or AI remains uncertain. Some of these decisions occur over months; intraday share-price reactions cannot identify the whole medium-term effect.

Election-linked volatility is especially relevant after an oil-price shock. A rapid fall in crude can ease inflation expectations, while uncertainty about fiscal spending can push the cost of long-term finance in the opposite direction. The net impact on the dollar, global credit spreads and emerging-market currencies depends on which force dominates and on changes in risk appetite.

## 6. Asia: yesterday's factory divergence still shapes today's credit exposure

The previous Global Macro edition documented Korea's manufacturing PMI slowing to **51.3 from 53.9**, Japan's to **53.3 from 54.1**, Taiwan's at **54.9**, and the difference between China's official PMI of **49.3** and private RatingDog/S&P index of **52.0**. These are October survey findings published or available by the early 2 November cutoff, not new observations on 3 November. Their relevance today lies in the interaction with the larger market moves subsequently recorded in the United States and energy markets. [20]

Semiconductor manufacturing and data-centre demand can expand at the same time as factories serving ordinary consumer demand lose momentum. Industry revenue can rise because of product prices, quantities or a shift toward more expensive equipment. It is therefore not sufficient to infer broad Asian productivity or household purchasing power from semiconductor customs receipts. Import-dependent manufacturers face additional exposure through fuel, freight and financing costs even when their export order book remains positive.

The Bank of Japan's October decision maintained a short-term rate setting of **1.25%**, while the authorities continued to assess domestic prices and income conditions. Yen funding costs matter for regional portfolio allocation and firms that finance dollar-denominated imports or equipment. A narrowing interest-rate gap can alter exchange-rate expectations, but a specific currency move cannot be deduced solely from central bank rates. [19]

The combination of falling Brent, stronger US manufacturing and weaker US technology equities creates mixed incentives for Asia. Lower input costs support importers, stronger US orders support suppliers, but declining valuations and financing of AI data centres may reduce investment appetite among technology customers. The outcomes will depend on physical orders, financing conditions and later releases rather than a single regional PMI headline.

## 7. Brazil: the market reopens after a holiday and a severe post-election repricing

The B3 explicitly suspended trading on **Monday, 2 November** because of the Finados holiday, including equities and major listed instruments, and scheduled reopening for **Tuesday, 3 November**. A price labelled as an Ibovespa close for Monday would therefore require careful explanation rather than treatment as an ordinary new B3 session. The relevant benchmark is the completed **Friday, 30 October** local session. [12]

On 30 October, the Ibovespa closed around **167,922 points**, down **0.75%** on the day and about **18.12% during the week** following the re-election of President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva. The dollar traded around **R$5.44** at the close, with a marked weekly rise. These are quoted market observations, not measures of current fiscal spending or an estimate of capital physically withdrawn from Brazil. A share-index fall changes the market valuation of listed equity; the currency movement changes the real price of dollar obligations and imported goods. [16]

Brazil's Central Bank last adjusted the Selic target to **13.75% on 16 September**. Its communiqué scheduled the next policy meeting for **3–4 November**, with the decision on **4 November**. The rate had not yet been determined when this edition was written. The communiqué acknowledged risks from foreign geopolitical uncertainty, domestic inflation expectations and the pass-through from a weaker exchange rate. The Focus survey scheduled for the morning of **3 November** had likewise not yet been released at the cutoff; forecasts from older releases should not be relabelled as today's market consensus. [13][14]

The latest IBGE labour release showed **5.2% unemployment for the quarter ended September**, while the broad labour-force underutilisation measure stood at **13.0%**. These figures describe the labour market, not a reason to presume unlimited fiscal room. A resilient employment base supports household income and tax collections, but a currency shock and higher long-term rates may increase borrowing and import costs even while employment remains firm. [15]

The fiscal channel is central to the reopening. Investors are reassessing the outlook for public spending, the economic team and the future path of debt; political outcomes alone do not reveal the eventual fiscal measures. A stronger real would reduce imported energy and equipment costs; a weaker real can offset some gains from falling dollar-denominated oil. For exporters, depreciation can raise receipts in reais, but input costs and foreign-currency debt complicate the result. For public finances, higher domestic long-term yields matter through refinancing needs and the interest bill, subject to the debt's maturity and indexation.

**The first tradable session on 3 November and the 4 November Copom decision are different events**. Price discovery on reopening will reflect external oil and equity moves accumulated during the domestic holiday, plus local fiscal expectations. A central-bank meeting will set a policy instrument according to its mandate and evidence, not retroactively validate an intraday market price.

## 8. Global transmission: commodity relief versus the cost of capital

The price of a barrel influences freight, fuels, petrochemicals, utility costs and household consumption; the price of finance influences factories, data centres, mortgages, government budgets and the value of future earnings. The two channels can reinforce each other or move in opposite directions. The November 2 session showed why they should not be reduced to a single story about risk appetite.

A sustained easing in physical energy costs would improve real incomes and reduce working-capital pressure. Faster supplies and lower insurance premiums would lower landed costs. Yet these effects generally reach consumer prices and monetary policy with delays and can be diminished by currency depreciation, tax structures or bottlenecks elsewhere in energy networks.

Conversely, heightened concerns about debt-funded AI investment can increase credit spreads and required equity returns even while manufacturers report expansion. When projects involve multi-year construction and equipment purchases, reduced access to funding can slow orders before completed infrastructure loses utilisation. This has consequences for chip producers, electricity suppliers, construction, metals and international trade, though effects will differ across firms.

```flow
Falling geopolitical risk premium → Lower crude futures → Potential lower landed fuel costs if cargo flows recover → Real-income and margin relief with a lag
Persistent factory price diffusion → Less immediate disinflation → Central banks cautious on rates → Elevated financing costs for households and firms
AI capital-raising needs + lower risk tolerance → Higher required returns and tighter funding terms → Reassessment of data-centre investment pipelines → Technology supplier orders and employment
Brazil post-election fiscal uncertainty + external rate environment → Local yield and currency repricing → Imported inflation and domestic credit conditions
```

The risk of a common squeeze arises when physical supply remains constrained, firms face expensive inputs and governments also pay more to borrow. The resilience path requires evidence on real cargo deliveries, inventory rebuilding, orders, corporate cash flow and less restrictive financing. Commodity futures and equity prices are important leading signals, but not substitutes for those observations.

## 9. Scenario matrix: conditional outcomes and what would challenge them

Three coherent interpretations follow from the confirmed releases without requiring invented probabilities.

**Energy relief with resilient output.** Diplomacy progresses beyond statements, transit and cargo deliveries become more reliable, and energy costs fall through to industrial inputs. US and European PMIs remain above 50 with new orders intact. AI investments proceed more selectively, favouring firms able to fund projects from contracted revenue. This interpretation would be weakened by renewed port restrictions, falling physical delivery volumes or a sustained deterioration in new orders.

**Cost pressure with tighter finance.** Geopolitical negotiations do not generate full supply restoration, so refinery and freight costs remain elevated despite the initial futures decline. The ISM price index stays high, borrowing spreads widen and highly leveraged technology projects are postponed. European importers and emerging markets face simultaneous real-income and credit pressure. Evidence against this scenario would be verified inventory rebuilding, lower realised input prices and firm financing conditions.

**Political and fiscal repricing.** US congressional results, fiscal negotiations and the Brazilian post-election policy debate increase uncertainty around future borrowing requirements or regulations. Exchange rates, sovereign curves and corporate financing terms adjust before investment spending changes. This interpretation weakens if clear fiscal agreements, predictable policy announcements and stable market functioning reduce long-term risk premiums.

The most informative joint test is whether Brent stays lower *and* manufacturers report cheaper actual inputs while corporate credit remains available. An oil rally after renewed violence would be informative about geopolitical repricing; an enduring decline in physical energy costs alongside narrower credit spreads would be evidence of a stronger relief mechanism. Election returns and policy decisions should be incorporated only once formally observed and dated.

The 3 November starting position is thus a divergence between **commodity-price relief, resilient industrial activity, persistent price pressure and narrower tolerance for debt-financed investment**. Political events can alter that distribution of risks, but their outcomes had not occurred at the research cutoff.
