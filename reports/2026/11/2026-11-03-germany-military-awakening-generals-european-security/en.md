# Germany's Military Awakening: From the First World War to Europe's Strategic Rearmament in 2026

For much of the period following reunification, Germany exercised continental influence without converting its economic weight into comparable military power. Export manufacturing, European monetary integration, production networks across Central Europe and the American security guarantee lowered the political incentive to maintain large land forces, substantial ammunition inventories and national infrastructure prepared for prolonged war. That division of labour was shaken by Russia's annexation of Crimea in 2014 and fundamentally challenged by its full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022. By 2026, the German response went beyond purchases of equipment: it encompassed military-service legislation, expanded fiscal capacity, permanent deployment on NATO's eastern flank, changes in senior command and the adaptation of civilian logistics to a possible major interstate conflict. [10][11][12][13]

The shift cannot be understood without the history of German military power between 1914 and 1945. In two world wars, senior officers accumulated extraordinary authority over national production and political decision-making, albeit under different regimes and circumstances. The concentration of authority during the First World War, the ambiguous loyalty of the Weimar officer corps, Nazi rearmament, and the Wehrmacht's role in aggressive war and mass atrocities explain why the Bundeswehr was founded in 1955 under constitutional limits, parliamentary supervision and the doctrine of the soldier as a citizen bound by law. Present-day Germany is neither Wilhelm II's empire nor the Nazi state: its institutions, alliances and declared objectives differ fundamentally. Historical memory nevertheless shapes the legitimacy of rearmament at home and the degree of trust among neighbours. [1][2][3][4][5][6]

## 1. Imperial Germany at war: when military strategy governed society

The Imperial German Army entered 1914 with considerable professional prestige, sophisticated operational planning and a constitutional order that reserved significant military prerogatives for the emperor. The prospect of fighting France and Russia simultaneously made railway scheduling, mobilisation speed and the rapid concentration of forces central to strategy. Failure to win a decisive victory in the west, followed by the entrenchment of opposing forces, transformed the war into a prolonged test of resources. Artillery, steel, coal, railway capacity, fertilisers, food and public borrowing increasingly determined how long the opposing societies could resist.

In August 1916, Paul von Hindenburg and Erich Ludendorff assumed the third Supreme Army Command. The resulting concentration of power exposed a recurring institutional hazard: the military leadership ceased merely to propose instruments for political goals and increasingly dictated the goals themselves. The Hindenburg Programme attempted to reorganise labour and industrial output around armaments, while press restrictions and pressure on critics intensified. Germany's Federal Agency for Civic Education describes the military leadership of this period as developing dictatorial tendencies beneath the remaining forms of imperial government. [1]

This mobilisation did not create unlimited power. Workers shifted toward armaments were no longer available for other essential tasks, while blockade and shortages impaired civilian consumption. A government can raise immediate military deliveries and simultaneously damage the social base that sustains an extended war. The 1914–1918 experience shows why highly competent general-staff planning should not be confused with a politically sustainable strategy for winning a war of attrition.

## 2. Versailles, the Reichswehr and the ambiguous legacy of defeat

Germany's defeat in 1918 did not eliminate military knowledge, professional networks or the political ambitions of some of its officers. The Treaty of Versailles restricted the army to 100,000 men, sharply limited the officer corps and prohibited compulsory military service. The victorious powers sought to prevent renewed aggression. German nationalists, however, frequently used the restrictions as evidence of national humiliation and as an argument against the legitimacy of the Weimar Republic. [2]

The small Reichswehr developed a professional cadre capable of training a future expanded force. That organisational competence coexisted with covert violations of disarmament provisions. The historical documents accompanying the treaty identify hidden weapons and support for illegal paramilitary formations. The problem was not only the number of troops: armed institutions maintaining political loyalties of their own undermined the complete subordination of force to a republican government. [2]

The period illustrates why military strength and militarism cannot be measured by headcount alone. A small army can preserve political autonomy and prepare clandestinely for expansion. Conversely, a larger military can remain subordinated to constitutional control. The central questions concern command authority, the rule of law, relations with society, strategic objectives and the institutions that constrain illegitimate violence.

## 3. Nazi rearmament and the political-moral rupture of 1933–1945

Adolf Hitler's regime inherited some plans for military enlargement that had been discussed before 1933. It converted those plans into a rapidly accelerating programme for conquest. In March 1935, conscription returned, the Reichswehr became the Wehrmacht and the state publicly announced military capabilities inconsistent with Versailles. Expansion served an aggressive project linked to the destruction of democracy and racial persecution, not a defensive alliance strategy comparable to Germany's current security commitments. [3][4]

The command system underwent an even more fundamental transformation. Personal loyalty to Hitler replaced the idea of military service pledged to an impersonal constitutional state. Military personnel became bound to the leader of a dictatorship. During the Second World War, the Wehrmacht carried out invasions and occupations. Responsibility for genocide and atrocities against civilians cannot be assigned exclusively to the SS while portraying the regular army as innocent of the regime's violence. Historical records document the military's involvement in a war of annihilation. [4][5]

A crucial distinction follows. The existence of tanks, conscription rules or influential generals in 2026 does not establish ideological continuity with National Socialism. History instead identifies the conditions that made German military power catastrophic: unrestricted political authority, economic militarisation without effective oversight, an ideology of conquest and the destruction of legal rights. Those conditions must be analysed empirically rather than presumed from the size of a modern defence budget.

## 4. From demilitarisation to an alliance army, 1945–1955

The May 1945 surrender brought the dissolution of the Wehrmacht, Allied occupation and extensive demilitarisation. Rising conflict between the Soviet Union and the Western powers, the Berlin blockade and the Korean War subsequently changed the calculations of the victorious states. Defending West Germany became essential to defending Western Europe. In 1950, former officers met at Himmerod to discuss the future armed forces. Their work exposed a tension between making use of existing military expertise and establishing an institution fundamentally different from the Wehrmacht. [6]

The Federal Republic joined NATO in May 1955, and the Bundeswehr was founded that November. Its political architecture differed from the imperial and Nazi models: an alliance-integrated defence force subject to law, parliamentary decisions and the budget. *Innere Führung* and the *Staatsbürger in Uniform*—the citizen in uniform—were intended to prevent a separate military political order from emerging within democratic society. The Bundestag was granted legislative, financial and institutional oversight, including a parliamentary commissioner to protect soldiers' rights. [6][7]

During the Cold War, Germany built a substantial territorial defence force because a war between NATO and the Warsaw Pact could begin on German soil. Geography was not an abstract diplomatic consideration: railways, highways, bridges, airfields, supply depots and repair facilities were essential to sustain allied armies. Germany's current rediscovery of military logistics revives that territorial function within a very different political order and with the likely main battlefield farther east.

## 5. Reunification, foreign missions and the erosion of wartime inventories

The fall of the Berlin Wall and reunification radically altered priorities. Large-scale land war in Europe appeared less likely, and NATO countries reduced troop numbers, barracks, stockpiles and industrial lines. The Bundeswehr underwent repeated reforms and prepared significant parts of the force for expeditionary operations and international missions in the Balkans, Afghanistan and Mali. Compulsory military service was suspended in 2011, although its constitutional basis remained in place. [8][9]

There was an economic rationale for the transition. Instead of maintaining large mechanised formations and equipment reserves designed for continental war, governments prioritised smaller professional forces, mobility and stabilisation operations. The fundamental difference between limited overseas deployments and high-intensity warfare is consumption. Months of combat between mechanised armies can require artillery ammunition, replacement vehicles, air defence interceptors and repairs on a scale that manufacturing chains organised for small, intermittent orders struggle to provide.

The peace dividend gradually became an entrenched political expectation. Reductions in public spending and surplus equipment were visible, while lost mobilisation skills, ageing inventories and diminished industrial surge capacity were harder to recognise. Security supplied in significant part by Washington enabled German governments to emphasise other priorities. Economic interdependence with Russia in energy and China in trade encouraged the expectation that integration would stabilise relations.

## 6. Between 2014 and 2022: the limits of security through interdependence

Russia's annexation of Crimea in 2014 and the conflict in Donbas already demonstrated the vulnerability of Europe's territorial order. The full-scale invasion on 24 February 2022 showed that sustained conflict involving artillery, trenches, drones, electronic warfare, attacks on infrastructure and large human resources remained possible. Chancellor Olaf Scholz announced a *Zeitenwende*, or historical turning point, and a special €100 billion fund to re-equip the Bundeswehr. [10][11]

The special fund was a response to accumulated equipment and investment deficiencies, not an immediate operational cure. Major weapons systems take time to manufacture and integrate. Missile systems need inventories, crews and training facilities. New formations require housing, infrastructure, experienced non-commissioned officers and mechanics. A procurement contract, a budget authorisation and a combat-ready brigade are three fundamentally different kinds of evidence.

Germany's diplomatic calculation also changed. Berlin increased military support for Ukraine and affirmed greater responsibility for collective defence, while grappling with the industrial costs of disrupted energy relations. Rearmament occurs within a complex civilian economy. It competes for skills and manufacturing capacity with the transformation of the car industry, electricity-grid investment, transport repair and the public services of an ageing society.

## 7. The 2025–2026 fiscal turn: making defence a continuing state commitment

In March 2025, Germany loosened the constitutional debt brake for specified defence and security expenditure beyond 1% of GDP. This widened the scope for multi-year military commitments beyond the temporary financing programme created in 2022. The change is structurally significant: a land force prepared for continuous deterrence needs recurring expenditure on people, maintenance and ammunition, not only an exceptional wave of equipment acquisitions. [11]

Germany's regular defence budget for 2026 was €82.7 billion, with an additional €25.5 billion from the Bundeswehr's special fund. Adding these two allocations yields €108.2 billion. In September 2026, the government's proposed 2027 budget envisaged €109.7 billion in regular defence spending and €29.9 billion from the special fund, a proposed combined €139.6 billion. The 2027 numbers are proposals, not measurements of money spent in that future year. Neither pair can automatically be equated with NATO defence expenditure statistics, which follow a different accounting definition. [12][13]

The four bars are not four complete annual observations. Each year has two separate budget components; 2027 is still within the legislative process. At NATO's 2025 Hague summit, allies agreed on a 2035 investment commitment equivalent to 5% of GDP, including at least 3.5% for core defence requirements and up to 1.5% for broader defence-related security and resilience. Germany's planned increase to 3.5% by 2029 is a fiscal trajectory, not military capability already delivered. [14]

## 8. Senior command in transition: Carsten Breuer and German influence in NATO

In September 2026, NATO chiefs of defence elected General Carsten Breuer, then Germany's Chief of Defence, as the next Chair of NATO's Military Committee. He is scheduled to assume the three-year position in summer 2027. The decision marks a tangible change in Germany's institutional standing: the Military Committee is NATO's senior collective military advisory body for its political decision-makers. The chair does not thereby gain operational command of every allied army. Operational command follows distinct NATO and national authorities. [15]

Breuer has advocated a transformation of German forces for national and collective territorial defence. In a public 2026 explanation of the military strategy and capability framework, he said Russia could be capable of a large-scale attack on NATO territory from 2029. He explicitly distinguished potential capability from a decision to launch such an attack. The date is neither proof of Russian intent nor an inevitable prediction of war. It is a planning assumption intended to accelerate readiness before a capability window might open. [16]

Breuer's planned move to NATO leadership indicates that allies expect Germany to assume more responsibility for coordination, standardisation, investment and force preparedness. Germany possesses the industrial resources and geographic position to serve as a central pillar of European defence. Yet chairing an advisory institution will only translate into sustained influence if German units demonstrate trained personnel, supply, equipment availability and the capacity to receive reinforcements from other countries.

## 9. Christian Freuding and the emerging doctrine of a transparent battlefield

In October 2025, Lieutenant General Christian Freuding became Inspector of the German Army, succeeding Alfons Mais. The Army's modernisation debate increasingly emphasises the time elapsed between detection, decision and action. In an interview published in April 2026, Freuding described a battlefield made more transparent by drones, sensors and data networks. An army able to interpret information faster than its opponent can survive and strike more effectively even when both forces deploy comparable tanks and artillery. [17][18]

The doctrinal change does not imply the disappearance of armoured warfare. Experience in Ukraine points to a combined system of protected infantry, engineers, long-range fire, electronic warfare, counter-drone defence, camouflage, dispersion and communications resilient against interference. A tank operating alone is highly vulnerable; integrated into reconnaissance, air protection and logistics, it remains a source of protected mobility and direct fire. The economic challenge is that this integration requires compatible software, secure communications and rapid replacement of inexpensive equipment consumed in large volumes.

Other leadership changes matter because strategy becomes operational readiness through multiple echelons. Heico Hübner serves as Deputy Chief of the Army and leads its basic military organisation; Harald Gante commands the field army; Maik Keller became Army Headquarters chief of staff in January 2026. These assignments show organisational consolidation, but none independently constitutes evidence of military independence from civilian government. [18]

## 10. Operational Plan Germany: infrastructure, transit and collective defence

*Operationsplan Deutschland* (OPLAN DEU) coordinates military and civilian responsibilities in territorial and alliance defence. The Bundeswehr states that the plan contemplates the transit of up to 800,000 allied service personnel and 200,000 vehicles through Germany over a six-month period, requiring transport, security, reception facilities, fuel, maintenance, medical assistance and extensive host-nation support. These numbers describe a potential allied logistics movement, not Germany's current military strength or a confirmed deployment. [19]

Geography determines the potential exposure. North Sea and Baltic ports, east–west rail connections, road bridges, electricity networks, digital systems and hospitals comprise infrastructure of indirect military significance. During a crisis, mobilisation might affect civilian passenger rail, manufacturing freight, fuel availability, hospital capacity and staffing. Private operators are indispensable to resilience because the armed forces neither possess nor need to possess every truck, terminal or workshop they would use in a major collective-defence operation.

```flow
Crisis on NATO eastern flank → political decision and activation of defence plans → allied troops arrive at German ports and airports → protected rail and road transit → supplies and medical support → allied defensive positioning
German force expansion → recruitment and training → available equipment and ammunition → combined exercises → verifiable readiness
```

The complete OPLAN is classified. Public summaries should not be turned into invented details of routes, specific vulnerable facilities or operational orders. What is publicly documented is sufficient for a substantive institutional conclusion: Germany is bringing regional authorities, private companies and civilian services into preparations for collective defence on a scale far beyond military bases.

## 11. The Lithuania brigade: from reinforcement to permanent forward presence

A permanent German brigade in Lithuania is a historical shift. Instead of relying exclusively on rotating units or reinforcement after a crisis begins, Germany is establishing a substantial combat formation on NATO's eastern flank. The programme foresees approximately 4,800 soldiers and 200 civilian personnel by the end of 2027. Its components include armoured and mechanised battalions, artillery, engineers, reconnaissance, logistics and supporting services. [20][21]

The advanced presence brings both deterrent value and lasting obligations. Forces already in position shorten the initial response time and signal that an attack would immediately involve German personnel, increasing the expected cost of aggression. At the same time, permanent bases create family and housing expenditures, require extensive host-country infrastructure and expose units to risks closer to Russia and Belarus.

In September 2026, the Bundeswehr acknowledged that staffing the brigade might require temporary assignments and, in particular specialist roles, service postings not based exclusively on soldiers' voluntary preferences. Operational readiness could outweigh individual wishes in some situations. This must not be confused with mandatory enlistment of civilians. It concerns the management of personnel already subject to military service obligations. It nonetheless reveals a binding manpower constraint that aggregate expansion targets can conceal. [21]

## 12. Recruitment and reserves: turning legal targets into trained personnel

Germany's military expansion faces a problem that money alone cannot solve. At the end of 2025, the Bundeswehr counted approximately 184,200 active personnel. The Military Service Modernisation Act effective in January 2026 established a growth path to 2035, including a target of up to 260,000 active soldiers and at least 200,000 reservists. The total of 460,000 is a future ambition, not the force currently fielded. The legal 2026 path specifies between 186,000 and 190,000 active soldiers and between 70,000 and 80,000 reservists. [22][23]

The initial model remains voluntary. Men reaching age 18 must complete a questionnaire, while the questionnaire is voluntary for women. Mandatory medical assessment of specified male cohorts is due to begin in July 2027. Should volunteers prove insufficient, parliament may enact compulsory service under the defined procedure and conditions. It would be incorrect to claim that all German young adults were already compulsorily enlisted in 2026. [23]

Enrolment is not the only constraint. Air-defence specialists, aviation mechanics, intelligence personnel, electronic warfare operators and commanders need many months or years of training. In 2026, the parliamentary commissioner for the armed forces stressed demographic pressure, competition for skilled workers, high attrition during service and structural deficiencies. He also questioned whether the balance between commissioned officers and enlisted personnel was appropriate for a sustainable expansion. These considerations determine how much of a stated personnel total can become a deployable formation. [24]

## 13. The defence industry: financial demand versus physical output

Increased procurement creates opportunities for German and European suppliers of armoured vehicles, missiles, optics, sensors, communications, propellants, artillery shells, ships, electronic systems and maintenance. Yet converting budgets into output requires long-term contracts, factory permits, access to raw materials and orders predictable enough for companies to invest in machines and workers. A manufacturer will not double capacity merely because a government announces a five-year spending ambition.

There are at least three industrial tensions. First, prioritising Bundeswehr orders may compete with exports and deliveries to partners including Ukraine. These categories are governed by different contractual and political decisions. Second, joint European procurement improves potential scale and interoperability, but multinational programmes can be slowed by divergent specifications, intellectual-property disputes and the distribution of industrial work between countries. Third, modern electronic and software-intensive weapons depend on international semiconductor, materials and energy chains that Germany does not control independently.

Defence industrial progress should be judged through physical outcomes: ammunition produced and stocked, vehicles in working condition, maintenance rates, factory productivity, reserves of critical components and trained crews. The immediate fiscal effect is expenditure; the intended strategic return is deterrence capability. A large nominal contract produces little near-term readiness when delivery and integration extend beyond the planning window identified by defence authorities.

## 14. Society, public consent and the unequal costs of service

German public opinion cannot be summarised as uniformly pacifist, militarist or committed to conscription. A Bundeswehr military-history and social-sciences survey of more than 2,200 people conducted in April and May 2026 found 87% expressing trust in the institution, 76% saying NATO membership was necessary for security, 58% favouring higher defence expenditure and 64% supporting a larger military. However, 55% supported the use of military force to defend an eastern NATO ally attacked by Russia, with 26% undecided and 14% opposed. These are answers to different questions, not additive measures of a universal willingness to fight. [25]

The distribution of costs matters. Recruitment affects age cohorts differently. Military service can interrupt education or civilian employment. Extended postings place pressures on families. Defence procurement competes with housing, schools, medical care and infrastructure for public resources. Broad support for defending the homeland does not establish consent for every overseas operation, every defence appropriation or each form of personal obligation.

The distributive conflict operates at two levels. In fiscal policy, another euro allocated to defence may be financed through taxes, borrowing or changed priorities, each with distinct effects on current income and future generations. In the labour market, military expansion increases demand for technicians and engineers and draws people into service while civilian industry attempts to manage retirement, energy transition and technological change. Effective growth requires social legitimacy alongside the practical machinery of recruitment.

## 15. France, Poland, the United States and the politics of European trust

Germany's economic position in the European Union creates a strategic asymmetry. Berlin has substantial fiscal and industrial capacity, but it cannot convert that capacity into a common European military strategy unilaterally. France, Poland, the Baltic states, Italy and NATO institutions all have independent interests and responsibilities. Poland and Lithuania may welcome permanent German defence commitments against Russian aggression while retaining historical memories of German occupation in the twentieth century. Security cooperation builds trust through rules, transparency and joint operations, not through forgetting history.

France maintains an independent nuclear arsenal and a tradition of strategic autonomy that Germany does not replicate. The United States remains central to NATO nuclear deterrence, intelligence, strategic transport and other high-end capabilities. The German programme is better understood as an attempt to reduce European vulnerabilities inside NATO than as an immediate replacement for American military power. Breuer's election increases German responsibility in military consultation, but does not place sovereign European armies under a German national chain of command.

Russian authorities depict NATO rearmament as threatening, while European governments justify their steps through the invasion of Ukraine and the need to prevent future aggression. These positions are not equivalent in relation to the documented invasion of 2022. Nevertheless, a security dilemma can emerge when defensive movements by one side are perceived as offensive by another, generating incentives for surveillance, propaganda and mobilisation. Deterrence seeks to make aggression unattractive without creating accidental escalation.

Three conclusions follow from these distinctions. First, Germany has made observable institutional changes through fiscal reform, service legislation and forward deployment decisions. Second, much of its projected land-power growth still depends on deliveries and training that will take years. Third, the current programme must be assessed against parliamentary democracy and collective-defence goals, rather than indiscriminately equating 2026 with 1914 or 1939.

## 17. Three strategic trajectories for the remainder of the decade

**Credible deterrence without direct war.** Germany meets a meaningful share of its objectives in recruitment, ammunition, logistics and eastern-flank presence. NATO achieves a more coherent defensive posture and reduces the perceived opportunity for conventional aggression. The budgetary and social costs are real, but successful deterrence avoids the vastly greater human costs of direct war. Relevant indicators include completed training, audited deliveries, realistic exercises, equipment availability and interoperability.

**Financial expansion without proportionate military readiness.** Spending rises, but procurement delays, administrative obstacles, inadequate reserve mobilisation and equipment downtime prevent it from becoming combat capability. Germany absorbs fiscal and social pressure without achieving the level of deterrence intended. Indicators include persistent gaps between appropriation and delivery, low readiness, specialist shortages and postponed schedules.

**Extended confrontation and European mobilisation.** Security deteriorates through aggression or a succession of crises requiring more forces, infrastructure protection and allied reinforcement. Germany acts as a logistics and industrial centre under pressure on rail, medical capacity, energy, communications and civilian reserves. A larger war is possible, but it does not follow automatically from an assessment that Russia may acquire certain capabilities in 2029. Diplomacy, deterrence and changed political conditions may still contain or reverse a crisis.

These trajectories depend on decisions by several governments; none is a historical destiny. What distinguishes 2026 from previous decades is the shrinking interval between a newly recognised threat and the declared need to rebuild capabilities that require years to mature.

## 18. A democratic military power or an incomplete strategic transformation?

Germany's military awakening is an effort to reconnect three dimensions separated after 1990: economic weight, territorial responsibility and the ability to fight. Germany remains a European industrial and logistics centre. It has again committed major public resources to defence, and its generals have gained prominence in reorganising collective security. Yet the country's constitutional architecture was deliberately designed to prevent military authority from merging with political sovereignty. The legitimacy of the new role depends on maintaining that separation.

The First World War demonstrated how a general staff could dominate civilian decisions when national survival became the justification for unrestricted authority. Weimar demonstrated how military institutions might preserve ambitions outside republican democracy. The Nazi period demonstrated the catastrophe of personal military submission to a regime of conquest and extermination. The Bundeswehr's foundation was a constitutional response to these experiences. The contemporary test is whether Germany can rebuild enough military capacity to deter aggression while preserving the controls that distinguish the Federal Republic from its predecessors.

Germany in 2026 is not simply returning to a former military identity. It is attempting to become a different kind of military power: parliamentary in its controls, multilateral in its commitments, territorially engaged in the defence of allies and materially dependent on a complex civilian economy. Whether it can convert that architecture into real readiness before the risks identified by defence planners materialise remains open, and will be tested by trained units, available equipment, sustained production and the credibility of democratic institutions.
