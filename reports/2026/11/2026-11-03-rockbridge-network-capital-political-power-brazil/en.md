# Rockbridge Network: Political Venture Capital, the American Tech Elite and the Contest for Influence in Brazil

The Rockbridge Network illustrates a transformation in contemporary political power: wealthy private investors have increasingly organised not merely to finance candidates, but to develop the institutions capable of selecting, promoting, advising and sustaining political movements over several electoral cycles. Founded in 2019 around J.D. Vance and Chris Buskirk, in an environment shaped by Peter Thiel's financial and intellectual connections, Rockbridge brought together donors, political operators, technology executives and conservative activists. Its reported activities range from voter mobilisation and polling to media projects, institutional access and the cultivation of future leadership. The objective attributed to its founding prospectus was to replace parts of the traditional Republican political ecosystem rather than rely on isolated donations to election campaigns. [1][2]

The emergence of a donor network of this kind is not a uniquely American phenomenon. Wealthy individuals have long supported newspapers, universities, research institutions, trade associations and parties to influence politics. Rockbridge's distinctive architecture lies in borrowing the language and allocation methods of venture capital: a small group of financiers identifies gaps in political infrastructure, concentrates resources on organisations thought capable of growing, evaluates projects against strategic objectives and seeks a persistent network capable of surviving the fortunes of individual candidates. Political returns cannot be expressed as dividends in the same way as investment returns. They appear as more durable communities, recruiting capacity, policy access, framing of public debates and changes in the supply of candidates.

The network's activities in the United States are supported by investigative reporting based on internal prospectuses, participants and federal campaign-finance records. Its projected Brazilian expansion, uncovered in September 2026, has a different evidentiary status. The registration of Rockbridge Brazil LLC in Wyoming is documented, as are presentations offering expensive memberships and proposing work in the media, universities and legislature. The American organisation says the Brazilian initiative was rejected and never funded or operational. People named in proposed structures dispute their participation. Reports of assistance to Flávio Bolsonaro's 2026 campaign, the subsequent complaints and the Federal Police investigation ordered by Supreme Court Justice Flávio Dino on 27 October must therefore be treated as disputed allegations or specific reported contacts—not as a judicial finding that unlawful foreign financing occurred. [9][10][11][12]

The Brazilian case raises a broader issue than a single electoral contest. The same investors and technical intermediaries can be associated with political mobilisation, venture-capital firms, artificial intelligence, defence industries and demands for access to energy and critical minerals. Shared personnel and political connections create channels through which influence might be exercised; they are not proof that corporate ownership, public contracts and campaign finance form one centrally controlled entity. Rockbridge Network, its legally distinct affiliated committees, the independently managed 1789 Capital and the proposed Brazilian structures must be examined on their own terms.

## 1. Origins in Ohio: an organisation designed to outlive a candidate

Rockbridge takes its name from the area around a small community in rural Ohio where conservative donors and activists met in 2019. According to subsequent reporting by the *Washington Post*, participants included Peter Thiel, J.D. Vance, Rebekah Mercer, Tucker Carlson and economist Oren Cass. The discussion took place after Donald Trump's first election had exposed a divide between older Republican institutions and a nationalist movement with different attitudes toward trade, migration, foreign commitments and the cultural establishment. The political problem was succession and organisation: a movement organised around one unusually influential politician lacked an equally durable network for recruiting, financing and training successors. [2]

Vance embodied a bridge between those environments. He had written *Hillbilly Elegy*, worked in venture capital and maintained a close association with Thiel before becoming a senator and eventually vice-president. Buskirk, a conservative commentator and entrepreneur associated with *American Greatness*, provided organisational leadership. Thiel helped bring the two together. Their economic and ideological interests intersected but were not identical: investors attracted to deregulation, nationalist industrial policy or technology investment do not necessarily agree on every tariff, social question or military intervention. [1][2][8]

A Reuters investigation published in August 2024 examined three internal documents and interviews with people familiar with the organisation. It described Rockbridge as a donor network aiming to build a centrally coordinated constellation of conservative groups, with a reported **2024 budget of approximately $75 million** and around **150–200 members** at that time. Membership commitments reportedly ranged from **$100,000 to $1 million**. Those figures concern a source-described organisational budget and membership scale, not independently audited annual income. An internal budget, pledged contributions and public political committee receipts cannot be added together as if they were disjoint revenue streams. [1]

The founding ambition was politically institutional rather than a conventional financial fund. While ordinary venture capital expects monetisable ownership interests, the proposed political version sought to support media, research, field organisation and relationships that might increase the capacity of a political coalition. A failed electoral result need not destroy those capabilities. Trained organisers, subscriber lists, donor relations, public narratives and contacts with legislators can persist and be reused in later elections.

## 2. The financial structure: club fees, affiliated groups and the limits of disclosure

Rockbridge is better understood as a private network with adjacent legal vehicles than as a single bank account. Reuters described a Delaware for-profit company, Rockbridge Network LLC, handling donor outreach, alongside political and nonprofit entities with distinct purposes. Some entities conduct voter registration or outreach; others can support journalism or polling. A political committee making independent expenditures must register and disclose according to applicable election rules, whereas donor information flowing through other legal organisations may be more difficult for outside researchers to reconstruct. [1][20]

The 2024 prospectus described five political organisations and one super PAC. Among those named in the Reuters investigation were Better Tomorrow and Over the Horizon, linked to voter mobilisation; Faithful in Action, oriented toward religious communities; and Firebrand Action, associated with politically aligned investigative work. The report cited a claimed Faithful in Action network exceeding 160,000 people, but Reuters could not independently verify its size or the full extent of its operations. An entity mentioned in a donor prospectus is not necessarily evidence that every project reached the promised scale. [1]

The legal distinctions also matter for financial transparency. Under the US Federal Election Commission's rules, a **super PAC** may accept unlimited contributions from eligible individuals, companies, labour organisations and other committees to pay for independent political expenditures, subject to disclosure and independence requirements. Contributions from foreign nationals, among other prohibited classes, are not permitted. A super PAC can pay for independent advertising and mobilisation, but it is not an ordinary candidate campaign bank account. Nonprofit advocacy, commercial investment and membership networking operate under different legal and reporting frameworks. [20][21]

This fragmentation does not in itself establish illegal financing. It complicates accountability because the public may see a registered committee's transactions without gaining an equally complete view of private meetings, membership funds or investment relationships. The relevant governance questions concern control over funds, the identity of decision-makers, the destination of payments, coordination with campaigns and the terms under which contractors provide services.

## 3. The scale that can be measured: Turnout for America

Turnout for America, a super PAC reported as linked to the Rockbridge circle, was registered with the US Federal Election Commission on **17 July 2024**, under committee identifier **C00883520**. This offers a rare opportunity to distinguish a concrete financial ledger from an organisation-wide political budget. The FEC identifies its legal category as an independent-expenditure-only committee. [3][4]

According to ProPublica's presentation of federal filings for the **2024 cycle**, Turnout for America recorded approximately **$45.67 million in total receipts**, **$34.54 million in spending**, and **$11.13 million in cash on hand at 31 December 2024**. The amounts represent one committee's filing period, not all Rockbridge organisations or the value of electoral influence produced by the spending. The difference between receipts and spending helps explain the year-end cash position; it should not be confused with annual profit or revenue from investments. [3]

## 4. Political venture capital: infrastructure rather than a cheque to a candidate

A direct donation transfers resources to a campaign within a defined election cycle. An infrastructure-building strategy instead invests in capacities that can be activated repeatedly: databases, volunteer training, polling relationships, content production, legal expertise and local networks. The distinction is critical to how political power compounds.

A voter database gains value when supporters can be contacted across successive elections, but its value depends on consent, accuracy and lawful data handling. A small news organisation can change the agenda of a community, yet an investment does not establish editorial control over each published article. A field operation can mobilise voters more effectively than expensive mass advertising in some electoral conditions, although actual effects depend on turnout probabilities, geography and mobilisation practice.

Reuters described Rockbridge-linked projects for field organising in swing states, church-based outreach, issue messaging and media operations. The 2024 account referred to plans involving thousands of field workers. A prospective staff count is a measure of intended mobilisation capacity, not proof that each worker reached a specified number of voters or changed the result of a state contest. [1]

```flow
Private donor commitments → Selection and financing of long-lived political organisations → Polling, field operations, messaging and institutional relationships
Local contacts and supporter activity → Voter records and organisational knowledge → Subsequent mobilisation and leadership recruitment → Reduced dependence on one presidential candidate
Political outcomes and policy access → Donor assessments and renewed commitments → Organisational expansion, contraction or substitution
```

The return on a political investment is partly informational. Field campaigns discover which messages mobilise people, which local institutions are trusted and which volunteers remain active after the election. The organisation can retain that knowledge even after losing an individual contest. This resembles venture-capital portfolio learning but differs economically: the outcomes are political and frequently nonexclusive, and their measurement can be highly contestable.

## 5. Churches, local associations and social trust

The emphasis on churchgoers and local organisations distinguishes Rockbridge's field strategy from a purely social-media campaign. Religious institutions, sports associations, charities and small-business networks contain pre-existing relationships in which advice and invitations may carry greater weight than unsolicited digital advertising. A campaign can seek to mobilise these relationships without the social group itself being reducible to a party organisation.

The Reuters prospectus referred to Faithful in Action and described outreach that would recruit churchgoers into political activity. Later reporting by the *Washington Post* described Buskirk's interest in gathering voter knowledge through affiliations such as churches, outdoor groups and small businesses. These reports establish the stated institutional method; they do not show that all members of a church or association endorsed the network or knowingly contributed personal data. [1][2]

Local social trust can make political mobilisation efficient. A message relayed by a known neighbour may be acted upon when the same message from an unknown campaign account would be ignored. This creates an incentive to invest in persistent intermediary organisations, while also raising questions about consent, privacy, the boundary between civic and partisan activity, and whether group leaders speak for their members.

Digital technology amplifies rather than replaces the offline component. Customer-relationship software, contact histories, phone outreach and targeted content can organise human relationships, but a database itself does not generate trust. The capacity to connect both is a form of organisational capital: it cannot be purchased instantly by an outsider without access to the existing relationships.

## 6. Media, polling and the construction of a political information environment

A movement that depends exclusively on coverage from established outlets has limited control over how its priorities are framed. Rockbridge's prospectus described support for conservative journalism, polling and aligned media projects. Reuters identified Firebrand Action among the organisations associated with these activities, but could not establish all particular reports or outlets funded by the network. The difference between the stated media programme and a verifiable inventory of supported publications remains substantial. [1]

The political economy of media financing is not simply that funders order journalists to adopt specific conclusions. Ownership and grants can affect which outlets exist, which topics receive resources, what research capacity is available and what audiences are reached. Professional editorial independence, contractual conditions and governance determine whether financiers can influence individual editorial decisions. When grant arrangements and ownership are opaque, the public may find it difficult to assess those relationships.

Polling is another part of the information environment. A survey can measure preferences, but it also helps political organisations decide where to invest, which concerns to emphasise and which supporters require additional contact. Survey data are strategic inputs, not guaranteed predictors. Sample composition, nonresponse and turnout uncertainty complicate their interpretation. Internal polling becomes valuable even when its findings are never published.

The same logic applies to online attention: messages are developed, tested and redistributed through digital platforms, while local organisations convert some exposure into registration, meetings or turnout. No evidence establishes that Rockbridge possesses proprietary control over Facebook, TikTok or other platforms' recommendation algorithms. Data-driven campaigning and ownership of a platform's algorithm are separate categories of power.

## 7. Vance, Thiel and Buskirk: connections without a single command structure

J.D. Vance's connection to Rockbridge is historical and political. Reuters identified him as cofounder but reported no current formal organisational role at the time of its 2024 investigation. In March 2026 he addressed a private Rockbridge donor summit in Nashville, attended by a crowd described by CBS News as roughly 250 donor-community participants. In September, Axios documented another Rockbridge gathering drawing senior officials, Republican politicians, donors and political professionals. Access and recurring participation are demonstrable; formal ownership, control of each affiliate and direction of every payment do not follow from them. [1][5][7]

Peter Thiel's role is important because he connected technical entrepreneurship, capital markets and politics before Rockbridge became prominent. Reuters and the *Washington Post* describe him introducing Vance and Buskirk and participating in the founding circle. Thiel also became a major supporter of Vance's earlier Senate bid. That history establishes personal and financial connections, not a legal finding that Thiel controls all projects undertaken by the network or every public decision made by Vance. [1][2][8]

Chris Buskirk appears as the operational organiser bridging political work and investment relationships. He was involved in American Greatness, Rockbridge and the formation of 1789 Capital. His public arguments have emphasised replacing an older Republican elite with organisers and investors more aligned with nationalist conservatism. The concepts of a political elite and a new conservative establishment are self-conscious features of this strand of politics, despite its anti-establishment rhetoric. [2]

Other participants have different motives. Rebekah Mercer is a longstanding conservative donor. Omeed Malik built financial and media interests. The Winklevoss brothers and other cryptocurrency figures have regulatory priorities relevant to the profitability of digital-asset businesses. Investors engaged in defence or artificial intelligence have interests in procurement, safety rules, export restrictions and industrial policy. These preferences overlap in some policy areas and conflict in others. Shared attendance at a private conference does not establish one coordinated position on every issue.

## 8. 1789 Capital: an affiliated circle, a separate investment company

The distinction between Rockbridge and **1789 Capital** is necessary to understand how political alignment and financial investment can coexist. Discussions at a Rockbridge gathering in 2022 reportedly involved Buskirk, Mercer, Omeed Malik and Blake Masters in the creation of a parallel economy of conservative-friendly businesses. These conversations contributed to a venture-capital firm named after the year of the US Constitution's founding era. Reuters reported in 2024 that 1789 Capital was owned by Buskirk, Malik and Mercer, with approximately **$150 million** then under management, and that Donald Trump Jr. was preparing to join it as a partner. The fund's reported scale increased in subsequent accounts; historical assets under management must not be treated as a permanent number. [1][16]

The firm describes its orientation as **patriotic capitalism**. This is an investment thesis and branding choice, not a legal investment category that guarantees public benefit or shared political opinion across all portfolio companies. The firm has backed media projects including Tucker Carlson's company and later appeared in reporting about investments in aerospace, artificial intelligence, defence technology and infrastructure. Folha reported more than $1 billion in its assets by September 2026, a dated public account rather than a current independent audit. [8][16]

A 2026 *Washington Post* investigation identified 1789 Capital investments in companies that also maintain relationships with the US defence sector, including SpaceX, Anduril and manufacturing technology ventures. The overlap raises legitimate questions about procurement conflicts, access to government decision-makers and whether capital allocation benefits from political proximity. It does **not**, by itself, demonstrate that a Pentagon contract was granted illegally, that every investee supports Rockbridge, or that 1789 is a financial subsidiary of Rockbridge. [17]

A commercial fund owns investments according to contractual and corporate arrangements; a donor network coordinates political resources; a politician exercises governmental authority under public law. Their connections can facilitate access and information exchange, but each relationship requires its own ownership records, disclosed transactions and decision evidence. Collapsing them into one enterprise would obscure where economic and political responsibility actually sits.

## 9. Defence, chips, AI and the material interests behind political access

The development of AI systems depends on material assets: semiconductors, power generation, electricity transmission, land for data centres, water and cooling, skilled labour and communications networks. Defence technology adds satellites, software, propulsion, sensors, secure manufacturing and public procurement. These fields are increasingly treated as strategic by governments concerned about competition with China, industrial security and military capability.

Investors connected to the Rockbridge circle may stand to gain from policies that accelerate permitting, allow more flexible financing, expand domestic procurement or reshape export controls. They may also face losses from sanctions, restrictive rules or changes in government. Their preferences are not automatically identical: a company dependent on global chip supply can oppose restrictions that another domestic producer welcomes. Analysis of concrete interests requires identifying an investor's actual holdings and the specific policy's expected effects.

When technology investors meet ministers or political candidates, potential influence is created by privileged access, the ability to supply technical expertise, the framing of policy alternatives and possible future financing. The existence of these channels is not an estimate of realised profit. A firm may join a meeting without obtaining a contract; a government may adopt an industrial policy that benefits a broad sector without doing so at a private investor's instruction.

The Brazilian proposal's focus on a *Technology Council* belongs in this context. It sought to place international investors and technical executives close to a prospective governing agenda, according to documents reported in Brazil. Yet a proposed list of invitees, projected foreign commitments and a plan for sectoral partnerships cannot establish that the named participants agreed to join or that the promised investment existed. [10][11]

## 10. Internationalisation: South Korea as the documented comparison

Rockbridge's connections to South Korea reveal a different degree of institutional development from that reported for Brazil. The *Korea Herald* documented the establishment of a Korean division in 2025 with prominent figures from politics and business, including former prime minister Kim Boo-kyum, former finance minister Bahk Jae-wan and Shinsegae chairman Chung Yong-jin. That does not mean the Korean group operates under the same legal entity or political mandate as the US donor network; its public activity was framed around policy, investment and the bilateral alliance. [14]

In September 2026, the *Korea JoongAng Daily* reported a planned **30 September security seminar in Seoul** on cooperation among South Korea, the United States and Japan. Former US and Japanese defence officials were listed among participants. The event's announced theme concerned trilateral security and defence cooperation, not a confirmed private takeover of government policy. The contrast with Brazil is instructive: the Korean arm's launch and public seminars were documented, while the Brazilian operation's governing status and activation remained contested. [15]

A political network can expand internationally by sponsoring dialogue, forming local associations, bringing investors and policymakers together or attempting campaign-adjacent activity. These forms are legally and democratically different. A published seminar with former officials can be legitimate civil-society diplomacy; undisclosed material support to a foreign campaign would be subject to the recipient country's financing laws. Informal networks may engage in both legitimate and prohibited conduct, but classification requires evidence of the actual acts.

## 11. Brazil: from political contacts to a Wyoming registration

In September 2026, Brazilian investigative reporting moved the story from an abstract network of US donors to a concrete controversy about electoral sovereignty. The *Intercept Brasil* established that a company called **Rockbridge Brazil LLC** had been incorporated in **Wyoming on 12 May 2026**, with Republican fundraiser Chris Applegate appearing as the formal *organizer*. That term denotes a role in filing incorporation documents; it does not by itself establish beneficial ownership or day-to-day management. Reporting also identified legal-administrative links to a law firm active in cross-border transactions. [9][24]

The existence of a registered LLC is a verifiable corporate event. It is neither proof of an operating Brazilian organisation nor evidence of a disbursement to a candidate. Corporate formation can precede fundraising, hiring, membership contracts or business activity; many entities never conduct the operations originally contemplated. Corporate ownership information may also be absent from the publicly available registry in a jurisdiction such as Wyoming.

The Rockbridge Network acknowledged that the company was created while stating that its Brazilian project had been rejected, never received funding, did not commence operations and had not been authorised to act politically in the network's name. The Brazilian intermediaries quoted by the press also presented the initiative as abandoned or suspended. These denials stand alongside documents and reported contacts showing that an expansion had at least been formulated. The evidence therefore establishes a **proposal and incorporation** while leaving **implementation, authorisation and financial execution** unresolved. [9][10]

Contacts with business executives and political figures were reported by Folha, including approaches involving Joesley Batista, André Esteves, Flávio Bolsonaro and Romeu Zema. Being contacted or meeting a participant does not mean accepting membership, contributing money or agreeing to any political initiative. Several named people did not comment publicly; others expressly disputed claims about their roles. Contact networks are relevant to understanding the ambition of the project, but the meaning of each contact remains constrained by the recorded evidence. [10]

Documents obtained by Folha described two proposed categories for Rockbridge Brazil, **Keystone**, with an annual contribution of **$500,000**, and **Visionary**, with **$1 million**. The offers reportedly included access to private meetings, summits and international donors; the higher tier added a proposed strategic council seat. Possible payment mechanisms were broad, encompassing dollars and certain financial assets. Those amounts are **proposed membership charges**, not verified Brazilian contributions, recorded turnover or campaign transfers. [10]

Political access has economic value even before a government makes a decision. A donor may gain information about agendas, meet investors with complementary projects, participate in discussions about industrial strategy or increase the visibility of selected policy proposals. These benefits differ from an unlawful purchase of official acts. The existence of a high-price private forum raises transparency and fairness concerns; it does not automatically demonstrate bribery or prohibited campaign contributions.

The proposed business model also exposed a contradiction familiar to cross-border political networks. Its presentation combined civic and ideological objectives with commercial language and promises of access to decision-makers. From the perspective of members, spending might be justified by expected political influence or business networks. From the perspective of a democratic state, political participation is legitimate only within rules ensuring transparency, lawful finance and equal protection of electoral sovereignty.

The difference between a planned membership scheme and actual receipts is decisive. There is no publicly verified account in the sources used here showing that Brazilian members paid these charges, that Rockbridge Brazil obtained the projected annual budget or that these proposed subscriptions were passed through to any candidate. Political scrutiny should follow identifiable transfers and services rather than multiplying advertised member prices by an imagined membership.

## 13. The three proposed institutional fronts: media, universities and the legislature

A Brazilian business plan dated April 2026 and examined by the *Intercept Brasil* described a structure resembling the American political-venture model. The project identified three priority environments: media, youth and university education, and legislative support. In its internal framing, these fields were presented as insufficiently represented by conservatives. Plans included supporting right-leaning independent media, creating channels for the intellectual and organisational formation of younger leaders, and supplying staff or data capabilities to allied parliamentarians. [9]

Media funding can affect the supply of reporting, distribution networks and public narratives. University training can generate candidates, policy professionals, lawyers or organisers years after the original investment. Legislative support services can improve the ability of parliamentarians to evaluate complex bills, monitor agencies and coordinate policy. These are enduring institutional capabilities, in contrast to a one-time advertising purchase.

The same functions can exist lawfully in civil society across the political spectrum. Think tanks, philanthropic foundations, trade associations and political education groups support debate and candidate formation in many democracies. The crucial distinctions concern who funds the initiative, which persons make decisions, whether resources or personnel are effectively used to support a particular campaign, and whether the relevant laws permit those forms of support.

The document's political ambition therefore deserves neither automatic criminalisation nor automatic acceptance. It describes a plan to shape the production of knowledge, public communication and legislative capacity by a relatively small, privately funded network. The democratic effects depend on transparency, editorial autonomy, institutional competition and whether funders obtain privileged authority unavailable through ordinary political processes.

## 14. The Technology Council: spectacular projections without committed investment

Other presentations reported by Folha proposed a **23-person Technology Council**, including several Brazilians and prominent international technology investors. The materials described possible economic impact exceeding **$100 billion over ten years** and creation of **500,000 jobs**. These were prospective figures in a promotional proposal, not an investment ledger, contracted capital expenditure or a verified employment forecast. No public model identifying projects, investment schedules, additionality, multipliers and counterfactual employment effects was provided. [10]

A proposal to gather international capital and technology around a future government's industrial agenda can have credible economic aims. Brazil has opportunities in renewable electricity, manufacturing, minerals, software, defence industries and digital infrastructure. But the distinction between contacts and transactions is material: a seat offered to an investor is not acceptance; a letter of interest is not a binding contract; a contract is not an executed construction project; an executed project is not necessarily a net gain in domestic employment.

Those distinctions matter particularly when large numbers are used in electoral communications. A $100 billion projection may combine foreign direct investment, domestic investment, financed assets, gross revenue or economic-impact estimates across a decade. Without published definitions, adding it to reserves, exports or GDP would be methodologically unsound. Similarly, 500,000 gross jobs over years may differ enormously from permanent net employment after displacement and productivity effects.

The people listed in the materials did not uniformly confirm their participation. Reporting recorded explicit denials by some named individuals or their companies; others said the proposed council never held a meeting. The evidentiary record supports the existence of ambitions and presentation materials, not the formation of a working investment board with binding international authority. [10][11]

## 15. Alleged advisers and the 2026 Brazilian presidential campaign

On 1 October, Folha reported that people linked to the Rockbridge environment had helped Flávio Bolsonaro's presidential campaign with monitoring, reports, logistical tasks and discussions about AI infrastructure policy, citing sources close to the campaign. The report described Pedro Sang and Tallis Gomes in different reported roles. It also recorded denials of foreign campaign financing and disputes over who formally belonged to the Brazilian initiative. [11]

A person may advise a candidate informally without an institution financing the campaign; a paid service may be a legally accountable donation in kind if supplied free by a prohibited source; a meeting can be purely informational. Determining which category applies requires identifying who performed the work, who directed it, who paid its cost, whether the campaign received an economic advantage and whether it knew of the arrangement. Those distinctions cannot be resolved from a photograph or a list of possible members.

The campaign denied receiving foreign money or campaign structure. Tallis Gomes publicly rejected involvement in electoral fundraising or foreign-resource intermediation and said he did not perform the alleged formal roles. People connected to the proposed initiative stated that it was not operational. The fact that competing Brazilian parties used these reports in campaign accusations does not settle the evidentiary dispute. [11][12]

The second-round presidential election ended in Lula's re-election in October 2026, as described in contemporary reports. The case did not disappear with the result: alleged foreign assistance, if established, would raise issues about electoral integrity and the economic value of undocumented services independently of which candidate ultimately won. The importance of the investigation lies in the legal character and provenance of any demonstrated assistance, not a claim that an unproven operation determined the election. [12][22]

## 16. The Supreme Court order of 27 October: investigation is not conviction

On **27 October 2026**, Supreme Court Justice **Flávio Dino** ordered Brazil's Federal Police to examine suspected foreign interference and prohibited support in the presidential election. Reporting by Folha, UOL and the *Washington Post* identifies Rockbridge-related contacts and alleged contributions or services among the investigated issues. Other allegations concerned social-media behaviour, paid foreign-based profiles and potential economic interests in critical minerals. The order authorises fact-finding; it is not a judicial finding that the named people or entities committed electoral crimes. [12][13][22]

The investigation's legal significance is that campaign support can take forms other than a bank transfer. A technically skilled adviser may supply reports, staff hours, customer data or advertising services that have measurable market value. If the donor or source is prohibited, the possibility of an *in-kind* benefit becomes relevant. The prosecution would have to establish the factual nature of the service, the payer, the beneficiary, legal classification, knowledge and responsibility according to the applicable rules.

The complaints involving alleged algorithmic favouritism by large platforms form another distinct factual category. A platform's moderation error, recommendation outcome or unequal reach does not prove coordination with a donor network. Separate investigations of platform conduct require evidence of decisions, internal processes, discriminatory treatment and causal effects. They cannot be treated as supporting one another simply because they appear in the same political dossier.

The allegations involving critical minerals likewise demand transaction-level evidence. The presence of mineral supply chains in a political prospectus may indicate strategic interest, but establishing an exchange of campaign support for mineral access would require agreements, communications, payments or decisions connecting the two. No such concluded bargain is established by the reviewed public reporting. [12][13]

## 17. Political funding and foreign sovereignty: the Brazilian legal boundary

Brazilian electoral law prohibits specified forms of foreign-origin campaign finance. **Article 24 of Law 9,504/1997** bars parties and candidates from receiving direct or indirect donations of money or benefits measurable in money from prohibited sources, including foreign governments and entities. The Superior Electoral Court's guidance for the 2026 election explicitly lists foreign-origin resources and corporate donations among prohibited sources. Its rules also address indirect support and publicity as potential forms of prohibited economic benefit. [18][19]

These provisions do not make it unlawful for a Brazilian politician to speak with a foreign businessman, attend an international conference or study an investment proposal. The legal issue emerges when the exchange becomes campaign support financed by a prohibited source or otherwise breaches applicable electoral obligations. Nationality alone and the foreign origin of a particular resource are not always interchangeable legal concepts; the TSE's annotated statute recognises distinctions involving the origin of funds and the position of resident individuals. [19]

The United States also imposes restrictions on foreign nationals making election-related contributions or expenditures. The FEC states that super PACs cannot accept money from foreign nationals even though they may accept unlimited contributions from eligible domestic sources. The two systems have different campaign-finance architectures, but both recognise that foreign-funded electoral activity presents a special sovereignty concern. [20][21]

Compliance cannot be established through branding. A payment routed through a domestic consultant may still require examination of its beneficial source; a public invitation to a foreign investor may remain lawful if no campaign resource is provided. Financial records, invoices, contract scopes, records of work actually done, communications and beneficial ownership information offer greater probative value than broad claims about ideological affinity.

## 18. Critical minerals, data centres and the competition for Brazilian strategic resources

Brazil's mineral reserves, electricity resources and domestic market make the country relevant to international industrial strategy. Rare earth elements are inputs to certain magnets and equipment; other critical minerals support batteries, power networks, defence systems and semiconductor supply chains. The existence of geological resources, however, is not equivalent to commercially proven reserves, operating mines, separation capacity, metal refining or magnet manufacturing. Each stage requires investment, permitting, technical competence and market access.

The Rockbridge Brazil presentations included technology, energy, data-centre infrastructure and references to the potential of international capital. The Brazilian electoral controversy added allegations that foreign-connected actors wanted favourable access to rare earths. Economic interest in a sector can be inferred from the stated policy agenda, but a claim that particular individuals obtained or were promised mineral assets requires identified concessions, beneficial owners, contracts or official commitments. Those are not established by the sources describing the Rockbridge proposal. [10][12]

A data centre is not merely a software investment. Its feasibility depends on reliable electricity, transmission capacity, cooling, land, permits, connectivity and a customer able to pay for computation. An investment advertised as a technology partnership may entail negotiation of tariffs, electricity contracts, tax treatment and access to infrastructure. Those material dependencies give economic importance to regulatory and diplomatic relationships but do not make every such relationship an improper bargain.

The geopolitics also involve competition with China and industrial sovereignty. The United States may seek diversified supply chains and trusted partners; Brazil may seek higher-value industrialisation rather than merely exporting unprocessed ore. These goals can overlap if technology transfer, local production and stable returns exist. They can conflict when agreements concentrate ownership, limit domestic competition or give away public advantages without transparent reciprocal obligations.

## 20. Private political capital and democratic accountability

Private donors can expand political pluralism by funding organisations neglected by established parties or media. They can also concentrate influence when only a small number of exceptionally wealthy people gain repeated access to ministers, candidates and infrastructure decisions. The same mechanism may create a new political elite while presenting itself as a challenge to the old one. Its democratic effect depends on who can participate, how organisations are governed and whether funding and decisions remain contestable.

There is no reason to assume that the problem is exclusive to one ideology. Philanthropic foundations, advocacy networks, labour organisations, business associations and major media investors across the political spectrum seek durable influence. The relevance of Rockbridge lies in its particular blend of political venture capital, high-priced membership, technology-sector wealth, partisan organising and attempted international expansion.

A serious accountability framework would follow three forms of power. **Financial power:** who supplies resources, who receives them, and under which legal instrument. **Organisational power:** who recruits people, owns databases, controls media grants and determines priorities. **Institutional power:** who obtains meetings, influences policy drafts, secures contracts or exercises public authority. Evidence about one dimension cannot automatically establish the others.

The risk of oligarchic governance increases when economic concentration and privileged political access reinforce one another without transparent decision procedures. The risk of state overreach increases when governments use foreign-interference allegations to suppress legitimate civic association or punish political opponents without evidence. Both dangers require independent courts, disclosure, competitive institutions and procedural safeguards.

## 21. Scenarios after Brazil's election and before the US midterms

As of the **early 3 November 2026** Brazilian information cutoff, the US congressional midterm election scheduled for that day had not concluded. Its outcome could affect the balance of power for the Republicans and Vance's prospects for 2028, but no result is incorporated in this study. The Brazilian presidential election had ended in Lula's re-election, while the Federal Police inquiry remained a distinct and ongoing matter. [12][17]

**Institutional consolidation in the United States.** Rockbridge-linked organisations continue to build field capacity, donor relations and conservative media or research platforms through multiple elections. Evidence would include verified committee filings, functioning organisations and durable local programmes. The thesis weakens if promised spending is not executed, turnover erodes field capacity or donors move to competing networks.

**International political-network expansion with formal boundaries.** The Korean initiative continues public policy or defence dialogue, while any renewed Brazilian project becomes transparent, legally constituted and separated from prohibited campaign financing. Evidence would require disclosed incorporation, lawful local funding, verifiable meetings and published governance. It weakens if planned bodies remain on paper or lack confirmed participants and resources.

**Legal and diplomatic confrontation.** Brazilian investigators identify actual prohibited electoral services or transfers and the controversy creates disputes between governments and private organisations. This would require findings grounded in transactions, communications and applicable law. Without such evidence, allegations alone cannot establish wrongdoing or justify asserting that investment negotiations and electoral support were exchanged.

A fourth, less dramatic outcome is also possible: the American network continues domestically, the Brazilian LLC remains dormant, the inquiry resolves without proof of illegal financing, and strategic interests in Brazil are pursued through conventional investment and diplomacy. Political significance does not require that every proposal become operational.

## 22. The distribution of power in the new conservative infrastructure

Rockbridge occupies an intersection between private capital and institutions that distribute political influence. Its reported American model invests in durable political capabilities rather than campaign advertising alone. Its associated financial circle includes investors positioned in technologies whose markets are shaped by public policy, defence procurement and strategic competition. Its Korean activity shows that policy-network forms can travel internationally, while its Brazilian episode demonstrates the tension between political aspiration, formal registration, conflicting accounts and electoral sovereignty.

Three differences prevent inflated conclusions. A **donor network** is not the same legal entity as an **investment firm**; an **investment firm's portfolio** is not proof of **control over public contracts**; and a **foreign political proposal** is not proof of a **completed unlawful campaign contribution**. These boundaries do not diminish the significance of Rockbridge. They locate it precisely: in the capacity of private actors to assemble money, expertise, social trust and access into organisations that may persist beyond individual election results.

Political venture capital brings a logic familiar from finance into democratic competition: portfolios of projects, investment horizons, concentrated sponsors and the expectation of cumulative returns. But unlike financial ventures, the returns can be public decisions that affect millions of citizens who never joined the investment club. The resulting question of legitimacy is not resolved by the investors' professed ideals or by their critics' suspicions. It is resolved, insofar as democratic systems can resolve it, by the visibility of financial flows, equality before the law, autonomy of public institutions and the ability of citizens to contest the exercise of power.
